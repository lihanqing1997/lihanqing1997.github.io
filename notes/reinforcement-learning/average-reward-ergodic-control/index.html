Replace discounted contraction by a Poisson equation, and prove average-reward control and a vanishing-discount limit under an explicit mixing condition.

A finite irreducible policy chain

Fix a stationary policy with transition matrix \(P\) and bounded mean reward vector \(r\). This chapter uses no discount in the average-reward objective. Let \(d\) be its invariant probability vector, \(d^\top P=d^\top\), and define \(g=d^\top r\). Here \(d\) is a stationary distribution, not the discounted occupancy used earlier.

Theorem (Poisson equation and gain). If \(P\) is finite and irreducible, there is \(h\in\mathbb R^S\) satisfying \[ g\mathbf1+h=r+Ph. \] The scalar \(g\) is unique, \(h\) is unique up to constants, and for every initial state \(s\), \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\mathbb E_s\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}R_{t+1}=g. \] Periodicity does not obstruct this expected-average conclusion.

Proof. First, an invariant distribution exists: the Cesàro averages \(d_n^\top=n^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}e_s^\top P^t\) lie in a compact simplex, and \[ d_n^\top P-d_n^\top=(e_s^\top P^n-e_s^\top)/n\longrightarrow0. \] Every convergent subsequence therefore has an invariant limit \(d\). Irreducibility makes \(d\) strictly positive: if \(d_i>0\), then for every \(j\) some \(m\) has \(P^m(i,j)>0\), and \(d_j=(d^\top P^m)_j\ge d_iP^m(i,j)>0\). If \(u=Pu\) and \(u_i=\max_j u_j\), then \[ 0=u_i-\sum_jP(i,j)u_j=\sum_jP(i,j)(u_i-u_j). \] Each term is nonnegative; every positive-probability successor has the same maximum. Following paths and using irreducibility gives \(u=c\mathbf1\). Hence \(\ker(I-P)\) is one-dimensional, its rank is \(S-1\), and its left nullspace is exactly the span of \(d\). This also proves uniqueness of the invariant probability. Since \(d^\top(r-g\mathbf1)=0\), the finite-dimensional range/nullspace identity implies \(r-g\mathbf1\in\operatorname{range}(I-P)\), proving existence of \(h\). Multiplication of any Poisson equation by \(d^\top\) identifies \(g\); subtraction and the harmonic-function argument identify \(h\) modulo constants.

Iterate \(r-g\mathbf1=h-Ph\) along the chain: \[ \mathbb E_s\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(R_{t+1}-g) =\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}e_s^\top P^t(I-P)h =h(s)-\mathbb E_s h(S_n). \] The right-hand side has absolute value at most \(2\|h\|_\infty\). Division by \(n\) and passage to the limit prove the gain formula.

Bias as a stopped centered return

Normalize \(h(s_0)=0\), and let \(\tau=\inf\{t\ge0:S_t=s_0\}\). Finite irreducibility supplies integers \(m\) and \(\epsilon>0\) such that, from every state, the chance of hitting \(s_0\) within \(m\) steps is at least \(\epsilon\): choose a positive-probability path for each state and take the minimum of finitely many path probabilities. The Markov property gives \(\Pr_s(\tau>km)\le(1-\epsilon)^k\), hence \(\mathbb E_s\tau<\infty\).

Proof. For the bounded stopping time \(\tau\wedge n\), sum the conditional identity \(\mathbb E[h(S_t)-h(S_{t+1})\mid S_0,\ldots,S_t]=r(S_t)-g\), multiplied by \(\mathbf1_{\{t<\tau\wedge n\}}\). Finite telescoping yields \[ \mathbb E_s\sum_{t=0}^{\tau\wedge n-1}(r(S_t)-g) =h(s)-\mathbb E_s h(S_{\tau\wedge n}). \] The sum is dominated by \(\|r-g\mathbf1\|_\infty\tau\), which is integrable. Also \(h(S_{\tau\wedge n})\to h(s_0)=0\) almost surely and is bounded by \(\|h\|_\infty\). Dominated convergence on both sides gives \[ h(s)=\mathbb E_s\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}(r(S_t)-g). \] For \(s=s_0\), \(\tau=0\) and both sides are zero. The hitting-time convention is explicit; replacing it with a positive return time without explanation changes the representation being used.

Control under a uniform minorization condition

For the next results assume a finite controlled model and a common probability vector \(\nu\), with \(0<\epsilon\le1\), such that \[ P(s'\mid s,a)\ge\epsilon\nu(s')\qquad\text{for every }s,a,s'. \] This is a sufficient condition, stronger than general communicating or unichain assumptions. Let \((Th)(s)=\max_a\{r(s,a)+Ph(s,a)\}\) and \(\operatorname{sp}(u)=\max_su(s)-\min_su(s)\). On the subspace \(\mathcal H=\{h:h(s_0)=0\}\), span is a norm.

Proof. Write \(P_{s,a}=\epsilon\nu+(1-\epsilon)\widetilde P_{s,a}\) when \(\epsilon<1\); the case \(\epsilon=1\) is immediate. For \(u=v-w\), every actionwise backup difference lies in the common interval \[ [\,\epsilon\nu^\top u+(1-\epsilon)\min u,\, \epsilon\nu^\top u+(1-\epsilon)\max u\,]. \] The difference of maxima lies between the smallest and largest actionwise differences, so all components of \(Tv-Tw\) lie in that same interval. Therefore \[ \operatorname{sp}(Tv-Tw)\le(1-\epsilon)\operatorname{sp}(v-w). \] The normalized map \(Fh=Th-(Th)(s_0)\mathbf1\) maps \(\mathcal H\) into itself and is a contraction. This finite-dimensional normed space is complete, so \(F\) has a unique fixed point \(h_*\), and \(F^nh_0\to h_*\) geometrically. Define \(g_*=(Th_*)(s_0)\). Then \[ g_*\mathbf1+h_*=Th_*. \] For any history policy, the maximizing inequality gives \[ \mathbb E[R_{t+1}-g_*+h_*(S_{t+1})-h_*(S_t)\mid H_t]\le0. \] Summing \(n\) steps and dividing by \(n\) bounds its expected average reward by \(g_*+[h_*(s)-\mathbb E h_*(S_n)]/n\). The remainder tends to zero. A stationary maximizing selector gives equality at every step, hence average reward \(g_*\). This proves optimality and supplies a convergent relative-value iteration under the stated condition.

A justified vanishing-discount limit

Let \(V_\gamma^*\) be the discounted optimal value for this same uniformly minorized model, and put \(h_\gamma=V_\gamma^*-V_\gamma^*(s_0)\mathbf1\), \(g_\gamma=(1-\gamma)V_\gamma^*(s_0)\). With \(T_\gamma v=\max_a(r+\gamma Pv)\), the Bellman equation becomes \[ g_\gamma\mathbf1+h_\gamma=T_\gamma h_\gamma. \]

Proof. The normalized discounted map has span contraction coefficient \(\gamma(1-\epsilon)\). Moreover, each difference of corresponding action scores for \(T_\gamma h_*\) and \(Th_*\) lies between \(-(1-\gamma)\max h_*\) and \(-(1-\gamma)\min h_*\). Thus \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{sp}(h_\gamma-h_*) &\le\gamma(1-\epsilon)\operatorname{sp}(h_\gamma-h_*) +(1-\gamma)\operatorname{sp}(h_*),\\ \operatorname{sp}(h_\gamma-h_*) &\le\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma(1-\epsilon)} \operatorname{sp}(h_*)\longrightarrow0. \end{aligned} \] Both vectors vanish at \(s_0\), so their sup-norm difference is at most their span difference; hence \(h_\gamma\to h_*\) uniformly. By finite-dimensional continuity, \(g_\gamma=(T_\gamma h_\gamma)(s_0)\to(Th_*)(s_0)=g_*\). Finally \[ (1-\gamma)V_\gamma^*(s) =g_\gamma+(1-\gamma)h_\gamma(s)\longrightarrow g_* \] uniformly in \(s\). This proves convergence of the entire normalized family under the stated minorization, without an unsupported compactness or subsequence assertion.

General average-reward theory weakens this condition using recurrence classes, bias spans, and communicating structure. In multichain models the gain may depend on the initial state. Ordinary relative-value iteration may oscillate in periodic models, so the discounted contraction proof cannot simply be reused at \(\gamma=1\).

Worked example (a periodic chain and an oscillating iteration). Let \(P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(r=(1,0)^\top\). Then \(d=(1/2,1/2)^\top\), \(g=1/2\), and with \(h(1)=0\), the Poisson equation gives \(h=(0,-1/2)^\top\). The gain exists despite periodicity. But relative iteration from zero gives \[ (0,0)\ \mapsto\ (0,-1)\ \mapsto\ (0,0)\ \mapsto\cdots . \] The common-row minorization with \(\epsilon>0\) fails, so this example does not contradict the contraction theorem.

Exercises

  1. Verify the hitting-time representation of \(h(2)\) in the worked chain.

    Hint and solution

    Starting at state \(2\), the first hit of state \(1\) occurs at time one. The centered reward is \(r(2)-g=-1/2\), so \(h(2)=-1/2\).

  2. If \(P(\cdot\mid s,a)=\nu\) for every row, what does relative iteration do?

    Hint and solution

    Here \(\epsilon=1\). Since \(Th(s)=\max_a r(s,a)+\nu^\top h\), normalization removes the common continuation term. One iteration gives \(h_*(s)=\max_a r(s,a)-\max_a r(s_0,a)\), independently of the initial vector.
