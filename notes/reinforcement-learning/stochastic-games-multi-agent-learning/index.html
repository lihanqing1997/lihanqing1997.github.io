Learning goal. Prove the discounted zero-sum Shapley theorem and understand exactly what no-regret learning says about repeated general-sum play.

Two-player zero-sum Markov games

Let states, maximizing-player actions \(a\), and minimizing-player actions \(b\) be finite. The first player receives bounded reward \(r(s,a,b)\), the second its negative, and the next-state kernel is \(P(\cdot\mid s,a,b)\). Actions are chosen simultaneously using independent private randomization conditional on the common history. For a value vector \(v\), define the local matrix \[ M_s(v)_{ab}=r(s,a,b)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a,b)v(s'),\qquad 0\le\gamma<1. \] The external finite matrix-game minimax theorem states \[ \operatorname{val}(M)=\max_{x\in\Delta_A}\min_{y\in\Delta_B}x^\top My =\min_{y\in\Delta_B}\max_{x\in\Delta_A}x^\top My, \] with an attained saddle pair. This follows from finite LP duality; it is the game-theory input to the following proof.

Theorem 27.1 (discounted Shapley operator). The operator \((Tv)(s)=\operatorname{val}(M_s(v))\) is a \(\gamma\)-contraction. Its fixed point \(v_*\) is the game value, and saddle strategies in each matrix \(M_s(v_*)\) form optimal stationary policies against arbitrary history-dependent opponents.

Proof. For any matrices \(M,N\) and distributions \(x,y\), \[ |x^\top(M-N)y|\le\sum_{a,b}x_ay_b\|M-N\|_{\max}=\|M-N\|_{\max}. \] Taking the same min and max operations on both sides of the corresponding upper and lower inequalities yields \(|\operatorname{val}(M)-\operatorname{val}(N)|\le\|M-N\|_{\max}\). Also \(\|M_s(v)-M_s(w)\|_{\max}\le\gamma\|v-w\|_\infty\). Hence \(T\) contracts and has a unique bounded fixed point. Choose a saddle pair \((x_s^*,y_s^*)\) at each state. Against any distribution \(y\), \[ (x_s^*)^\top M_s(v_*)y\ge v_*(s); \] against any \(x\), \(x^\top M_s(v_*)y_s^*\le v_*(s)\). If the maximizing player uses \(x^*\) and the opponent uses any history policy, conditioning before the simultaneous actions gives \[ \mathbb E[R_{t+1}+\gamma v_*(S_{t+1})\mid H_t]\ge v_*(S_t). \] Multiply by \(\gamma^t\), take expectations, and sum \(0\le t<n\): \[ \mathbb E\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\gamma^tR_{t+1} +\gamma^n\mathbb Ev_*(S_n)\ge v_*(S_0). \] The terminal term tends to zero, and the reward series converges in \(L^1\) since its tail is bounded by \(R_{\max}\gamma^n/(1-\gamma)\). Thus \(x^*\) guarantees at least \(v_*\). The reversed inequality proves that \(y^*\) guarantees at most \(v_*\). Playing both gives equality, proving the value and stationary optimality.

The proof extends the MDP maximization to a matrix-game value. It does not extend unchanged to general-sum games, where players have distinct rewards and there is no common scalar minimax operator. If opponents are learning, one player's effective transition law changes over time; independent Q-learning no longer sees the fixed environment assumed by its convergence theorem.

External regret and coarse correlated equilibrium

For a finite one-stage general-sum game with utilities \(u_i(a_i,a_{-i})\), consider joint realized plays \(a^1,\ldots,a^T\). Define player \(i\)'s external regret \[ R_{i,T}=\max_{b_i}\sum_{t=1}^T [u_i(b_i,a_{-i}^t)-u_i(a^t)] \] and the empirical joint distribution \(\widehat q_T(a)=T^{-1}\sum_t\mathbf1_{\{a^t=a\}}\). A distribution \(q\) is a coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE) if no player benefits by committing in advance to a fixed alternative action: \[ \mathbb E_q[u_i(b_i,A_{-i})-u_i(A)]\le0 \quad\text{for every }i,b_i. \]

Proof. For each \(i,b_i\), the empirical definition gives the exact identity and bound \[ \sum_a\widehat q_T(a)[u_i(b_i,a_{-i})-u_i(a)] =\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T[u_i(b_i,a_{-i}^t)-u_i(a^t)] \le R_{i,T}/T. \] Thus if \(R_{i,T}\le\varepsilon_iT\), the empirical distribution is an approximate CCE with deviation gains at most \(\varepsilon_i\). If \(\limsup_T R_{i,T}/T\le0\), compactness of the finite simplex supplies subsequential limits. Along any convergent subsequence, every displayed finite linear sum converges; its limit is at most zero. Since there are finitely many players and alternative actions, every limit is a CCE. This proves convergence to the equilibrium set in the empirical-distribution sense, not convergence of the last joint action or of independent marginal policies to a Nash equilibrium.

Internal/swap regret permits deviations depending on the recommended action and leads to correlated-equilibrium inequalities. In Markov games, deviations affect future state visitation, so a stagewise no-regret calculation alone does not prove equilibrium for whole policies. Cooperative learning, centralized training with decentralized execution, opponent modeling, and multi-agent credit assignment each impose additional information and optimization choices.

Worked example 27.2 (matching pennies). In a one-state zero-sum game, let the reward matrix be \[ M=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}. \] If the row player chooses the first action with probability \(p\), its payoffs against the two pure columns are \(2p-1\) and \(1-2p\). Thus its guaranteed stage payoff is \(-|2p-1|\), maximized at \(p=1/2\), with value zero. The column player likewise mixes equally. With self-transition, \[ Tv=\operatorname{val}(M+\gamma v\,\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top)=\gamma v, \] so \(v_*=0\). Pure best responses can keep switching even though the equilibrium value is well defined.

Exercises

  1. Exercise 27.1. If a matrix-game solver has value error at most \(\eta\) at each backup, bound the asymptotic value-iteration error.

    Hint

    Apply the scalar contraction-with-error recurrence.

    Solution

    The recursion satisfies \(e_{n+1}\le\gamma e_n+\eta\), so \(e_n\le\gamma^ne_0+\eta(1-\gamma^n)/(1-\gamma)\). Hence \(\limsup e_n\le\eta/(1-\gamma)\). This controls value error, not automatically each player's exploitability.

  2. Exercise 27.2. Why does an empirical CCE not imply that the product of its marginal distributions is a CCE?

    Hint

    Identify which expectations depend on correlations between the players’ actions.

    Solution

    The inequalities use the joint distribution, including correlation between players' actions. Replacing it by independent marginals changes both realized utility and deviation payoffs. The regret proof supplies no equality relating these two distributions.
