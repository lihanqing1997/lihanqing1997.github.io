When several agents affect transitions and rewards, the environment faced by one learner changes as the others learn. The correct object is a stochastic game, whose solution concept and Bellman operator depend on whether interests are opposed, shared, or general-sum.

Discounted zero-sum stochastic games

At state \(s\), player 1 chooses \(a\), player 2 chooses \(b\), player 1 receives \(r(s,a,b)\), and the next state follows \(P(\cdot\mid s,a,b)\). Player 2 receives \(-r\). For mixed actions \(x\in\Delta(\mathsf A)\), \(y\in\Delta(\mathsf B)\), define \[ (TV)(s) =\max_x\min_y \sum_{a,b}x_ay_b \left\{r(s,a,b)+\gamma P_{ab}V(s)\right\}. \]

Theorem (Shapley). In a finite discounted zero-sum stochastic game, \(T\) is a \(\gamma\)-contraction in sup norm. Its unique fixed point is the game value, and stationary mixed policies attaining the statewise matrix-game saddle points are optimal.

Proof. The value of a matrix game is one-Lipschitz in the sup norm of its payoff matrix. Therefore \[ \|TV-TW\|_\infty\le\gamma\|V-W\|_\infty. \] Banach's theorem gives a unique fixed point. Finite-horizon backward induction uses the minimax theorem at each state; taking the discounted limit gives stationary saddle policies and the fixed-point value.

Minimax Q-learning

For zero-sum games, the optimal target becomes \[ R_{t+1}+\gamma \operatorname{val}\{Q_t(S_{t+1},\cdot,\cdot)\}, \] where \(\operatorname{val}\) solves the next-state matrix game. With full state-joint-action exploration and Robbins--Monro step sizes, tabular minimax Q-learning inherits contraction-based convergence. Solving a matrix game at each update replaces the ordinary maximum.

Cooperative multi-agent control

With a shared reward, the joint action \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\) turns the problem into an MDP, but its action space grows exponentially. Centralized training with decentralized execution uses global state and joint actions for critics while restricting each actor to local information. Value decomposition assumes structure such as \[ Q_{\mathrm{tot}}(s,a_1,\ldots,a_n) =\sum_iQ_i(o_i,a_i) \] or monotone mixing. These restrictions enable decentralized greedy actions but cannot represent arbitrary strategic complementarities.

General-sum games and equilibrium

For general-sum rewards \(r_i\), there may be multiple Nash equilibria with different payoffs, and independent policy learning need not converge. A stationary Nash equilibrium satisfies, for every player \(i\), \[ J_i(\pi_i^*,\pi_{-i}^*) \ge J_i(\pi_i,\pi_{-i}^*) \quad\text{for all }\pi_i. \] Best-response dynamics can cycle even in one-state games. Correlated equilibria, coarse correlated equilibria, no-regret learning, and potential games provide alternative structures with stronger convergence properties.

Theorem (no regret implies coarse correlated equilibrium). In a repeated finite game, if every player's external regret is \(o(T)\), then every weak limit of the empirical joint-action distribution is a coarse correlated equilibrium.

Proof. External regret for player \(i\) states that, for every fixed deviation \(a_i'\), \[ \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T \{u_i(a_i',a_{-i,t})-u_i(a_t)\}\le o(1). \] These are exactly the finite collection of linear coarse-correlated-equilibrium inequalities for the empirical distribution. Passing to a weakly convergent subsequence preserves them.

Nonstationarity and credit assignment

From one agent's viewpoint, other learners make transition and reward laws nonstationary, invalidating ordinary replay and Markov-chain arguments. Counterfactual baselines, difference rewards, opponent modeling, and population training address credit and adaptation, but guarantees require an explicit game class and solution concept. “Multi-agent performance” without naming the equilibrium or regret criterion is mathematically incomplete.
