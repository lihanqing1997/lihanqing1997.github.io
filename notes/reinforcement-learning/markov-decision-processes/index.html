Learning goal. Specify the probability law behind an algorithm, distinguish the policy from the environment, and justify infinite discounted returns.

A common model and notation

Definition 1.1 (Finite discounted MDP and trajectory law).

The core chapters use a finite state set \(\mathsf S\), finite nonempty action sets \(\mathsf A(s)\), an initial distribution \(\rho\), and \(0\le\gamma<1\). The environment is a joint kernel \(K(\mathrm{d}r,s'\mid s,a)\) for the reward \(R_{t+1}\) and next state \(S_{t+1}\). Its transition marginal and reward mean are \[ P(s'\mid s,a)=\int K(\mathrm{d}r,s'\mid s,a),\qquad r(s,a)=\sum_{s'}\int r\,K(\mathrm{d}r,s'\mid s,a). \] Unless a chapter states otherwise, \(|R_{t+1}|\le R_{\max}\) almost surely. This is a bound on realized rewards, not merely on their conditional means. Reward and next state may be dependent.

Write \(H_t=(S_0,A_0,R_1,S_1,\ldots,A_{t-1},R_t,S_t)\) and \(\mathcal H_t=\sigma(H_t)\). A history-dependent policy is a sequence \(\pi_t(a\mid H_t)\). The model assumptions are \[ \mathbb P(A_t=a\mid\mathcal H_t)=\pi_t(a\mid H_t),\qquad \mathbb P((R_{t+1},S_{t+1})\in C\mid\mathcal H_t,A_t) =K(C\mid S_t,A_t). \] For example, the law of a finite trajectory is the iterated product \[ \rho(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{n-1} \pi_t(a_t\mid h_t)K(\mathrm{d}r_{t+1},s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t). \] These compatible finite laws define the infinite trajectory law by the standard extension theorem for kernels. A stationary Markov policy has \(\pi_t(a\mid H_t)=\pi(a\mid S_t)\); a deterministic one chooses a single action at each state.

From a policy to a Markov chain

Proposition 1.2 (From a policy to a Markov chain). Under a stationary Markov policy, \[ P_\pi(s,s')=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)P(s'\mid s,a),\qquad r_\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)r(s,a) \] are the state transition matrix and expected one-step reward.

Proof. For every \(s'\), conditional expectation and the finite partition by actions give \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb P(S_{t+1}=s'\mid\mathcal H_t) &=\sum_a\mathbb P(A_t=a\mid\mathcal H_t) \mathbb P(S_{t+1}=s'\mid\mathcal H_t,A_t=a)\\ &=\sum_a\pi(a\mid S_t)P(s'\mid S_t,a)=P_\pi(S_t,s'). \end{aligned} \] Thus the conditional law depends only on \(S_t\). Likewise, \[ \mathbb E[R_{t+1}\mid\mathcal H_t] =\sum_a\pi(a\mid S_t)r(S_t,a)=r_\pi(S_t). \] Both calculations use the environment kernel; the policy does not change \(K\).

Discounting and legitimate limiting operations

Proposition 1.3 (return and truncation). For every policy, define \[ G^{(n)}=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\gamma^tR_{t+1},\qquad G=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR_{t+1},\qquad J_\rho(\pi)=\mathbb E_\rho^\pi G. \] Then \(G\) exists absolutely almost surely, \[ |G|\le\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma},\qquad |G-G^{(n)}|\le\frac{R_{\max}\gamma^n}{1-\gamma}, \] and \(J_\rho(\pi)=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t\mathbb E_\rho^\pi r(S_t,A_t)\).

Proof. The deterministic majorant gives \[ \sum_{t\ge0}|\gamma^tR_{t+1}| \le R_{\max}\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^t=\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}. \] For \(m>n\), \[ |G^{(m)}-G^{(n)}|\le R_{\max}\sum_{t=n}^{m-1}\gamma^t \le\frac{R_{\max}\gamma^n}{1-\gamma}. \] Hence partial sums are Cauchy and the same bound holds for the infinite tail. In particular, \(\mathbb E|G-G^{(n)}|\to0\). Finite linearity and the tower property yield \[ \mathbb E G^{(n)} =\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\gamma^t\mathbb E R_{t+1} =\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\gamma^t\mathbb E r(S_t,A_t). \] The left side tends to \(\mathbb E G\) in \(L^1\); the right side is an absolutely convergent numerical series since \(|r|\le R_{\max}\). This proves the identity without exchanging an uncontrolled infinite sum and expectation.

History dependence and Markovization

Theorem 1.4 (History dependence and Markovization). For any history-dependent policy \(\pi\), there is a possibly time-dependent Markov policy \(\bar\pi_t(a\mid s)\) with the same joint law of \((S_t,A_t)\) at every time. Consequently \(J_\rho(\bar\pi)=J_\rho(\pi)\).

Proof. Set \(m_t(s,a)=\mathbb P_\rho^\pi(S_t=s,A_t=a)\) and \(m_t(s)=\sum_a m_t(s,a)\). Define \[ \bar\pi_t(a\mid s)=\frac{m_t(s,a)}{m_t(s)}\quad\text{if }m_t(s)>0, \] and choose any probability vector when \(m_t(s)=0\). Suppose the state law under \(\bar\pi\) is \(m_t\), as it is at \(t=0\). Then \[ \mathbb P^{\bar\pi}(S_t=s,A_t=a)=m_t(s)\bar\pi_t(a\mid s)=m_t(s,a), \] including zero-mass states, and \[ \mathbb P^{\bar\pi}(S_{t+1}=s') =\sum_{s,a}m_t(s,a)P(s'\mid s,a)=m_{t+1}(s'). \] Induction proves equality of all state-action marginals. For every \(n\), the expected truncated rewards therefore agree. The previous tail bound gives \[ |J_\rho(\bar\pi)-J_\rho(\pi)| \le\frac{2R_{\max}\gamma^n}{1-\gamma}\longrightarrow0. \] This does not make \(\bar\pi\) stationary. Stationary optimality will follow later from Bellman verification.

Worked example 1.5 (a two-state reward process). Let state \(g\) reward \(1\) and move to absorbing state \(b\) with probability \(p\); at \(b\), reward is \(0\). Starting at \(g\), \(\mathbb P(S_t=g)=(1-p)^t\), so \[ J_g=\sum_{t\ge0}[\gamma(1-p)]^t=\frac1{1-\gamma(1-p)}. \] For \(p=1/2\) and \(\gamma=0.9\), this is \(20/11\), rather than the upper bound \(10\). Transition persistence and discounting both determine the effective reward horizon.

Other regimes and prerequisites

Finite-horizon problems use a time index and backward recursion. Average reward requires recurrence assumptions rather than setting \(\gamma=1\) in the results above. On standard Borel spaces, sums become kernel integrals; measurable selection and integrability must be checked before asserting optimal policies. Continuous-state LQR and belief-state control appear later with their own assumptions. The general-space discussion is an extension, not an implicit hypothesis change in the finite core.

Exercises

  1. Exercise 1.1. If \(R_{\max}=1\) and \(\gamma=0.9\), how many terms guarantee return truncation error at most \(0.01\)?

    Hint

    Insert the reward and discount values into the deterministic tail bound and solve the geometric inequality.

    Solution

    Solve \(10(0.9)^n\le0.01\). Thus \(n\ge\log(0.001)/\log(0.9)\), and \(n=66\) suffices. This is a deterministic bound, not a confidence statement.

  2. Exercise 1.2. Can Markovization preserve all correlations between actions at different times?

    Hint

    Compare a single persistent random choice with fresh independent choices at each time.

    Solution

    No. In a one-state MDP, choose a fair bit at time zero and repeat it forever. Markovization uses a fresh fair action at each time. Both have the same one-time marginals, but the original has \(\mathbb P(A_1=A_0)=1\) and the new policy has probability \(1/2\). Additive expected returns agree despite different path laws.
