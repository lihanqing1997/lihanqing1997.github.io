Dirichlet characters are the finite Fourier modes of \((\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^\times\). They isolate residue classes in prime counting, and their \(L\)-functions extend Euler's argument from all primes to primes in a prescribed arithmetic progression.

Characters and orthogonality

Definition. A Dirichlet character modulo \(q\) is a group homomorphism \[ \chi:(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^\times\to\mathbb C^\times \] extended periodically to \(\mathbb Z\) by \(\chi(n)=0\) when \(\gcd(n,q)>1\). The principal character \(\chi_0\) equals \(1\) on units.

Orthogonality relations. For \(a,b\in(\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z)^\times\), \[ \frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\bmod q}\chi(a)\overline{\chi(b)} =\mathbf1_{\{a=b\}}, \] and for characters \(\chi,\psi\), \[ \frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_{a\bmod q}^{*} \chi(a)\overline{\psi(a)}=\mathbf1_{\{\chi=\psi\}}. \]

Proof. If a character \(\eta\) is nontrivial, choose \(g\) with \(\eta(g)\ne1\). Translation by \(g\) permutes the finite group, so \[ \sum_a\eta(a)=\sum_a\eta(ga)=\eta(g)\sum_a\eta(a), \] forcing the sum to vanish. Apply this to \(\eta=\chi\overline\psi\), and dually to the character group.

Dirichlet \(L\)-functions

Definition. For \(\operatorname{Re}s>1\), \[ L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} =\prod_p(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})^{-1}. \]

For a nonprincipal character, boundedness of partial sums \(\sum_{n\le x}\chi(n)\) and summation by parts extend \(L(s,\chi)\) holomorphically to \(\operatorname{Re}s>0\). The principal \(L\)-function is \[ L(s,\chi_0)=\zeta(s)\prod_{p\mid q}(1-p^{-s}) \] and has a simple pole at \(s=1\).

Theorem (nonvanishing at one). For every nonprincipal character \(\chi\), \[ L(1,\chi)\ne0. \]

For complex characters this follows from positivity after combining conjugate Euler products. Real characters require a subtler argument: assuming \(L(1,\chi)=0\) would force coefficients of \(\zeta(s)L(s,\chi)\) to violate their nonnegative partial-sum growth. This nonvanishing is the decisive analytic input.

Primes in arithmetic progressions

Theorem (Dirichlet). If \(\gcd(a,q)=1\), there are infinitely many primes \[ p\equiv a\pmod q. \] In fact, \[ \pi(x;q,a)\sim\frac1{\varphi(q)}\frac{x}{\log x}. \]

Analytic reduction. Orthogonality extracts the residue class: \[ \mathbf1_{\{n\equiv a\bmod q\}} =\frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\bmod q}\overline{\chi(a)}\chi(n). \] Applying this to the von Mangoldt function gives \[ \psi(x;q,a) =\frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(a)} \sum_{n\le x}\chi(n)\Lambda(n). \] The Dirichlet series of the inner sum is \(-L'(s,\chi)/L(s,\chi)\). Only the principal character has a pole at \(s=1\); nonvanishing on the boundary controls all others. A Tauberian argument yields \(\psi(x;q,a)\sim x/\varphi(q)\), and partial summation gives the stated prime asymptotic.

Example. Modulo \(4\), the nontrivial character is \(\chi_4(n)=0\) for even \(n\), \(1\) for \(n\equiv1\), and \(-1\) for \(n\equiv3\). Its value \[ L(1,\chi_4)=1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots=\frac\pi4 \] is nonzero, and the primes are asymptotically split equally between \(1\) and \(3\) modulo \(4\).
