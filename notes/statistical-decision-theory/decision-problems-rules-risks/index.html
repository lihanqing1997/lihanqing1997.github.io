A decision rule maps data into consequences. Decision theory keeps the observation model, action space, and loss separate, because the same experiment may support estimation, testing, prediction, classification, or confidence procedures. The risk integrates data randomness and any deliberate randomization in the action.

Measurable decision rules

Definition. A statistical decision problem consists of:

  1. a parameter set \(\Theta\);
  2. an experiment \(\mathcal E=(\mathcal X,\mathcal A_{\mathcal X},\{P_\theta\})\);
  3. an action space \((\mathbb A,\mathcal A_{\mathbb A})\);
  4. a jointly measurable loss \(L:\Theta\times\mathbb A\to[0,\infty]\).

A randomized rule is a Markov kernel \(\delta(x,\mathrm{d}a)\) from \(\mathcal X\) to \(\mathbb A\). Its risk is \[ R(\theta,\delta) = \int_{\mathcal X}\int_{\mathbb A} L(\theta,a)\delta(x,\mathrm{d}a)P_\theta(\mathrm{d}x). \]

Standard Borel observation and action spaces ensure that conditional distributions and kernel compositions behave well. A nonrandomized measurable map \(d:\mathcal X\to\mathbb A\) corresponds to the degenerate kernel \(\delta(x,\cdot)=\delta_{d(x)}\).

Examples of actions and losses

Example (point estimation). For \(\mathbb A=\Theta\subset\mathbb R^p\) and quadratic loss \[ L(\theta,a)=\|a-\theta\|_2^2, \] the risk is mean squared error. With a positive semidefinite matrix \(W(\theta)\), the local quadratic loss \[ L(\theta,a)=(a-\theta)^\top W(\theta)(a-\theta) \] permits parameter-dependent geometry.

Example (testing). For \(H_0:\theta\in\Theta_0\) against \(H_1:\theta\in\Theta_1\), take \(\mathbb A=\{0,1\}\), where \(1\) denotes rejection, and \[ L(\theta,a) = c_0\,1_{\Theta_0}(\theta)a +c_1\,1_{\Theta_1}(\theta)(1-a). \] The two pieces of risk are weighted type-I and type-II error probabilities.

Example (confidence set). Let an action be a measurable subset \(C\subset\Theta\). A loss can trade noncoverage against size: \[ L(\theta,C)= 1\{\theta\notin C\}+\lambda\,\nu(C), \] where \(\nu\) is a volume or complexity measure. Thus confidence procedures also fit decision theory, although coverage constraints are often imposed directly rather than scalarized by one \(\lambda\).

Convexification by randomization

Proposition. If \(\delta_0,\delta_1\) are decision rules and \(0\leq\lambda\leq1\), the kernel \[ \delta_\lambda =\lambda\delta_1+(1-\lambda)\delta_0 \] is a decision rule with \[ R(\theta,\delta_\lambda) = \lambda R(\theta,\delta_1) +(1-\lambda)R(\theta,\delta_0) \] for every \(\theta\).

Proof. A convex combination of probability kernels is again a probability kernel, and measurability is preserved. Linearity of integration gives \[ \begin{aligned} R(\theta,\delta_\lambda) &=\int P_\theta(\mathrm{d}x)\int L(\theta,a) \{\lambda\delta_1+(1-\lambda)\delta_0\}(x,\mathrm{d}a)\\ &=\lambda R(\theta,\delta_1) +(1-\lambda)R(\theta,\delta_0). \end{aligned} \] \(\square\)

This mixture may be implemented by drawing one independent Bernoulli variable before seeing the data. More general randomized rules permit the action distribution to depend on \(x\). For convex losses and convex action spaces, conditional Jensen often shows that randomization is unnecessary for estimation, but it remains essential for exact tests and for convex-analytic closure.

Dominance and admissibility

Definition. A rule \(\delta_1\) dominates \(\delta_0\) if \[ R(\theta,\delta_1)\leq R(\theta,\delta_0) \quad\text{for every }\theta, \] with strict inequality for at least one \(\theta\). A rule is admissible if it is not dominated.

Admissibility is a Pareto property of the full risk function. It does not assert that the risk is small, only that no other rule is uniformly no worse and somewhere better. Two admissible rules may cross substantially.

Bayes, minimax, and constrained values

Definition. For a prior \(\Pi\), the Bayes value is \[ r_\Pi^*=\inf_\delta\int R(\theta,\delta)\Pi(\mathrm{d}\theta). \] The minimax value is \[ r^*=\inf_\delta\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}R(\theta,\delta). \] A constrained value, for example in testing, is \[ \inf_{\delta:\,\sup_{\theta\in\Theta_0}R_0(\theta,\delta)\leq\alpha} \sup_{\theta\in\Theta_1}R_1(\theta,\delta). \]

These are different scalarizations of one risk set. A prior uses a positive linear functional; minimax uses the support function in the direction of the constant vector; admissibility retains the full coordinatewise partial order.

Posterior minimization

Theorem (Bayes rule by posterior loss). Suppose a prior \(\Pi\) and regular conditional posterior \(\Pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x)\) exist. If a measurable action \(a_\Pi(x)\) minimizes \[ \rho_\Pi(a\mid x) = \int L(\theta,a)\Pi(\mathrm{d}\theta\mid x) \] for almost every prior-predictive \(x\), then \(a_\Pi\) is a Bayes rule.

Proof. Under the joint law \(\Pi(\mathrm{d}\theta)P_\theta(\mathrm{d}x)\), iterated expectation gives \[ r(\Pi,\delta) = \int\left[ \int_{\mathbb A}\rho_\Pi(a\mid x)\delta(x,\mathrm{d}a) \right]m_\Pi(\mathrm{d}x), \] where \(m_\Pi\) is the prior predictive law. Pointwise minimization of the bracketed posterior expected loss minimizes its integral. \(\square\)

Corollary. Under squared-error loss, the posterior mean is Bayes; under absolute-error loss, any posterior median is Bayes; under \(0\)-\(1\) loss on a discrete parameter space, a posterior mode is Bayes.

Decision theory therefore distinguishes three sources of uncertainty cleanly: the sampling law \(P_\theta\), deliberate randomization in \(\delta\), and Bayesian averaging over \(\Pi\). Risk is frequentist even for a Bayes rule; it remains a function of the fixed true parameter.
