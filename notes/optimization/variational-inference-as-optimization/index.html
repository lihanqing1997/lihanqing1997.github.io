Variational inference turns posterior approximation into optimization over probability measures. The objective, feasible family, and divergence jointly determine both computational tractability and the approximation's systematic error.

Reverse KL and the evidence lower bound

Let \(p(x,z)\) be a joint density and \(\pi(z\mid x)=p(x,z)/p(x)\) the posterior. For any density \(q\) absolutely continuous with respect to the posterior, \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{KL}(q\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)) &=\mathbb E_q\log\frac{q(Z)}{p(x,Z)}+\log p(x),\\ \mathcal L(q) &=\mathbb E_q[\log p(x,Z)]-\mathbb E_q[\log q(Z)]. \end{aligned} \] Therefore \[ \log p(x)=\mathcal L(q)+ \operatorname{KL}(q\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)). \]

Variational projection. For a family \(\mathcal Q\), \[ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{q\in\mathcal Q}\mathcal L(q) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q\in\mathcal Q} \operatorname{KL}(q\|\pi(\cdot\mid x)). \] The ELBO gap to \(\log p(x)\) is exactly the reverse-KL approximation error.

The equality follows immediately from the decomposition because \(\log p(x)\) is fixed with respect to \(q\). It also shows that an increasing ELBO need not imply an accurate posterior unless the variational family can make the KL gap small.

Mean-field coordinate optimization

Coordinate-ascent update. Let \(z=(z_1,\ldots,z_m)\) and \(\mathcal Q=\{q:q(z)=\prod_{j=1}^mq_j(z_j)\}\). Holding all factors except \(q_j\) fixed, the ELBO is maximized by \[ q_j^\star(z_j) = \frac{\exp\{\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}\log p(x,z_j,Z_{-j})\}} {\int\exp\{\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}\log p(x,u,Z_{-j})\}\,\mathrm{d}u}, \] whenever the normalizing integral is finite.

Proof. Terms independent of \(q_j\) may be discarded. The coordinate objective is \[ \int q_j(z_j)h_j(z_j)\,\mathrm{d}z_j -\int q_j(z_j)\log q_j(z_j)\,\mathrm{d}z_j, \quad h_j=\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}\log p(x,z). \] If \(\widetilde q_j\propto e^{h_j}\), this expression equals \(\log\int e^{h_j}-\operatorname{KL}(q_j\|\widetilde q_j)\). It is uniquely maximized at \(\widetilde q_j\).

Each exact coordinate update increases the ELBO, but the mean-field objective is generally nonconcave in all factors jointly. Coordinate ascent can therefore depend on initialization and converge to a local optimum.

Forward and reverse KL projections

Separated modes. Suppose \(\pi=\frac12N(-a,1)+\frac12N(a,1)\) with large \(a\), while \(\mathcal Q\) contains single Gaussian densities. Minimizing \(\operatorname{KL}(q\|\pi)\) heavily penalizes placing \(q\)-mass between the modes where \(\pi\) is tiny; a local optimum can concentrate on one mode. Minimizing \(\operatorname{KL}(\pi\|q)\) instead penalizes failing to cover either mode and favors a broad Gaussian centered near zero. This is the mode-seeking versus mass-covering distinction.

Neither behavior is universally superior. Reverse KL is computationally convenient because expectations are under \(q\); forward KL often requires posterior expectations that are unavailable precisely because inference is difficult.

Stochastic gradients of variational objectives

For a differentiable reparametrization \(Z=T_\lambda(\varepsilon)\), \(\varepsilon\sim s\), the pathwise gradient is \[ \nabla_\lambda\mathcal L(\lambda) =\mathbb E_{\varepsilon\sim s} \nabla_\lambda[ \log p(x,T_\lambda(\varepsilon)) -\log q_\lambda(T_\lambda(\varepsilon))]. \] When reparametrization is unavailable, the score identity gives \[ \nabla_\lambda\mathbb E_{q_\lambda}h_\lambda(Z) =\mathbb E_{q_\lambda} [\nabla_\lambda h_\lambda(Z) +h_\lambda(Z)\nabla_\lambda\log q_\lambda(Z)]. \] Both identities require domination conditions permitting differentiation under the integral. Control variates and Rao-Blackwellization reduce their Monte Carlo variance.

The Bayesian consequences of the projection, including contraction and uncertainty, are developed in variational Bayes and KL projections. Here the emphasis is the optimization structure and its stationary equations.
