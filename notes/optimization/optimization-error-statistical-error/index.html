A computed estimator differs from an ideal population target for three reasons: the model class may be restricted, the sample criterion fluctuates, and the algorithm may stop before exact empirical optimization. These errors live in different mathematical spaces and should not be collapsed into one tolerance.

Excess-risk decomposition

Let \(\mathcal F\) be a model class, \(R(f)=P\ell_f\) population risk, and \(\widehat R_n(f)=P_n\ell_f\) empirical risk. Let \[ f^\circ\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{f\in\mathcal F}R(f),\qquad \widehat f_n\in\mathcal F, \] with \[ \widehat R_n(\widehat f_n) \le\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}\widehat R_n(f)+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}. \]

Basic excess-risk bound. \[ R(\widehat f_n)-R(f^\circ) \le 2\sup_{f\in\mathcal F} |\widehat R_n(f)-R(f)| +\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}. \] Relative to an unrestricted Bayes rule \(f^\star\), add the approximation error \(R(f^\circ)-R(f^\star)\).

Proof. Add and subtract empirical risks: \[ \begin{aligned} R(\widehat f_n)-R(f^\circ) &=[R-\widehat R_n](\widehat f_n) +[\widehat R_n(\widehat f_n)-\widehat R_n(f^\circ)]\\ &\quad+[\widehat R_n-R](f^\circ). \end{aligned} \] The middle term is at most \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}\), and each outer term is bounded by the uniform deviation.

This inequality is deliberately agnostic about algorithms and probability. Empirical-process or concentration theory controls the deviation; an optimization theorem controls \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}\).

Objective tolerance versus parameter tolerance

Curvature conversion. Suppose the empirical criterion \(M_n\) is \(\mu\)-strongly convex with exact minimizer \(\widehat\theta_n\), and an algorithm returns \(\widetilde\theta_n\) with \[ M_n(\widetilde\theta_n)-M_n(\widehat\theta_n) \le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}. \] Then \[ \|\widetilde\theta_n-\widehat\theta_n\| \le\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}}{\mu}}. \]

If statistical estimation error is \(r_n\) in parameter norm, making algorithmic error negligible requires \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt}}=o(\mu r_n^2)\). For an asymptotically linear root-\(n\) estimator, a gradient-based stopping rule often needs \(\|\nabla M_n(\widetilde\theta_n)\|=o_P(n^{-1/2})\), which is more directly connected to the estimating equation than machine precision is.

Early stopping at the statistical scale

Example. Suppose an algorithm has deterministic optimization error \(M_n(\theta_k)-\inf M_n\le C\rho^k\), \(0<\rho<1\), while the statistical excess-risk scale is \(a_n\). Choosing \[ k_n\ge \frac{\log(C/a_n)}{\log(1/\rho)} \] makes optimization error no larger than sampling error. Further iterations improve the numerical solution but not the leading statistical rate. Early stopping can also act as regularization, in which case its bias is part of the statistical analysis rather than disposable optimization error.

Uniform stability of regularized ERM

Replace-one stability. Let \[ F_S(w)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(w;Z_i)+\frac\lambda2\|w\|^2, \] where each \(\ell(\cdot;z)\) is convex and \(L\)-Lipschitz. Let \(w_S\) minimize \(F_S\), and let \(S^{(i)}\) replace one observation. Then \[ \|w_S-w_{S^{(i)}}\|\le\frac{2L}{\lambda n}, \] and for every \(z\), \[ |\ell(w_S;z)-\ell(w_{S^{(i)}};z)| \le\frac{2L^2}{\lambda n}. \]

Proof. Strong convexity and optimality imply \[ F_S(w_{S^{(i)}})-F_S(w_S) \ge\frac\lambda2\|w_S-w_{S^{(i)}}\|^2, \] and the analogous inequality with \(S,S^{(i)}\) reversed. Add them. All common loss terms cancel, leaving only the two replaced losses, whose total change is at most \((2L/n)\|w_S-w_{S^{(i)}}\|\). Cancel one distance factor to obtain the parameter bound. Lipschitz continuity gives the loss bound.

Taking expectation over a fresh observation and a replaced training point turns this stability bound into an expected generalization bound of the same order. The optimization geometry, here strong convexity, directly controls sensitivity to the data.

A valid stopping rule must therefore be matched to the inferential target: objective error for prediction, parameter or score error for asymptotic inference, feasibility error for constrained estimands, and distributional error for approximate posteriors.
