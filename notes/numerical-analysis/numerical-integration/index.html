Numerical integration replaces an integral by a weighted finite sum. The central questions are exactness, smoothness, and error control.

Quadrature rule

Definition. A quadrature rule approximates \(\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) by \(\sum_{j=1}^n w_j f(x_j)\). The nodes \(x_j\) and weights \(w_j\) determine the degree of exactness and stability of the rule.

Newton-Cotes rules

Proposition. Newton-Cotes rules integrate the polynomial interpolant through equally spaced nodes. The trapezoidal and Simpson rules are the most stable low-order examples; high-order closed Newton-Cotes rules may have undesirable weights.

Gaussian quadrature

Theorem. For a positive weight on an interval, the \(n\)-point Gaussian quadrature rule chooses nodes as zeros of an orthogonal polynomial and is exact for all polynomials of degree at most \(2n-1\).

Error representation

Proposition. Quadrature error can be expressed through derivatives, Peano kernels, or orthogonal polynomial remainders. Each representation emphasizes a different cause of error: smoothness, endpoint behavior, or projection residual.

Oscillatory and singular integrals

Remark. No single quadrature rule is universally best. Oscillation, endpoint singularities, high dimension, and nonsmoothness each require specialized transformations or adaptive strategies.

Interpolatory quadrature

Choose nodes \(x_i\) and integrate the interpolant: \[ \int_a^bf(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \approx\sum_{i=0}^nw_if(x_i), \qquad w_i=\int_a^b\ell_i(x)\,\mathrm{d}x. \] The rule is exact on \(\mathcal P_n\). Symmetry can increase the degree of exactness by canceling odd moments.

Theorem (composite trapezoid error). For \(f\in C^2[a,b]\) and a uniform mesh of width \(h=(b-a)/N\), \[ \int_a^bf(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-T_N(f) =-\frac{b-a}{12}h^2f''(\xi) \] for some \(\xi\in(a,b)\), and therefore \[ |\text{error}| \le\frac{b-a}{12}h^2\|f''\|_\infty. \]

Proof. On each subinterval, integrate the linear-interpolation remainder \[ f(x)-I_1f(x) =\frac{f''(\xi_x)}2(x-x_j)(x-x_{j+1}). \] The integral of the quadratic factor is \(-h^3/6\), giving local error \(-h^3f''(\xi_j)/12\). Sum and apply the weighted mean-value theorem.

Gaussian quadrature

Theorem. Let \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) be the zeros of the degree-\(n\) orthogonal polynomial for positive weight \(w\). There are positive weights \(\omega_i\) such that \[ \int_a^bf(x)w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \approx\sum_{i=1}^n\omega_if(x_i) \] is exact for every polynomial of degree at most \(2n-1\). No \(n\)-node rule has a higher degree of exactness.

Proof. Divide \(p\in\mathcal P_{2n-1}\) as \(p=q\phi_n+r\), with \(q,r\in\mathcal P_{n-1}\). Orthogonality makes \(\int q\phi_nw=0\), while \(\phi_n(x_i)=0\), so both integral and quadrature reduce to \(r\), interpolated exactly at the \(n\) nodes. A polynomial \(\prod_i(x-x_i)^2\) proves that degree \(2n\) cannot be exact for a positive weight.

Peano kernels

If a linear quadrature error functional \(L\) annihilates \(\mathcal P_{m-1}\), then \[ L(f)=\int_a^bf^{(m)}(t)K(t)\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad K(t)=L\left(\frac{(\,\cdot-t)_+^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}\right). \] This Peano-kernel representation yields sharp norm bounds and clarifies the sign of an error. It replaces repeated appeals to an unspecified intermediate point when the derivative changes sign.

Periodic analytic functions

For periodic \(f\) analytic in a strip around the real axis, the composite trapezoid rule converges exponentially. Fourier modes sampled on an equispaced grid alias, and all nonmultiples of the grid frequency cancel exactly. This explains why a nominally second-order rule can be spectrally accurate in the periodic analytic setting.
