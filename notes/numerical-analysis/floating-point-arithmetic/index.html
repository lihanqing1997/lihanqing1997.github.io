Floating-point arithmetic is the model that explains why algebraically equivalent formulas may have very different numerical behavior.

Floating-point system

Definition. A floating-point system stores numbers as \(\pm m\beta^e\), where the base \(\beta\), precision, and exponent range are finite. The rounding map \(\mathrm{fl}\) sends a real number to a nearby representable number.

Standard rounding model

Lemma. For a normalized operation \(\circ\in\{+,-,\times,/\}\), the computed result is modeled as \(\mathrm{fl}(a\circ b)=(a\circ b)(1+\delta)\), with \(|\delta|\le u\), provided no overflow or underflow occurs. The constant \(u\) is the unit roundoff.

Catastrophic cancellation

Proposition. Subtracting nearly equal numbers can turn small relative input errors into large relative output errors. If \(x\approx y\), then the relative condition of \(x-y\) with respect to perturbations in \(x,y\) is proportional to \((|x|+|y|)/|x-y|\).

Stable summation

Remark. Summing in a fixed order may lose small terms when magnitudes vary. Pairwise summation and compensated summation reduce error growth; the point is not that rounding disappears, but that the rounding errors are prevented from accumulating in the worst possible order.

Backward stability of elementary operations

Proof. The standard rounding model can be rewritten as an exact operation on slightly perturbed inputs. This observation is the seed of backward error analysis: a computed answer is accepted when it solves a nearby problem and the nearby problem is statistically or physically indistinguishable from the original one.

The standard model

For normalized binary floating point with precision \(p\), unit roundoff is \(u=2^{-p}\) under rounding to nearest. Away from underflow and overflow, \[ \operatorname{fl}(x)=x(1+\delta),\qquad|\delta|\le u, \] and for \(\circ\in\{+,-,\times,/\}\), \[ \operatorname{fl}(x\circ y)=(x\circ y)(1+\delta), \qquad|\delta|\le u. \] The relative model for addition is valid for the rounded exact result, even though cancellation can make that exact result small relative to the operands.

Products of rounding factors

Lemma. If \(|\delta_i|\le u\), \(nu<1\), then \[ \prod_{i=1}^n(1+\delta_i)^{\rho_i}=1+\theta_n, \qquad |\theta_n|\le\gamma_n:=\frac{nu}{1-nu}, \] for \(\rho_i\in\{-1,1\}\), after harmless adjustment of individual \(\delta_i\).

Proof. For positive factors, \[ \prod_i(1+|\delta_i|) \le(1+u)^n\le\frac1{1-nu}, \] using the binomial series comparison. The lower deviation is bounded similarly through inverse factors. Combining gives the standard \(\gamma_n\) envelope.

Sequential summation

Theorem. Naive left-to-right summation of \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) produces \[ \widehat s=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i(1+\theta_{n-i+1}), \qquad |\theta_k|\le\gamma_k. \] Hence \[ |\widehat s-\sum_i x_i| \le\gamma_n\sum_i|x_i|. \]

Proof. Unroll each rounded addition. Each input is multiplied by the rounding factors from its insertion through the last addition. Apply the product lemma and the triangle inequality.

The relative error bound is \[ \frac{|\widehat s-s|}{|s|} \le\gamma_n\frac{\sum_i|x_i|}{|\sum_i x_i|}. \] The second factor is the condition number of summation and becomes large under cancellation. No summation algorithm can recover digits destroyed by ill-conditioned data, though pairwise or compensated summation reduces algorithmic error.

Pairwise and compensated summation

A balanced pairwise tree has depth \(\lceil\log_2n\rceil\), giving an error of order \(\gamma_{\lceil\log_2n\rceil}\sum|x_i|\). Kahan summation carries a correction for low-order bits and, under standard assumptions, achieves a leading error near \(2u\sum|x_i|\), essentially independent of \(n\), plus higher-order terms.

Cancellation and stable reformulation

Example. For large positive \(x\), \[ \sqrt{x^2+1}-x \] loses significant digits. Rationalization gives \[ \frac1{\sqrt{x^2+1}+x}, \] which is algebraically identical but avoids subtracting close numbers. Stability often comes from changing the formula, not increasing precision.

Underflow, overflow, and subnormals

The relative-error model fails at gradual underflow, where an absolute error bound is more appropriate. Scaling, logarithmic representations, and log-sum-exp prevent avoidable range failures. Reproducibility can also depend on evaluation order, fused multiply-add, compiler contraction, and parallel reduction trees.
