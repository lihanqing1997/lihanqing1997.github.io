Semiparametric estimators combine a low-dimensional target with one or more infinite-dimensional nuisance functions. Plugging estimated nuisances into a score creates a random empirical-process index. Classical Donsker arguments control that index on the same sample; sample splitting converts it into a conditionally fixed function. Orthogonality controls the separate population bias.

An asymptotically linear target

Definition. An estimator of a scalar functional \(\theta(P)\) is asymptotically linear with influence function \(\varphi_P\in L_2^0(P)\) if \[ \hat\theta-\theta(P) = P_n\varphi_P+o_p(n^{-1/2}), \qquad P\varphi_P=0. \] Then \[ \sqrt n\{\hat\theta-\theta(P)\} \rightsquigarrow N(0,P\varphi_P^2). \]

In practice \(\varphi_P=\varphi(\cdot;\eta_0)\) depends on nuisance functions \(\eta_0\), and the estimator uses \(\hat\eta\). The fundamental decomposition is \[ \begin{aligned} (P_n-P)\varphi_{\hat\eta}+P\varphi_{\hat\eta} &=(P_n-P)\varphi_{\eta_0}\\ &\quad +(P_n-P)(\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}) +P(\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}). \end{aligned} \] The first term produces the limit. The second is a random-index empirical process. The third is plug-in bias.

The same-sample Donsker route

Theorem. Suppose, with probability tending to one, \(\varphi_{\hat\eta}\in\mathcal F\), where \(\mathcal F\) is \(P\)-Donsker, and \[ \|\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}\|_{P,2}\to_p0. \] Then \[ \mathbb G_n(\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}) =o_p(1). \]

Proof. A Donsker process is asymptotically equicontinuous in its canonical semimetric, which is bounded by \(L_2(P)\)-distance. Apply the random-index lemma to \(\varphi_{\hat\eta}\) and \(\varphi_{\eta_0}\). \(\square\)

The fixed-class condition is substantive. A flexible learner can have excellent \(L_2\)-risk while ranging over a class too large to be Donsker. The data-interpolation counterexample on page 9 shows that same-sample \(L_2\)-consistency by itself is insufficient.

Neyman orthogonality

Definition. Let \[ \Psi(\theta,\eta)=P\psi(W;\theta,\eta), \qquad \Psi(\theta_0,\eta_0)=0. \] The score is Neyman orthogonal at \((\theta_0,\eta_0)\) if, for every admissible nuisance direction \(h\), \[ \partial_\eta\Psi(\theta_0,\eta_0)[h] = \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \Psi(\theta_0,\eta_0+th)\right|_{t=0} =0. \]

Proposition. If \(\Psi\) is twice differentiable in \(\eta\), orthogonality and a bounded second derivative imply \[ |\Psi(\theta_0,\hat\eta)-\Psi(\theta_0,\eta_0)| \lesssim\|\hat\eta-\eta_0\|^2. \] With several nuisance components, the remainder is often a product of their errors rather than the square of one norm.

Proof. Taylor's formula along the segment \(\eta_t=\eta_0+t(\hat\eta-\eta_0)\) gives \[ \Psi(\theta_0,\hat\eta)-\Psi(\theta_0,\eta_0) = \partial_\eta\Psi(\theta_0,\eta_0)[\hat\eta-\eta_0] +\int_0^1(1-t) \partial_{\eta\eta}^2\Psi(\theta_0,\eta_t) [\hat\eta-\eta_0]^{\otimes2}\,\mathrm{d}t. \] The first term vanishes by orthogonality; boundedness of the bilinear second derivative controls the integral. \(\square\)

Orthogonality controls the population term \(P(\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0})\). It does not, by itself, control the empirical-process term. These are logically different remainders.

Cross-fitting

Partition the observations into folds \(I_1,\ldots,I_K\). For fold \(k\), estimate \(\hat\eta_{-k}\) using observations outside \(I_k\), then evaluate the score on \(I_k\). Conditional on the training data, \(\varphi_{\hat\eta_{-k}}\) is fixed relative to the held-out observations.

Lemma (cross-fitted empirical remainder). Let \(I\) be a validation sample of size \(m\), independent of a training estimate \(\hat\eta\). If \[ \|\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}\|_{P,2}=o_p(1), \] then \[ \frac1{\sqrt m}\sum_{i\in I} \left[ \{\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}\}(W_i) -P(\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0}) \right] =o_p(1). \]

Proof. Conditional on the training sample, the summands are i.i.d. and centered. Their conditional variance is at most \[ P(\varphi_{\hat\eta}-\varphi_{\eta_0})^2=o_p(1). \] Conditional Chebyshev inequality proves convergence to zero; integrating over the training data gives the unconditional conclusion. \(\square\)

Cross-fitting replaces a global Donsker requirement by independence plus \(L_2\)-consistency. It does not eliminate moment conditions, the orthogonal bias remainder, or the need for fold sizes proportional to \(n\).

A doubly robust example

Example (average treatment effect). Observe \(W=(Y,A,X)\), \(A\in\{0,1\}\). Let \[ \mu_a(x)=E(Y\mid A=a,X=x), \qquad e(x)=P(A=1\mid X=x), \] and \[ \theta_0=P\{\mu_1(X)-\mu_0(X)\}. \] An influence function is \[ \begin{aligned} \varphi(W;\eta,\theta) ={}&\mu_1(X)-\mu_0(X)-\theta\\ &+\frac{A}{e(X)}\{Y-\mu_1(X)\} -\frac{1-A}{1-e(X)}\{Y-\mu_0(X)\}. \end{aligned} \] Under positivity, direct calculation gives a population remainder bounded by \[ |P\varphi(W;\hat\eta,\theta_0)| \lesssim \|\hat e-e\|_{P,2} \left( \|\hat\mu_1-\mu_1\|_{P,2} +\|\hat\mu_0-\mu_0\|_{P,2} \right). \]

Thus root-\(n\) inference is possible if the product of propensity and outcome-regression errors is \(o_p(n^{-1/2})\). Both nuisances may converge more slowly than \(n^{-1/2}\); rates near \(n^{-1/4}\) each are sufficient. If either nuisance is consistently estimated while the other is correctly specified in a suitable sense, the population moment can still be unbiased, which is the source of double robustness.

A general orthogonal-score theorem

Theorem. Let \(\hat\theta\) solve a cross-fitted empirical equation based on \(\psi(W;\theta,\hat\eta)\). Suppose:

  1. \(\hat\theta\to_p\theta_0\);
  2. \(A=\partial_\theta P\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)\) is nonsingular;
  3. \(P\|\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)\|^2<\infty\);
  4. the cross-fitted \(L_2\) difference of scores is \(o_p(1)\);
  5. \[ \sqrt n\, P\{\psi(W;\theta_0,\hat\eta)-\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)\} =o_p(1); \]
  6. the estimating-equation and \(\theta\)-linearization remainders are \(o_p(n^{-1/2})\).

Then \[ \sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta_0) = -A^{-1}\mathbb G_n\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0)+o_p(1), \] and its covariance is \[ A^{-1}P(\psi_0\psi_0^\top)A^{-\top}. \]

The theorem separates four jobs that should never be compressed into the phrase “by orthogonality”: identify the score derivative in \(\theta\), control nuisance bias, control the random empirical index, and verify the central limit theorem for the true score.

Efficiency and the larger theory

When \(\psi_0\) is the efficient influence function, its variance is the semiparametric efficiency bound. Tangent spaces, projections, pathwise differentiability, and convolution lower bounds are developed in Statistical inference, §27 and the corresponding chapters of Statistical decision theory. The empirical-process contribution is now explicit: it is the uniform or cross-fitted control that turns an influence-function identity into a valid sample expansion.
