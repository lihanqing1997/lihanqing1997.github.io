A fitted Cox model makes several distinct claims: proportional relative hazards, a specified linear predictor, correctly formed risk sets, noninformative censoring, and adequate absolute-risk calibration. No single residual can test all of them. Diagnostics should be tied to the martingale or conditional-moment identity implied by the particular claim.

Martingale and Cox-Snell residuals

Definition. For subject \(i\), the fitted cumulative hazard and martingale residual are \[ \widehat H_i(t) =\int_0^tY_i(u)e^{\widehat\beta^\top Z_i(u)} \,\mathrm{d}\widehat\Lambda_0(u), \qquad \widehat M_i(t)=N_i(t)-\widehat H_i(t). \] At the end of follow-up, \[ \widehat M_i=\Delta_i-\widehat H_i(X_i). \] The Cox-Snell residual is \[ \widehat R_i=\widehat H_i(X_i). \]

Proposition (exponential transform). If \(T\) has continuous conditional cumulative hazard \(H(t\mid Z)\), then \[ R=H(T\mid Z)\mid Z\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1). \]

Proof. For \(r\ge0\), let \(H^{-1}(r\mid Z)=\inf\{t:H(t\mid Z)\ge r\}\). Then \[ P\{H(T\mid Z)>r\mid Z\} =P\{T>H^{-1}(r\mid Z)\mid Z\} =e^{-r}. \] Thus the transformed event time is standard exponential.

With censoring, \(R_i\) is itself right-censored, so a Nelson-Aalen plot of cumulative hazard versus \(r\) should approximate the line \(r\mapsto r\) under global fit. This omnibus plot does not localize whether a deviation comes from covariate form, proportionality, or baseline prediction.

Deviance residuals and functional form

Definition. The martingale residual lies in \((-\infty,1]\) and is strongly skewed. The deviance residual is \[ D_i=\operatorname{sign}(\widehat M_i) \left[ -2\{\widehat M_i+ \Delta_i\log(\Delta_i-\widehat M_i)\} \right]^{1/2}, \] with \(0\log0=0\).

Proposition. The quantity inside the square root is twice the difference between the saturated one-observation counting-process log-likelihood and the fitted log-likelihood contribution.

Proof. For observed count \(\Delta_i\in\{0,1\}\) and fitted cumulative mean \(\widehat H_i\), the Poisson-type log-likelihood is \(\Delta_i\log\widehat H_i-\widehat H_i\). The saturated mean is \(\Delta_i\), giving difference \[ \Delta_i\log\frac{\Delta_i}{\widehat H_i} -(\Delta_i-\widehat H_i) =-\{\widehat M_i+\Delta_i\log(\Delta_i-\widehat M_i)\}, \] because \(\widehat H_i=\Delta_i-\widehat M_i\). Multiply by two and attach the residual sign.

Plotting \(\widehat M_i\) or \(D_i\) against an omitted continuous covariate \(X_i\) can reveal nonlinear mean structure. A smoother near zero supports the fitted form; curvature suggests adding a spline or transformation. The points are neither independent nor homoscedastic, so the plot is exploratory unless embedded in a formal score test.

Schoenfeld residuals

Definition. If subject \(i_k\) fails at \(t_k\), the Schoenfeld residual is \[ r_k=Z_{i_k}(t_k)-\bar Z_n(\widehat\beta,t_k). \] The score is their sum: \[ U_n(\widehat\beta)=\sum_{k=1}^{D}r_k=0 \] for an interior maximum. A scaled Schoenfeld residual is commonly \[ r_k^\star=I_n(\widehat\beta)^{-1}r_k \] or a time-local covariance-scaled version.

Proposition (time-varying local alternative). Suppose \[ \beta(t)=\beta_0+\theta g(t) \] for a scalar function \(g\). The score for \(\theta\) at \(\theta=0\) and \(\beta=\beta_0\) is \[ U_\theta =\sum_k g(t_k)^\top \{Z_{i_k}(t_k)-\bar Z_n(\beta_0,t_k)\}. \]

Proof. The expanded linear predictor is \(\beta_0^\top Z+\theta^\top\{g(t)Z\}\). Applying the ordinary Cox score formula to the added time-dependent covariate \(g(t)Z\) gives the result.

Regressing scaled Schoenfeld residuals on a chosen function of time is therefore a score test for a specified nonproportional alternative. A visible slope suggests how the coefficient changes; failure to reject does not prove proportionality against every possible temporal pattern.

Cumulative score processes

Definition. For a predictable diagnostic covariate \(h_i(t)\), define \[ \mathbb U_n(t) =\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i\int_0^t \{h_i(u)-\bar h_n(u)\}\,\mathrm{d}\widehat M_i(u), \] where \(\bar h_n\) is the fitted risk-set weighted mean. Under the fitted model, \(\mathbb U_n\) is approximately a mean-zero Gaussian process after accounting for parameter estimation.

Theorem (multiplier calibration). Let \(\widehat\phi_i(t)\) be estimated subject-level influence processes for \(\mathbb U_n(t)\), including the effect of estimating \(\beta\) and \(\Lambda_0\). If the relevant class is Donsker or satisfies an appropriate martingale tightness condition, then conditionally on the data, \[ \mathbb U_n^\xi(t) =\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i\xi_i\widehat\phi_i(t) \] converges to the same Gaussian limit as \(\mathbb U_n\), for independent multipliers with mean zero and variance one.

Proof outline. Asymptotic linearization reduces the process to a sum of independent mean-zero influence functions. Conditional multiplier central limit theory gives the same covariance kernel because \[ E_\xi\{\mathbb U_n^\xi(s)\mathbb U_n^\xi(t)\} =\frac1n\sum_i\widehat\phi_i(s)\widehat\phi_i(t). \] Stochastic equicontinuity transfers convergence from finite-dimensional distributions to the supremum norm. Parameter-estimation corrections are necessary to reproduce the projected covariance.

Supremum statistics such as \(\sup_t\|\mathbb U_n(t)\|\) detect broad departures, while directional integrals are more powerful against prespecified alternatives.

Influence and leverage

Definition. A one-step approximation to the change in \(\widehat\beta\) after deleting subject \(i\) is \[ \operatorname{DFBETA}_i \approx I_n(\widehat\beta)^{-1}U_i(\widehat\beta), \] where \[ U_i(\widehat\beta) =\int\{Z_i(t)-\bar Z_n(\widehat\beta,t)\} \,\mathrm{d}\widehat M_i(t) \] is a subject-level score residual.

Derivation. Let \(U_{-i}\) be the score without subject \(i\). Expanding \(0=U_{-i}(\widehat\beta_{-i})\) at \(\widehat\beta\) gives \[ 0\approx-U_i(\widehat\beta) -I_n(\widehat\beta)(\widehat\beta_{-i}-\widehat\beta), \] which yields the approximation up to the sign convention used for the displayed deletion contrast.

Large influence may arise from an unusual covariate, a rare risk-set comparison, an early event, or data error. It should trigger subject-level inspection and sensitivity analysis, not automatic deletion.

Calibration and discrimination

Definition. At horizon \(t\), the inverse-censoring-weighted Brier score for prediction \(\widehat S(t\mid Z_i)\) is \[ \widehat{\operatorname{BS}}(t) =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left[ \frac{1\{X_i\le t,\Delta_i=1\}} {\widehat G(X_i-\mid Z_i)} \widehat S(t\mid Z_i)^2 + \frac{1\{X_i>t\}} {\widehat G(t\mid Z_i)} \{1-\widehat S(t\mid Z_i)\}^2 \right], \] where \(G(t\mid Z)=P(C\ge t\mid Z)\).

Proposition. If censoring is conditionally independent given \(Z\) and \(G\) is correctly estimated, the weighted Brier score is unbiased, up to estimation error, for \[ E\left[ \{1(T>t)-\widehat S(t\mid Z)\}^2 \right]. \]

Proof. For the event term, \[ E\left[ \frac{1\{T\le t,T\le C\}}{G(T-\mid Z)} h(T,Z) \middle|T,Z \right] =1\{T\le t\}h(T,Z). \] For the survival term, \[ E\left[ \frac{1\{T>t,C>t\}}{G(t\mid Z)} \middle|T,Z \right]=1\{T>t\}. \] Choose the corresponding squared-error functions and add.

Concordance evaluates ordering among comparable pairs; it is insensitive to a common distortion of absolute risk. Calibration plots compare observed and predicted risk within prediction strata, while calibration-in-the-large and slope quantify systematic level and spread errors. Validation data should be independent, or optimism must be corrected by resampling the entire modeling pipeline.

Competing explanations for a bad diagnostic

A residual pattern can be caused by:

  • nonlinearity in a covariate effect;
  • nonproportional hazards;
  • omitted interactions or time-dependent confounding;
  • incorrect entry, censoring, or event coding;
  • cluster dependence or frailty;
  • an inappropriate time scale;
  • a wrong event type in the presence of competing risks.

Diagnosis is therefore iterative: derive the moment restriction, inspect a process targeted to it, fit a scientifically plausible alternative, and reassess prediction and interpretation. Passing a battery of plots does not establish the censoring or causal assumptions, which are not fully testable from observed data.
