Sufficiency asks whether a statistic preserves the entire experiment; ancillarity asks whether a statistic has a parameter-free distribution. Completeness is a uniqueness property of expectations. Together these notions explain why certain exact procedures reduce the data without losing information and why some reductions admit unique unbiased estimators.

Sufficiency and factorization

Definition (sufficiency). A statistic \(T:\mathcal X\to\mathcal T\) is sufficient for \(\{P_\theta\}\) if there is a Markov kernel \(K(t,\mathrm{d}x)\), independent of \(\theta\), that is a version of the conditional law \[ P_\theta(X\in\mathrm{d}x\mid T)=K(T,\mathrm{d}x) \] for every \(\theta\). Equivalently, the original experiment can be reconstructed from the reduced experiment \(\{P_\theta\circ T^{-1}\}\) by a parameter-free randomization.

Neyman-Fisher factorization theorem. Suppose the model is dominated by a \(\sigma\)-finite measure \(\mu\) and regular conditional distributions exist. Then \(T\) is sufficient if and only if versions of the densities can be written \[ p_\theta(x)=g_\theta(T(x))h(x), \] where \(h\ge0\) is independent of \(\theta\).

Proof. If the factorization holds, define the finite measure \(\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=h(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\). Disintegrate \(\nu\) along \(T\): \[ \nu(\mathrm{d}x)=K(T(x),\mathrm{d}x)\,\nu_T(\mathrm{d}T(x)). \] Multiplication by \(g_\theta(T(x))\) changes only the marginal law of \(T\), not the conditional kernel \(K\); hence \(T\) is sufficient. Conversely, sufficiency gives \[ P_\theta(\mathrm{d}x)=K(T(x),\mathrm{d}x)\,P_{\theta,T}(\mathrm{d}T(x)). \] Choose a dominating mixture \(Q\) of the model and let \(Q_T\) be its \(T\)-marginal. The Radon-Nikodym derivative \(\mathrm{d}P_{\theta,T}/\mathrm{d}Q_T\) depends on \(x\) only through \(T(x)\), while \(\mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}\mu\) is parameter-free. Their product supplies \(g_\theta(T(x))h(x)\).

Example (normal reduction). If \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are i.i.d. \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \[ \prod_{i=1}^n p_{\mu,\sigma}(x_i) =(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[-\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2+n(\bar x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]. \] Therefore \((\bar X,\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2)\) is sufficient for \((\mu,\sigma^2)\). If \(\sigma^2\) is known, \(\bar X\) alone is sufficient for \(\mu\).

Minimal sufficiency

Definition. A sufficient statistic \(T\) is minimal sufficient if, for every sufficient statistic \(S\), there is a measurable \(f\) such that \(T=f(S)\) almost surely under every \(P_\theta\).

Likelihood-ratio criterion. In a dominated model, suppose a statistic \(T\) satisfies \[ T(x)=T(y) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(y)} \text{ is independent of }\theta \] whenever the ratio is defined. Then \(T\) is minimal sufficient.

Proof. Fix \(\theta_0\) with a positive reference density on the common support. The criterion implies \[ \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_{\theta_0}(x)} =g_\theta(T(x)), \] so factorization proves sufficiency. If \(S\) is sufficient, its factorization makes \(p_\theta(x)/p_{\theta_0}(x)\) a function of \(S(x)\) for every \(\theta\). Hence \(S(x)=S(y)\) implies the ratio criterion and therefore \(T(x)=T(y)\). Thus \(T\) is a function of \(S\).

Rao-Blackwellization and completeness

Rao-Blackwell theorem. Let \(T\) be sufficient and let \(U\) estimate \(g(\theta)\). For every convex loss \(L(\theta,a)\), \[ U^\star=\mathbb E_\theta[U\mid T] \] can be chosen independently of \(\theta\), and \[ \mathbb E_\theta L(\theta,U^\star) \le \mathbb E_\theta L(\theta,U). \] Under squared error, \(\operatorname{Var}_\theta(U^\star)\le\operatorname{Var}_\theta(U)\), with equality exactly when \(U\) is \(T\)-measurable almost surely.

Proof. Sufficiency makes the conditional law of \(X\), hence the conditional expectation of \(U\), parameter-free once \(T\) is fixed. Conditional Jensen gives \[ L(\theta,\mathbb E[U\mid T]) \le\mathbb E[L(\theta,U)\mid T]. \] Taking expectations proves the risk inequality. For squared loss, the conditional variance decomposition \[ \operatorname{Var}(U) =\operatorname{Var}\{\mathbb E(U\mid T)\} +\mathbb E\{\operatorname{Var}(U\mid T)\} \] gives the sharper statement.

Definition (completeness). A statistic \(T\) is complete for \(\{P_\theta\}\) if \[ \mathbb E_\theta a(T)=0\ \text{for every }\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(T)=0\quad P_\theta\text{-a.s. for every }\theta \] for every integrable measurable \(a\).

Lehmann-Scheffe theorem. If \(T\) is complete and sufficient and \(U=u(T)\) is unbiased for \(g(\theta)\), then \(U\) is the unique unbiased estimator of \(g(\theta)\) that is a function of \(T\), and it has minimum variance among all unbiased estimators.

Proof. Rao-Blackwellization maps any unbiased estimator \(V\) to \(V^\star=\mathbb E(V\mid T)\), which remains unbiased and cannot have larger variance. Both \(V^\star\) and \(U\) are functions of \(T\), and \[ \mathbb E_\theta(V^\star-U)=0\quad\text{for all }\theta. \] Completeness forces \(V^\star=U\) almost surely. Thus \(\operatorname{Var}_\theta(U)\le\operatorname{Var}_\theta(V)\), and uniqueness follows in the same way.

Exponential families

Definition. A \(k\)-parameter natural exponential family has densities \[ p_\eta(x)=\exp\{\eta^\top T(x)-A(\eta)\}h(x), \qquad A(\eta)=\log\int e^{\eta^\top T(x)}h(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x), \] for \(\eta\) in the natural parameter space \[ \mathcal N=\left\{\eta:\int e^{\eta^\top T}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu\lt\infty\right\}. \]

Cumulant identities. At every interior point where differentiation is justified, \[ \nabla A(\eta)=\mathbb E_\eta T,\qquad \nabla^2A(\eta)=\operatorname{Cov}_\eta(T). \] Hence \(A\) is convex, and it is strictly convex whenever no nonzero linear combination of \(T\) is almost surely constant.

Proof. Let \(Z(\eta)=e^{A(\eta)}\). Differentiating \(Z\) gives \[ \partial_jA=\frac{\partial_jZ}{Z} =\frac{\int T_j e^{\eta^\top T}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu} {\int e^{\eta^\top T}h\,\mathrm{d}\mu} =\mathbb E_\eta T_j. \] Differentiating once more yields \(\partial_{jk}^2A=\mathbb E_\eta(T_jT_k)-\mathbb E_\eta T_j\,\mathbb E_\eta T_k\).

Completeness of a full exponential family. If the natural parameter space contains a nonempty open set in \(\mathbb R^k\), then the natural statistic \(T\) is complete, subject to the common-support representation above.

Proof. Suppose \(\mathbb E_\eta a(T)=0\) throughout an open set \(U\). Fix \(\eta_0\in U\). Then \[ 0=e^{A(\eta)}\mathbb E_\eta a(T) =\int a(T)e^{(\eta-\eta_0)^\top T}\,\nu_{\eta_0}(\mathrm{d}x), \] where \(\nu_{\eta_0}(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{\eta_0^\top T(x)}h(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\). This is the moment-generating transform of the finite signed measure induced by \(a(T)\nu_{\eta_0}\). It vanishes in a neighborhood of the origin, so uniqueness of Laplace transforms makes that signed measure zero. Therefore \(a(T)=0\) almost surely.

A curved exponential family restricts \(\eta\) to a lower-dimensional surface. The open-set argument then fails, and the natural statistic need not be complete even though it remains sufficient.

Ancillarity and Basu independence

Definition. A statistic \(A(X)\) is ancillary if its distribution is the same under every \(P_\theta\). It carries no marginal information about \(\theta\), although conditioning on it can change the precision or relevance of another statistic.

Basu theorem. Every complete sufficient statistic \(T\) is independent of every ancillary statistic \(A\).

Proof. For a measurable set \(B\) in the range of \(A\), sufficiency gives a parameter-free version \[ q_B(T)=P_\theta(A\in B\mid T). \] Since \(A\) is ancillary, \(P_\theta(A\in B)=c_B\) is constant in \(\theta\). Therefore \[ \mathbb E_\theta\{q_B(T)-c_B\}=0 \] for every \(\theta\). Completeness implies \(q_B(T)=c_B\) almost surely, which is exactly \(P_\theta(A\in B\mid T)=P_\theta(A\in B)\). A monotone-class argument extends from generating sets \(B\) to the full sigma-field.

Example. For a normal sample with unknown \(\mu\) and known \(\sigma^2\), \(\bar X\) is complete sufficient for \(\mu\), while the centered residual direction \[ \frac{(X_1-\bar X,\ldots,X_n-\bar X)} {\{\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2\}^{1/2}} \] is ancillary. Basu's theorem yields their independence without a direct Gaussian covariance calculation.

Likelihood and conditionality

Likelihood principle. If observations \(x_1,x_2\) from possibly different experiments produce proportional likelihoods, \[ L_1(\theta;x_1)=c(x_1,x_2)L_2(\theta;x_2) \quad\text{for every }\theta, \] then they should lead to the same evidential conclusion about \(\theta\).

Bayesian updating with a fixed prior obeys this principle because the proportionality constant cancels in posterior normalization. Frequentist procedures are calibrated over repetitions of an experiment, so stopping rules and sampling plans can change their error probabilities even when the observed likelihoods are proportional. Sufficiency, conditionality, and frequentist calibration are therefore related but not interchangeable ideas.
