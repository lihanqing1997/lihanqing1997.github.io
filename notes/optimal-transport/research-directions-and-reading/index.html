The earlier chapters establish a language for reading research: couplings, potentials, stability, and dynamics. This page offers a selective route into current work, rather than a claim to survey every frontier. The research links were checked on 28 October 2026. A recent paper is an entry point, not evidence that a whole research problem has been solved.

Statistical maps and uncertainty

Estimating an optimal cost, estimating a potential, and estimating a map are different tasks. A map differentiates a convex potential, so a small objective error does not automatically give a small map error. Smoothness, source geometry, and the norm of interest determine what is statistically possible. After the empirical and inference chapters, ask: which estimator is used, what is its target, is the distribution fixed or allowed to vary over a class, and is the statement a risk bound or a limit law?

Balakrishnan, Manole, and Wasserman, Statistical inference for optimal transport maps: recent advances and perspectives (2025 preprint), reviews map estimation and limit theory. A useful research question is how to obtain reliable uncertainty statements when smoothing, dimensionality, and map regularity interact. Do not infer a universal map CLT from a cost CLT.

Entropic methods and joint limits

At fixed positive regularization, smooth optimization is available in settings where unregularized transport is nonsmooth. But a sequence of regularization parameters approaching zero changes both the bias and the conditioning. The practical problem has at least three scales: sample size, regularization, and numerical tolerance. The decomposition in the computational chapters is a way to read a theorem: identify which scales are fixed, which vanish, and how constants depend on them.

Begin with Peyré–Cuturi, Computational optimal transport, and Feydy et al., Interpolating between optimal transport and MMD using Sinkhorn divergences. These are foundations for the frontier, not recent frontier claims. Questions about joint statistical and computational accuracy should be formulated for a specified cost and sampling model.

Flow matching, bridges, and generative models

A coupling can generate a curve by interpolating paired samples; a learned velocity field can then approximate its continuity equation. The choice of coupling affects trajectories. An arbitrary coupling, a batchwise optimal matching, an optimal population coupling, and a Schrödinger bridge solve different problems. Straight training paths do not by themselves certify that the learned population flow minimizes kinetic action.

Wald–Steidl, Flow matching: Markov kernels, stochastic processes and transport plans (revised April 2026), connects these constructions to Wasserstein curves. Read it after the continuity-equation and bridge chapters. Useful questions concern the effects of coupling approximation, regression error, and numerical ODE integration on the resulting distribution.

Structured data and Gromov–Wasserstein computation

Comparing intrinsic geometry adds two difficulties to sampling error: symmetries can prevent a unique point correspondence, and the optimization is generally nonconvex. A theorem about the exact GW value does not automatically justify an algorithm's output. Conversely, a stationary-point guarantee is not a global-optimality guarantee.

Rioux–Goldfeld–Kato, Entropic Gromov–Wasserstein distances: stability and algorithms (2024), develops guarantees in a specified quadratic entropic setting, including global conclusions under quantitative regularization conditions. The September 2026 preprint by Rioux, Marks, Passeggeri, and Goldfeld studies discrete GW duality, algorithms, and isomorphism testing. Its finite-space scope and preprint status matter; neither reference establishes a universal global GW solver.

Geometry, curvature, and nonlinear evolution

Entropy convexity along Wasserstein geodesics provides a way to express lower curvature bounds without classical coordinates. This leads from the displacement-convexity and gradient-flow chapters to analysis on metric-measure spaces. A useful distinction is between defining curvature through entropy convexity and proving stronger analytic properties of the space; the latter requires further structure.

Lott–Villani, Ricci curvature for metric-measure spaces via optimal transport, is a foundational source. The present note prepares the transport vocabulary; it does not develop synthetic curvature theory. For unbalanced evolution, Liero–Mielke–Savaré provides the entropy–transport and HK framework. Questions about singular limits and transport–reaction PDEs belong to this analytic branch.

A reading ladder

  1. First complete account. Santambrogio, Optimal transport for applied mathematicians: use Chapters 1–2 for plans, maps, and one-dimensional transport; Chapter 5 for Wasserstein geometry; Chapter 8 for gradient flows.
  2. Proofs behind the geometry. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport: §1 for optimality and maps, §2 for Wasserstein geometry, and §3 for flows.
  3. Metric evolution. Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré, Gradient flows: Chapters 2–4 for minimizing movements, Chapters 8 and 11 for continuity equations and Wasserstein evolution.
  4. Computation and specialized branches. Use the sources linked above and on individual chapters. Follow one branch deeply before trying to read every extension.

A final self-study project

Take a one-dimensional pair with known quantiles. Compute its exact quadratic cost and map, approximate the laws on a grid, solve both the finite LP and entropic problem, and draw the displacement interpolation. Vary grid size and regularization separately. Explain which observed discrepancy comes from discretization, sampling (if samples are used), regularization, or an unfinished numerical solve. Finally, read one research paper and identify its estimand, assumptions, main theorem, and one limitation. Being able to do this is a more useful completion test than recognizing every topic name.
