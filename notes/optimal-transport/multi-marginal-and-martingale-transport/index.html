Learning goal. Recognize compatible multi-way couplings and test martingale feasibility using convex order.

Two important extensions alter the set of admissible couplings in different directions. Multi-marginal transport couples more than two laws simultaneously. Martingale transport keeps two marginals but imposes a conditional-mean constraint. Their compactness arguments resemble classical transport, whereas their geometry and dual variables are genuinely different.

The multi-marginal Kantorovich problem

Definition 27.1 (The multi-marginal Kantorovich problem). Let \(X_i\) be Polish spaces, let \(\mu_i\in\mathcal P(X_i)\), and let \(c:X_1\times\cdots\times X_N\to(-\infty,\infty]\). Write \[ \Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N) = \{\gamma\in\mathcal P(X_1\times\cdots\times X_N): (p_i)_\#\gamma=\mu_i,\ 1\le i\le N\}. \] The multi-marginal transport value is \[ \mathsf{MMOT}_c(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N) = \inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N)} \int c(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma. \]

A plan \(\gamma\) describes a joint law with all prescribed one-coordinate laws. Pairwise optimal couplings generally cannot be assembled independently: their shared coordinates must be compatible with one common joint distribution.

Theorem 27.2 (Existence). Suppose \(c\) is lower semicontinuous and \[ c(x_1,\ldots,x_N)\ge \sum_{i=1}^N a_i(x_i) \] for continuous functions \(a_i\in L^1(\mu_i)\). If the value is finite, an optimal multi-marginal plan exists. In particular, the conclusion holds whenever \(c\) is nonnegative and lower semicontinuous.

Proof. For \(\varepsilon>0\), choose compact \(K_i\subset X_i\) with \(\mu_i(K_i^c)\le\varepsilon/N\). Every admissible \(\gamma\) satisfies \[ \gamma\left(\left(\prod_iK_i\right)^c\right) \le\sum_i\gamma(p_i^{-1}(K_i^c))=\sum_i\mu_i(K_i^c)\le\varepsilon. \] Thus the coupling family is tight. If \(\gamma_n\Rightarrow\gamma\), then for each \(f\in C_b(X_i)\), \[ \int f\,\mathrm{d}(p_i)_\#\gamma =\int f\circ p_i\,\mathrm{d}\gamma =\lim_n\int f\circ p_i\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_n=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_i. \] So it is also weakly closed, hence compact by Prokhorov.

Let \(a(x)=\sum_i a_i(x_i)\), \(A=\sum_i\int a_i\,\mathrm{d}\mu_i\), and \(g=c-a\). Continuity of \(a_i\) makes \(g\) lower semicontinuous, and the hypothesis gives \(g\ge0\). Also, for every feasible \(\gamma\), \(\int a\,\mathrm{d}\gamma=A\) is finite. A minimizing sequence has a feasible limit \(\gamma_*\), and Portmanteau gives \[ \inf\int c\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_* =A+\int g\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_* \le A+\liminf_n\int g\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_n =\liminf_n\int c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_n=\inf\int c. \] Thus the limit attains the finite optimal value.

Duality and splitting sets

Theorem 27.3 (Multi-marginal Kantorovich duality). On compact \(X_i\), for continuous \(c\), \[ \inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N)}\int c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma = \sup_{\sum_i\varphi_i(x_i)\le c(x_1,\ldots,x_N)} \sum_{i=1}^N\int\varphi_i\,\mathrm{d}\mu_i. \] The dual contains one potential for each marginal.

Proof of duality and attainment. Put \(Z=\prod_iX_i\), and let \(p_i\) be the coordinate projections. Define \[ L(\gamma,\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_N)=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma+\sum_i\int\varphi_i\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_i-(p_i)_\#\gamma). \] If some marginal is wrong, a continuous test \(f\) has \(a=\int f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_i-(p_i)_\#\gamma)\ne0\). Choosing \(\varphi_i=t\operatorname{sgn}(a)f\), other potentials zero, sends \(L\) to infinity. Therefore \[ \sup_{\varphi_i\in C(X_i)}L =\begin{cases}\int c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma,&\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N),\\+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \] The set \(\mathcal P(Z)\) is compact convex and \(L\) is continuous affine in both arguments. Sion's minimax theorem gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathsf{MMOT}_c &=\inf_\gamma\sup_{\varphi_i}L=\sup_{\varphi_i}\inf_\gamma L\\ &=\sup_{\varphi_i}\left[\sum_i\mu_i\varphi_i+\min_Z\left(c-\sum_i\varphi_i\right)\right]. \end{aligned} \] The infimum of a continuous function over probability laws is its pointwise minimum, attained by a Dirac law. If that minimum is \(m\), replace \(\varphi_1\) by \(\varphi_1+m\); then \(\sum_i\varphi_i+m\le c\), and the bracket is exactly the new feasible objective. This gives primal value at most dual supremum. Conversely, for feasible potentials and any coupling, \[ \sum_i\int\varphi_i\,\mathrm{d}\mu_i =\int\sum_i\varphi_i(x_i)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\gamma, \] proving the reverse inequality.

For attainment, take a maximizing sequence and, for each member, successively replace coordinates \(i=1,\ldots,N\) by \[ \widetilde\varphi_i(x_i)=\inf_{x_{-i}}\left(c(x_1,\ldots,x_N)-\sum_{j\ne i}\varphi_j(x_j)\right). \] Feasibility gives \(\widetilde\varphi_i\ge\varphi_i\), and the new tuple remains feasible; thus values cannot decrease. Each replaced coordinate satisfies \[ |\widetilde\varphi_i(x_i)-\widetilde\varphi_i(x_i')| \le\sup_{x_{-i}}|c(x_i,x_{-i})-c(x_i',x_{-i})|. \] Later replacements do not change this function. Normalize the first \(N-1\) coordinates to vanish at base points, adding the opposite total shift to the last. With \(M=\|c\|_\infty\), \[ \|\varphi_i\|_\infty\le2M\quad(i<N), \qquad \varphi_N(x_N)=\inf_{x_{-N}}\left(c-\sum_{i<N}\varphi_i\right), \qquad \|\varphi_N\|_\infty\le(2N-1)M. \] The last equality remains valid because the compensating shifts cancel inside its transform. Uniform continuity of \(c\) makes all coordinate families equicontinuous. Arzelà–Ascoli and a finite diagonal extraction give uniform limits \(\varphi_i^{(n_k)}\to\varphi_i\). Then \[ \sum_i\varphi_i\le c,\qquad \left|\sum_i\mu_i\varphi_i^{(n_k)}-\sum_i\mu_i\varphi_i\right| \le\sum_i\|\varphi_i^{(n_k)}-\varphi_i\|_\infty\to0. \] The limiting feasible tuple attains the dual value.

Definition 27.4 (Duality and splitting sets). A set \(\Gamma\subset X_1\times\cdots\times X_N\) is a \(c\)-splitting set if there are functions \(\varphi_i\) such that \[ \sum_i\varphi_i(x_i)\le c(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \quad\text{everywhere},\qquad \sum_i\varphi_i(x_i)=c(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \quad\text{on }\Gamma. \]

Proposition 27.5 (Generalized cyclical monotonicity). If \(\Gamma\) is \(c\)-splitting, \(x^1,\ldots,x^m\in\Gamma\), and \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_N\) are permutations of \(\{1,\ldots,m\}\), then \[ \sum_{k=1}^m c(x_1^k,\ldots,x_N^k) \le \sum_{k=1}^m c(x_1^{\sigma_1(k)},\ldots,x_N^{\sigma_N(k)}). \]

Proof. On a splitting set, \(c(x^k)=\sum_i\varphi_i(x_i^k)\). For arbitrary coordinate permutations, \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^m c(x_1^k,\ldots,x_N^k) &=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^m\varphi_i(x_i^k)\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^m\varphi_i(x_i^{\sigma_i(k)})\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^m\sum_{i=1}^N\varphi_i(x_i^{\sigma_i(k)})\\ &\le\sum_{k=1}^m c(x_1^{\sigma_1(k)},\ldots,x_N^{\sigma_N(k)}). \end{aligned} \] The second equality uses that each \(\sigma_i\) reorders the same finite sum; the last uses feasibility at each recombined tuple. If primal and dual optimizers exist, their nonnegative slack satisfies \[ \int\left(c-\sum_i\varphi_i\right)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma =\text{primal value}-\text{dual value}=0. \] Thus the slack vanishes \(\gamma\)-almost everywhere, giving the asserted splitting set.

Quadratic interaction and Wasserstein barycenters

Let \(\lambda_i>0\), \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\), and put \[ \bar x=\sum_{i=1}^N\lambda_i x_i,\qquad c_\lambda(x_1,\ldots,x_N) =\sum_i\lambda_i|x_i-\bar x|^2 =\sum_{i<j}\lambda_i\lambda_j|x_i-x_j|^2. \] Thus the cost measures dispersion around the Euclidean barycenter of each transported tuple.

Theorem 27.6 (Barycenter equivalence). For \(\mu_i\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\), \[ \inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N)} \int c_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\gamma = \inf_{\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)} \sum_{i=1}^N\lambda_iW_2^2(\mu_i,\nu). \] If \(\gamma^\star\) is optimal, then \(\nu^\star=(\sum_i\lambda_i p_i)_\#\gamma^\star\) is a Wasserstein barycenter.

Proof. Let \(B(x)=\sum_i\lambda_ix_i\). Expansion and \(\sum_i\lambda_i(x_i-B)=0\) give \[ \sum_i\lambda_i|x_i-y|^2=c_\lambda(x)+|y-B(x)|^2\ge c_\lambda(x). \] Given a candidate \(\nu\), disintegrate its optimal couplings with \(\mu_i\) as \(\nu(\mathrm{d}y)K_i(y,\mathrm{d}x_i)\) and form \(P=\nu(\mathrm{d}y)\prod_iK_i(y,\mathrm{d}x_i)\). Its input marginal \(\gamma\) belongs to \(\Pi(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_N)\), so \[ \sum_i\lambda_iW_2^2(\mu_i,\nu) =\int\sum_i\lambda_i|x_i-y|^2\,\mathrm{d}P \ge\int c_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\ge\inf_\gamma\int c_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\gamma. \] Taking the infimum in \(\nu\) gives one direction. Conversely, for any input coupling \(\gamma\), put \(\nu=B_\#\gamma\). Jensen gives its finite second moment, and \((x_i,B)_\#\gamma\) is a coupling with \(\mu_i\). Hence \[ \inf_\nu\sum_i\lambda_iW_2^2(\mu_i,\nu) \le\sum_i\lambda_iW_2^2(\mu_i,B_\#\gamma) \le\int\sum_i\lambda_i|x_i-B|^2\,\mathrm{d}\gamma=\int c_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}\gamma. \] Taking the infimum in \(\gamma\) gives equality. For optimal \(\gamma_*\), both last inequalities are equalities, so \(B_\#\gamma_*\) is a barycenter.

When is a multi-marginal optimizer a graph?

In two-marginal quadratic transport, absolute continuity of one marginal produces a unique Monge map. For \(N\ge3\), pairwise twist is not enough. A useful replacement is twist on splitting sets: for fixed \(x_1\), the map \[ (x_2,\ldots,x_N)\longmapsto D_{x_1}c(x_1,\ldots,x_N) \] must be injective when restricted to every \(c\)-splitting set.

Theorem 27.7 (Graph theorem). Assume the primal and dual optima are finite and attained, \(c\) is differentiable in \(x_1\), twist on splitting sets holds, and the first dual potential is differentiable \(\mu_1\)-almost everywhere. For instance, local bounds on \(D_{x_1}c\) uniform in the remaining coordinates make transformed first potentials locally Lipschitz, so an absolutely continuous \(\mu_1\) gives this differentiability by Rademacher. If \(\mu_1\) is absolutely continuous, then every optimizer is concentrated on a graph \[ (x_1,T_2(x_1),\ldots,T_N(x_1)). \] Consequently the optimizer is unique.

Proof. For a contact tuple \((x_1,\ldots,x_N)\), fix \(x_2,\ldots,x_N\) and define \[ F(z)=c(z,x_2,\ldots,x_N)-\varphi_1(z)-\sum_{i=2}^N\varphi_i(x_i). \] Then \(F\ge0\) and \(F(x_1)=0\). At a differentiability point, the difference quotients along each vector \(a\) are nonnegative for \(h>0\) and nonpositive for \(h<0\); their common limit is \(DF(x_1)\cdot a=0\). Thus \[ \nabla\varphi_1(x_1)=D_{x_1}c(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_N). \] For fixed \(x_1\), twist on splitting sets permits at most one tuple \((x_2,\ldots,x_N)\). Disintegrate \(\gamma=\mu_1(\mathrm{d}x_1)K_{x_1}(\mathrm{d}x_2\cdots\mathrm{d}x_N)\). Its contact-set concentration gives \[ K_{x_1}\{(x_2,\ldots,x_N):D_{x_1}c=\nabla\varphi_1(x_1)\text{ and contact holds}\}=1 \] almost everywhere. Twist makes the set a singleton; the measurable Dirac-kernel criterion gives \(K_{x_1}=\delta_{(T_2(x_1),\ldots,T_N(x_1))}\) for measurable \(T_i\). If \(\gamma\) and \(\widetilde\gamma\) were two optimizers, their average would also be optimal and hence would also have Dirac conditional laws. But its conditional law is \(\tfrac12\delta_{(T_2,\ldots,T_N)}+\tfrac12\delta_{(\widetilde T_2,\ldots,\widetilde T_N)}\); if it equals \(\delta_{S(x_1)}\), evaluation on the complement of \(\{S(x_1)\}\) forces both atoms to equal \(S(x_1)\). Thus the two tuples agree \(\mu_1\)-almost everywhere. Thus \(\gamma=\widetilde\gamma\).

Martingale couplings and convex order

Definition 27.8 (Martingale couplings and convex order). For \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_1(\mathbb R^d)\), a coupling \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) is a martingale coupling if \[ \int (y-x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)=0 \] for every bounded measurable scalar function \(f\). Equivalently, under \((X,Y)\sim\pi\), \(\mathbb E[Y\mid X]=X\). Denote the set by \(\Pi_M(\mu,\nu)\).

Definition 27.9 (Martingale couplings and convex order). We say that \(\mu\) is below \(\nu\) in convex order, written \(\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu\), if \[ \int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu\le\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\nu \] for every finite convex \(\phi\) of at most linear growth. Affine test functions show that the two measures must have the same mean.

Theorem 27.10 (Strassen's theorem (general existence theorem)). \[ \Pi_M(\mu,\nu)\ne\varnothing \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu. \]

Necessity and a complete finite-support converse. For a convex test \(\phi\), conditional Jensen gives \[ \int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\mathbb E\phi(X) =\mathbb E\phi(\mathbb E[Y\mid X]) \le\mathbb E\bigl[\mathbb E[\phi(Y)\mid X]\bigr] =\int\phi\,\mathrm{d}\nu. \] For linearly growing tests all quantities are integrable by the first moments; for general convex tests the affine lower bound and monotone truncation justify extended positive values. Thus every martingale coupling implies convex order.

For the converse with finite supports, write \(\mu=\sum_i a_i\delta_{x_i}\), \(\nu=\sum_j b_j\delta_{y_j}\), with positive weights. Let \(K=\operatorname{conv}\{y_j\}\). The convex linearly growing function \(x\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(x,K)\) has zero \(\nu\)-integral, so convex order forces each \(x_i\in K\). We seek \(P\ge0\) with row sums \(a\), column sums \(b\), and \(\sum_jP_{ij}(y_j-x_i)=0\). Farkas's finite-dimensional feasibility theorem says this system is solvable if and only if every tuple \((u_i,v_j,h_i)\) satisfying \[ u_i+v_j+h_i\cdot(y_j-x_i)\ge0\quad\text{for all }i,j \] has \(\sum_i a_i u_i+\sum_j b_jv_j\ge0\).

For such a tuple define the lower convex envelope on \(K\), \[ \chi(x)=\min\left\{\sum_jq_jv_j:q_j\ge0,\ \sum_jq_j=1,\ \sum_jq_jy_j=x\right\}. \] It is convex and piecewise affine: its epigraph is the convex hull of the finitely many lifted points \((y_j,v_j)\), plus the upward vertical ray. On \(K\), it is the maximum of the finitely many affine functions defining its nonvertical lower facets, working in the affine hull if necessary. This maximum extends to a finite convex function of at most linear growth on \(\mathbb R^d\). For any feasible \(q\) in the definition of \(\chi(x_i)\), multiply the Farkas inequalities by \(q_j\) and sum: \[ 0\le\sum_jq_j[u_i+v_j+h_i\cdot(y_j-x_i)] =u_i+\sum_jq_jv_j+h_i\cdot\left(\sum_jq_jy_j-x_i\right) =u_i+\sum_jq_jv_j. \] Minimizing the last expression over \(q\) yields \(u_i\ge-\chi(x_i)\). For \(x=y_j\), the feasible choice \(q=\delta_j\) gives \(\chi(y_j)\le v_j\). Thus convex order gives \[ \sum_i a_i u_i+\sum_j b_jv_j \ge-\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\chi\,\mathrm{d}\nu\ge0. \] Farkas now supplies the martingale matrix, completing the finite-support proof. The extension to arbitrary \(\mathcal P_1\) laws is the external Strassen theorem; it requires an approximation preserving convex order and first moments, and is not inferred from weak compactness alone.

Example 27.11 (Martingale couplings and convex order). Let \(\mu=\delta_0\) and \(\nu=\tfrac12\delta_{-a}+\tfrac12\delta_a\). Then \(\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu\), and the only coupling is \[ \pi=\tfrac12\delta_{(0,-a)}+\tfrac12\delta_{(0,a)}. \] It is a martingale because the conditional mean of \(Y\) is zero. The second law is a mean-preserving spread of the first.

Martingale optimal transport duality

For a lower semicontinuous cost \(c\), the primal problem is \[ \mathsf{MOT}_c(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi\in\Pi_M(\mu,\nu)}\int c(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] The martingale restriction introduces a vector-valued multiplier \(h(x)\).

Theorem 27.12 (Martingale duality). Suppose \(\mu,\nu\) have compact support, \(\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu\), and \(c\) is continuous on the product of their convex hulls. Then \[ \mathsf{MOT}_c(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\varphi,\psi,h} \left\{ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu: \varphi(x)+\psi(y)+h(x)\cdot(y-x)\le c(x,y) \right\}, \] where the supremum may be taken over continuous \(\varphi,\psi\) and continuous \(h\) with values in \(\mathbb R^d\).

Proof. Let \(A,B\) be the compact convex hulls of the supports and work on the compact convex set \(\mathcal P(A\times B)\). Define \[ L(\pi,\varphi,\psi,h)=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\pi_X)+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}(\nu-\pi_Y) -\int h(x)\cdot(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] If the marginals are correct but the martingale constraint fails, its vector signed measure \(M(E)=\int\mathbf1_E(x)(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi\) is nonzero. By uniqueness of finite signed measures, some continuous vector field \(h_0\) has \(a=\int h_0\cdot\mathrm{d}M\ne0\). With \(h=-t\operatorname{sgn}(a)h_0\), \[ -\int h(x)\cdot(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi=t|a|\longrightarrow+\infty. \] Incorrect marginals are detected similarly. Thus \(\inf_\pi\sup L\) equals the martingale primal value, whose feasible set is nonempty by Strassen. All integrands are bounded continuous on the compact product. Sion's theorem applies to this continuous affine Lagrangian and gives \[ \mathsf{MOT}_c=\sup_{\varphi,\psi,h}\left[\mu\varphi+\nu\psi+ \min_{A\times B}(c-\varphi-\psi-h(x)\cdot(y-x))\right]. \] If \(m\) is the displayed minimum, then \[ \varphi(x)+m+\psi(y)+h(x)\cdot(y-x)\le c(x,y), \qquad \mu(\varphi+m)+\nu\psi=\mu\varphi+\nu\psi+m. \] Thus each bracket is a feasible dual value. Conversely, for a martingale plan, \[ \int h(x)\cdot(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi =\int h(x)\cdot\mathbb E[Y-X\mid X=x]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0. \] Integrating dual feasibility gives \(\mu\varphi+\nu\psi\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi\), proving weak duality. This proves equality of values. It asserts no dual attainment; compact primal feasibility does not by itself give bounded dual maximizers.

The term \(h(x)\cdot(y-x)\) has no counterpart in ordinary transport. In robust pricing it is the gain of a self-financing linear hedge, while \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are static positions depending on the initial and terminal states.

One-dimensional structure

On \(\mathbb R\), convex order can be checked through call functions: \[ \mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\nu \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \int(x-k)_+\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x) \le \int(x-k)_+\,\mathrm{d}\nu(x) \quad\text{for every }k, \] together with equality of means. Canonical couplings such as the left-curtain coupling transport each infinitesimal source interval to at most two target locations and are selected by a martingale analogue of monotonicity. For suitable Spence--Mirrlees costs, this coupling is the unique optimizer.

Stability

Proposition 27.13 (Closedness of the martingale constraint). Suppose \(\pi_n\in\Pi_M(\mu_n,\nu_n)\), \(\pi_n\Rightarrow\pi\), and the first moments are uniformly integrable. Then \(\pi\in\Pi_M(\mu,\nu)\), where \(\mu,\nu\) are its marginals.

Proof. For a bounded continuous \(f\), choose a continuous cutoff \(\chi_R(x,y)\) equal to one when \(|x|+|y|\le R\), zero when \(|x|+|y|\ge2R\), and between zero and one otherwise. Weak convergence passes the bounded continuous integral \(\int\chi_R(y-x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_n\) to its limit. The error is at most \[ \|f\|_\infty\int_{\{|x|+|y|>R\}}(|x|+|y|)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_n\longrightarrow0 \] uniformly as \(R\to\infty\). Marginal uniform integrability implies this joint bound by the small-event cutoff estimate proved in the stability chapter, with \(p=1\). Set \(r=|x|+|y|\) and \(\tau(R)=\sup_n\int_{r>R}r\,\mathrm{d}\pi_n\to0\). Portmanteau for the continuous nonnegative function \((r-R)_+\) yields \[ \int_{r>2R}r\,\mathrm{d}\pi\le2\int(r-R)_+\,\mathrm{d}\pi \le2\liminf_n\int(r-R)_+\,\mathrm{d}\pi_n\le2\tau(R). \] Thus the limit also has integrable first moments and vanishing tails. For fixed \(R\), let \(n\to\infty\) in the cutoff integral; since \(\int(y-x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_n=0\), \[ \left|\int(y-x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi\right| \le\|f\|_\infty\left[\int_{r>R}r\,\mathrm{d}\pi+\tau(R)\right]\longrightarrow0. \] Hence the integral is zero. Define the finite vector signed measure \(M(E)=\int\mathbf1_E(x)(y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi\). Continuous tests annihilate it, so uniqueness of finite Borel measures gives \(M=0\). This is exactly the constraint for every bounded measurable \(f\).

Existence and stability of martingale optimizers then follow by the direct method. The essential extra hypothesis is first-moment control: weak convergence alone does not preserve conditional means.

Exercises

joint laws and conditional means

  1. Exercise 27.1. Can a martingale coupling send \(\delta_0\) to \(\delta_1\)? Can it send \(\delta_0\) to \((\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)/2\)?

    Hint

    Use preservation of the conditional mean, and then test the two proposed target means.

    Solution

    The first is impossible because means differ; the second is feasible, with the unique plan having conditional mean zero.

  2. Exercise 27.2. For three fair bits, can every pair be unequal almost surely under one joint law?

    Hint

    Fix one bit and use the two inequalities involving it to determine the relation between the remaining bits.

    Solution

    No. If the first differs from the second and the second differs from the third, the first equals the third. Pairwise feasible couplings need not be jointly compatible.
