Learning goal. Solve a small transport problem and certify its optimality with dual variables.

For finitely supported measures, optimal transport is a linear program. This is not merely a numerical approximation: the finite model displays the primal polytope, dual potentials, complementary slackness, integrality, and nonuniqueness in completely explicit form.

The transport polytope

Definition 3.1 (The transport polytope). Let \[ \mu=\sum_{i=1}^m a_i\delta_{x_i}, \qquad \nu=\sum_{j=1}^n b_j\delta_{y_j}, \] where \(a_i,b_j\ge0\) and \(\sum_i a_i=\sum_j b_j=1\). A coupling is an \(m\times n\) matrix \(P=(p_{ij})\) satisfying \[ p_{ij}\ge0,\qquad \sum_jp_{ij}=a_i,\qquad \sum_ip_{ij}=b_j. \] The feasible set \[ U(a,b)=\{P\ge0:P\mathbf1_n=a,\ P^\top\mathbf1_m=b\} \] is the transport polytope.

Proposition 3.2 (The transport polytope). If all \(a_i,b_j>0\), the affine hull of \(U(a,b)\) has dimension \((m-1)(n-1)\). The polytope is nonempty, compact, and convex.

Proof. The product matrix \(P^0=(a_ib_j)\) satisfies \[ \sum_jp^0_{ij}=a_i\sum_jb_j=a_i,\qquad \sum_ip^0_{ij}=b_j\sum_ia_i=b_j. \] Thus \(U(a,b)\ne\varnothing\). Every feasible matrix has \(0\le p_{ij}\le\min(a_i,b_j)\); the equality constraints and the nonnegative orthant are closed. Therefore \(U(a,b)\) is closed and bounded, hence compact. For feasible \(P,Q\), \[ ((1-t)P+tQ)\mathbf1=a,\quad ((1-t)P+tQ)^\top\mathbf1=b,\quad (1-t)P+tQ\ge0 \] for \(0\le t\le1\), proving convexity.

Let \(L(P)=(P\mathbf1,P^\top\mathbf1)\). A dependence among the \(m+n\) constraints has coefficients \((\alpha,\beta)\) satisfying \[ 0=\alpha\cdot P\mathbf1+\beta\cdot P^\top\mathbf1 =\sum_{ij}(\alpha_i+\beta_j)p_{ij}\quad\text{for every }P. \] Testing matrices with one nonzero entry gives \[ \alpha_i+\beta_j=0\ (\forall i,j) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha_i=t\ (\forall i),\ \beta_j=-t\ (\forall j). \] Thus \(\dim\ker L^\top=1\), \(\operatorname{rank}L=m+n-1\), and \[ \dim\{P:L(P)=(a,b)\}=mn-(m+n-1)=(m-1)(n-1). \] Since \(\delta=\min_{ij}a_ib_j>0\), every \(H\in\ker L\) with \(\|H\|_\infty<\delta\) satisfies \(P^0+H>0\) and is feasible. Hence the polytope contains a relative open neighborhood in this affine space and has that entire space as its affine hull.

Primal and dual linear programs

Theorem 3.3 (Finite Kantorovich duality). For the cost matrix \(C=(c_{ij})\), \[ \min_{P\in U(a,b)}\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}p_{ij} = \max_{\substack{u_i+v_j\le c_{ij}}} \left\{\sum_i a_iu_i+\sum_jb_jv_j\right\}. \]

Proof using finite-dimensional linear-programming duality. With row multipliers \(u\in\mathbb R^m\) and column multipliers \(v\in\mathbb R^n\), \[ \begin{aligned} L(P,u,v) &=\sum_{ij}c_{ij}p_{ij}+\sum_i u_i\left(a_i-\sum_jp_{ij}\right)+\sum_jv_j\left(b_j-\sum_ip_{ij}\right)\\ &=a\cdot u+b\cdot v+\sum_{ij}(c_{ij}-u_i-v_j)p_{ij}. \end{aligned} \] If some \(c_{ij}-u_i-v_j<0\), taking \(p_{ij}\to\infty\) and all other entries zero makes \(L\to-\infty\). If all coefficients are nonnegative, the minimum over \(P\ge0\) is attained at zero. Therefore \[ \inf_{P\ge0}L(P,u,v)= \begin{cases}a\cdot u+b\cdot v,&u_i+v_j\le c_{ij}\ \forall i,j,\\-\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \] For feasible \(P\) and feasible \((u,v)\), the gap is \[ \sum_{ij}c_{ij}p_{ij}-a\cdot u-b\cdot v =\sum_{ij}p_{ij}(c_{ij}-u_i-v_j)\ge0. \] The primal is feasible and compact, and its objective is finite because \(C\) is a real matrix. The finite-dimensional LP duality theorem therefore gives equality and attainment of the two optimal values. The gap vanishes exactly when every nonnegative summand vanishes: \[ p_{ij}(c_{ij}-u_i-v_j)=0\quad(\forall i,j). \] Together with equality of optimal values, this proves the complementary-slackness corollary as well.

Corollary 3.4 (Complementary slackness). A feasible \(P\) and feasible \((u,v)\) are optimal if and only if \[ p_{ij}>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_i+v_j=c_{ij}. \] Thus positive transport occurs only along tight dual edges.

Assignment and the Birkhoff polytope

Theorem 3.5 (Birkhoff--von Neumann theorem). Every \(n\times n\) doubly stochastic matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices. Consequently, the extreme points of \[ \mathcal B_n=\{P\ge0:P\mathbf1=\mathbf1,\ P^\top\mathbf1=\mathbf1\} \] are exactly the permutation matrices.

Proof. Join row \(i\) to column \(j\) when \(p_{ij}>0\). For row set \(S\) with neighbor set \(N(S)\), \[ |S|=\sum_{i\in S}\sum_jp_{ij} =\sum_{j\in N(S)}\sum_{i\in S}p_{ij} \le\sum_{j\in N(S)}\sum_ip_{ij}=|N(S)|. \] Hall's matching theorem (the finite combinatorial input) supplies a permutation \(\sigma\) with \(p_{i,\sigma(i)}>0\) for every \(i\). Set \(\varepsilon=\min_i p_{i,\sigma(i)}\). If \(\varepsilon=1\), the row sums force \(P=P_\sigma\). Otherwise \(0<\varepsilon<1\) and \[ Q=\frac{P-\varepsilon P_\sigma}{1-\varepsilon}\ge0,\qquad Q\mathbf1=\frac{\mathbf1-\varepsilon\mathbf1}{1-\varepsilon}=\mathbf1, \qquad Q^\top\mathbf1=\mathbf1. \] Thus \(P=\varepsilon P_\sigma+(1-\varepsilon)Q\). At least one selected positive entry becomes zero, and no zero entry becomes positive: \[ \#\{(i,j):q_{ij}>0\}<\#\{(i,j):p_{ij}>0\}. \] Induction on this finite integer terminates at permutation matrices. Substitution gives \(P=\sum_{r=1}^N\theta_rP_{\sigma_r}\), \(\theta_r\ge0\), \(\sum_r\theta_r=1\).

If \(P_\sigma=tA+(1-t)B\) with \(0<t<1\) and \(A,B\in\mathcal B_n\), then \[ (P_\sigma)_{ij}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ a_{ij}=b_{ij}=0, \qquad \sum_ja_{ij}=\sum_jb_{ij}=1 \ \Longrightarrow\ a_{i,\sigma(i)}=b_{i,\sigma(i)}=1. \] Hence \(A=B=P_\sigma\): permutations are extreme. Conversely the convex decomposition shows an extreme matrix must itself be a permutation. Finally, linearity gives \[ \langle C,P\rangle=\sum_r\theta_r\langle C,P_{\sigma_r}\rangle \ge\min_\sigma\langle C,P_\sigma\rangle, \] which proves the assignment corollary after dividing by \(n\) for probability masses.

Corollary 3.6 (Assignment corollary). For equal-weight measures with \(m=n\), a linear transport cost has an optimal permutation plan. The Kantorovich relaxation therefore has the same value as the discrete assignment problem, though the relaxed optimizer may be nonunique.

Network structure and integrality

The constraint matrix is the incidence matrix of a bipartite graph and is totally unimodular. Hence if the supplies and demands are integers, every vertex of the transport polytope is integral. This is why a min-cost flow algorithm returns an integral shipment without adding integer constraints. In the equal-unit case, integrality reduces the vertices to assignments.

Proposition 3.7 (Sparse vertices). Every nondegenerate vertex of \(U(a,b)\) has exactly \(m+n-1\) positive entries; every vertex has at most \(m+n-1\) positive entries.

Proof. Suppose the positive support contains a simple cycle with edges \((i_r,j_r)\), \((i_{r+1},j_r)\), \(1\le r\le k\), where \(i_{k+1}=i_1\). Define \(H\) by \[ h_{i_rj_r}=1,\qquad h_{i_{r+1}j_r}=-1, \qquad h_{ij}=0\text{ off the cycle}. \] Each row and column of \(H\) sums to zero. For \(0<\varepsilon<\min\{p_{ij}:(i,j)\text{ lies on the cycle}\}\), \[ P^\pm=P\pm\varepsilon H\ge0,\quad P^\pm\mathbf1=a,\quad (P^\pm)^\top\mathbf1=b, \quad P=\tfrac12(P^++P^-),\quad P^+\ne P^-. \] So a vertex cannot have a cycle. Its support is a forest on \(m+n\) vertices; with \(k\ge1\) connected components, the number of positive entries is \[ |E|=(m+n)-k\le m+n-1. \] A nondegenerate basic feasible solution has as many positive variables as the constraint rank, namely \(m+n-1\). Its forest therefore has \(k=1\), so it is a spanning tree.

A complete two-by-three example

Example 3.8 (A complete two-by-three example). Take \[ a=(1/2,1/2),\qquad b=(1/4,1/2,1/4),\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&3\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}. \] Consider \[ P^\star= \begin{pmatrix} 1/4&1/4&0\\ 0&1/4&1/4 \end{pmatrix}. \] Its row and column sums are \(a,b\), and its cost is \(1/2\). The dual variables \[ u=(0,0),\qquad v=(0,1,0) \] satisfy \(u_i+v_j\le c_{ij}\) and have dual value \(b\cdot v=1/2\). Primal and dual values agree, proving optimality. Every positive entry of \(P^\star\) lies on a tight edge, which displays complementary slackness directly.

From discrete to continuous transport

Approximating \(\mu,\nu\) by empirical or quantized measures turns continuous transport into a sequence of finite linear programs. The later stability theorem in Existence and stability of optimal plans explains when their optimal values and plans converge. Entropic regularization will later replace the sparse polytope optimizer by a smooth positive matrix that can be found through diagonal scaling.

Exercises

primal and dual certificates

  1. Exercise 3.1. In the two-by-three example, replace \(c_{13}=3\) by zero. Does the displayed plan remain optimal?

    Hint

    Recheck the dual inequality at the modified entry; an old certificate may cease to be feasible.

    Solution

    Yes. The displayed dual variables remain feasible, and both primal and dual values remain \(1/2\). Changing a cost on an unused edge need not change the optimizer, but feasibility must be rechecked.

  2. Exercise 3.2. For \(a=b=(1/2,1/2)\) and \(C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), find the optimal plan and a dual certificate.

    Hint

    Match the identical locations, then seek potentials whose sum never exceeds an entry of the cost matrix.

    Solution

    The diagonal plan \(P=I/2\) has cost zero; zero dual variables give the same value. Positive off-diagonal mass costs more, so the plan is unique.
