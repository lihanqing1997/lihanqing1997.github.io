Learning goal. Explain how convex differentiability turns a plan into a unique map, and compute a Gaussian example.

For squared Euclidean distance, cyclical monotonicity becomes ordinary convexity. This identifies the optimal map as a gradient and gives a multidimensional replacement for monotone rearrangement.

Convex subgradients

Definition 9.1 (Convex subgradients). For a proper lower-semicontinuous convex function \(u:\mathbb R^d\to(-\infty,\infty]\), its subdifferential at \(x\) is \[ \partial u(x)=\{y:u(z)\ge u(x)+y\cdot(z-x)\ \text{for every }z\}. \] If \(u\) is differentiable at \(x\), then \(\partial u(x)=\{\nabla u(x)\}\).

Theorem 9.2 (Rockafellar theorem). A set \(\Gamma\subset\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d\) is cyclically monotone for the pairing, meaning \[ \sum_i x_i\cdot y_i\ge\sum_i x_i\cdot y_{\sigma(i)}, \] if and only if it is contained in \(\partial u\) for some proper lower-semicontinuous convex \(u\).

For \(c(x,y)=\tfrac12|x-y|^2\), expanding the squares shows that \(c\)-cyclical monotonicity is exactly cyclical monotonicity of the pairing. The quadratic terms cancel under permutations.

Proof of Rockafellar's theorem. If \(\Gamma=\varnothing\), take \(u=0\). Otherwise fix \((x_0,y_0)\in\Gamma\). For a finite chain \((x_i,y_i)\in\Gamma\), \(1\le i\le m\), define \[ A_{\mathcal C}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\langle x_{i+1}-x_i,y_i\rangle+\langle x-x_m,y_m\rangle, \qquad u(x)=\sup_{\mathcal C}A_{\mathcal C}(x). \] For \(m=0\), take \(A_{\mathcal C}(x)=\langle x-x_0,y_0\rangle\). Every \(A_{\mathcal C}\) is finite affine continuous, so their supremum is convex, lower semicontinuous, and never \(-\infty\). Cyclical monotonicity for a chain closed at \(x_0\) gives \[ A_{\mathcal C}(x_0)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\langle x_{i+1}-x_i,y_i\rangle+\langle x_0-x_m,y_m\rangle\le0. \] The chain of length zero gives zero, hence \(u(x_0)=0\) and \(u\) is proper.

For \((a,b)\in\Gamma\), close the chain through \((a,b)\) and back to \(x_0\). The same inequality reads \[ A_{\mathcal C}(a)+\langle x_0-a,b\rangle\le0, \quad\text{so}\quad \langle a-x_0,y_0\rangle\le u(a)\le\langle a-x_0,b\rangle<\infty. \] Appending \((a,b)\) to a chain gives an admissible expression at \(z\): \[ u(z)\ge A_{\mathcal C}(a)+\langle z-a,b\rangle. \] Taking the supremum over \(\mathcal C\) yields \(u(z)\ge u(a)+\langle z-a,b\rangle\) for every \(z\), so \(b\in\partial u(a)\).

Conversely, if \(y_i\in\partial u(x_i)\), the subgradient inequality gives \[ u(x_{\sigma^{-1}(i)})\ge u(x_i)+y_i\cdot(x_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}-x_i). \] Sum in \(i\). The \(u\)-terms cancel under a permutation, giving \[ 0\ge\sum_i y_i\cdot x_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}-\sum_i y_i\cdot x_i =\sum_j x_j\cdot y_{\sigma(j)}-\sum_jx_j\cdot y_j, \] which is the required cyclical monotonicity.

Brenier's theorem

Theorem 9.3 (Brenier's theorem). Let \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\), and suppose \(\mu\) is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Then there exists a convex lower-semicontinuous \(u\) such that \[ T=\nabla u,\qquad T_\#\mu=\nu. \] The plan \((\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu\) is the unique optimal plan for the cost \(\tfrac12|x-y|^2\). Consequently \(T\) is the unique optimal map \(\mu\)-almost everywhere.

Proof. Existence and convex structure. The product plan has finite quadratic cost because \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2\); the existence theorem gives an optimizer \(\pi\). Its support \(\Gamma\) is cyclically monotone for quadratic cost. Cancelling the marginal square terms in the cyclic inequality gives \[ \sum_i\left(\tfrac12|x_i|^2+\tfrac12|y_i|^2-x_i\cdot y_i\right) \le\sum_i\left(\tfrac12|x_i|^2+\tfrac12|y_{\sigma(i)}|^2-x_i\cdot y_{\sigma(i)}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_ix_i\cdot y_i\ge\sum_ix_i\cdot y_{\sigma(i)}. \] Rockafellar therefore gives proper lower-semicontinuous convex \(u\) with \(\Gamma\subset\partial u\).

Concentration on a graph. Write \(D=\operatorname{dom}u\). The first marginal satisfies \(\mu(D)=1\). A convex set with empty interior is contained in a proper affine subspace, of Lebesgue measure zero; this contradicts \(\mu\ll\mathcal L^d\) and \(\mu(D)=1\). Thus \(D\) has nonempty interior. We use two convex-analysis facts: \(\mathcal L^d(\partial D)=0\), and \(u\) is locally Lipschitz on \(D^\circ\). Rademacher's theorem gives a Lebesgue-null set \(N\subset D^\circ\) outside which \(u\) is differentiable. Hence \[ \mu(D^\circ\setminus N)=1,\qquad \partial u(x)=\{\nabla u(x)\}\quad(x\in D^\circ\setminus N). \] Choose a Borel representative \(T=\nabla u\) on this full-measure set and extend it arbitrarily elsewhere. Disintegrate \(\pi=\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\pi_x(\mathrm{d}y)\). Since \(\pi(\partial u)=1\), \[ 1=\int\pi_x(\partial u(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad 0\le\pi_x(\partial u(x))\le1, \] so \(\pi_x(\{T(x)\})=1\) almost everywhere. Thus \[ \pi=\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\delta_{T(x)}(\mathrm{d}y)=(\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu, \qquad T_\#\mu=\pi_Y=\nu. \]

Uniqueness. If \(\widetilde\pi\) is another optimizer, linearity of the cost gives \[ \int c\,\mathrm{d}\bigl((\pi+\widetilde\pi)/2\bigr) =\tfrac12K_c+\tfrac12K_c=K_c. \] The argument just proved makes this average a graph plan, say with kernel \(\delta_{S(x)}\). Uniqueness of disintegration gives \[ \tfrac12\delta_{T(x)}+\tfrac12\widetilde\pi_x=\delta_{S(x)}\quad\mu\text{-a.e.} \] Evaluating the complement of \(\{S(x)\}\), both nonnegative summands must vanish. Thus \(T(x)=S(x)\) and \(\widetilde\pi_x=\delta_{S(x)}\) almost everywhere. Consequently \(\widetilde\pi=\pi\), and every optimal map equals \(T\) \(\mu\)-almost everywhere.

Converse optimality

Proposition 9.4 (Converse optimality). Let \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\). If \(u\) is convex, \(\nabla u\) exists \(\mu\)-almost everywhere, and \(T=\nabla u\) pushes \(\mu\) to \(\nu\), then \(T\) is optimal for quadratic cost.

Proof. Choose \((x_0,y_0)\in\partial u\). The subgradient inequality and conjugate definition imply \[ u(x)\ge u(x_0)+y_0\cdot(x-x_0),\qquad u^*(y)\ge x_0\cdot y-u(x_0). \] Thus for a finite constant \(C\), \[ u^-(x)\le C+|y_0||x|\in L^1(\mu),\qquad (u^*)^-(y)\le C+|x_0||y|\in L^1(\nu). \] Fenchel equality on \(T=\nabla u\) gives \(u(x)+u^*(T(x))=x\cdot T(x)\) almost everywhere, and \[ \int|x\cdot T(x)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu \le\left(\int|x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/2} \left(\int|T(x)|^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/2} =\left(\int|x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu\int|y|^2\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{1/2}<\infty. \] The identity also gives the positive-part bounds \[ u^+(x)\le|x\cdot T(x)|+(u^*)^-(T(x)),\quad (u^*)^+(T(x))\le|x\cdot T(x)|+u^-(x). \] Hence \(u\in L^1(\mu)\), \(u^*\in L^1(\nu)\), and all ensuing subtractions are finite.

For any \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), integrate \(x\cdot y\le u(x)+u^*(y)\): \[ \int x\cdot y\,\mathrm{d}\pi \le\int u\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int u^*\,\mathrm{d}\nu =\int x\cdot T(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu. \] Let \(A=\tfrac12\int|x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\tfrac12\int|y|^2\,\mathrm{d}\nu\). Then \[ \int\tfrac12|x-y|^2\,\mathrm{d}\pi =A-\int x\cdot y\,\mathrm{d}\pi \ge A-\int x\cdot T(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu =\int\tfrac12|x-T(x)|^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu, \] which proves optimality.

Inverse transport and Legendre duality

If both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are absolutely continuous, the reverse optimal map is \(\nabla u^*\), where \[ u^*(y)=\sup_x\{x\cdot y-u(x)\}. \] At differentiability points, \[ y=\nabla u(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=\nabla u^*(y). \] Thus the forward and reverse maps are inverse almost everywhere with respect to the corresponding measures.

Gaussian transport

Proposition 9.5 (Gaussian transport). Let \(\mu=N(m_0,\Sigma_0)\), \(\nu=N(m_1,\Sigma_1)\), with positive-definite covariances. The Brenier map is \[ T(x)=m_1+A(x-m_0), \quad A=\Sigma_0^{-1/2} (\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2} \Sigma_0^{-1/2}, \] and \[ W_2^2(\mu,\nu) =|m_0-m_1|^2+ \operatorname{tr}\!\left( \Sigma_0+\Sigma_1 -2(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2} \right). \]

Proof. Put \(B=(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2}\), \(A=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}B\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\). Symmetry is immediate from transposition, and for \(z\ne0\), \[ z^\top Az=(\Sigma_0^{-1/2}z)^\top B(\Sigma_0^{-1/2}z)>0. \] Thus \(T(x)=m_1+A(x-m_0)=\nabla u(x)\) for the convex quadratic \(u(x)=m_1\cdot x+\tfrac12(x-m_0)^\top A(x-m_0)\). Matrix multiplication gives \[ \begin{aligned} A\Sigma_0A^\top &=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}B\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\Sigma_0\Sigma_0^{-1/2}B\Sigma_0^{-1/2}\\ &=\Sigma_0^{-1/2}B^2\Sigma_0^{-1/2}=\Sigma_1. \end{aligned} \] An affine image of a Gaussian is Gaussian with transformed mean and covariance, so \(T_\#\mu=\nu\). The preceding proposition proves optimality.

Let \(Z=X-m_0\), \(\Delta=m_0-m_1\). Then \(\mathbb EZ=0\), \(\mathbb EZZ^\top=\Sigma_0\), and \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E|X-T(X)|^2 &=\mathbb E|\Delta+(I-A)Z|^2\\ &=|\Delta|^2+2\Delta^\top(I-A)\mathbb EZ +\operatorname{tr}((I-A)\Sigma_0(I-A)^\top)\\ &=|\Delta|^2+\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0+A\Sigma_0A-\Sigma_0A-A\Sigma_0). \end{aligned} \] Cyclicity of trace yields \[ \operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma_0)=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_0^{-1/2}B\Sigma_0^{1/2})=\operatorname{tr}B. \] Together with \(A\Sigma_0A=\Sigma_1\), this gives the stated formula without assuming the covariance matrices commute.

Polar factorization

Theorem 9.6 (Polar factorization with a nondegenerate image). Let \(\mu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\) be absolutely continuous, \(S\in L^2(\mu;\mathbb R^d)\), and suppose \(\nu=S_\#\mu\) is also absolutely continuous. If \(T=\nabla u\) is the Brenier map from \(\mu\) to \(\nu\), then there is a \(\mu\)-preserving map \(s\) such that \(S=T\circ s\) almost everywhere.

Proof. The optimal plan \(\pi=(\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu\) has reversed plan \(\pi^{\mathrm{rev}}=(T,\operatorname{id})_\#\mu\), optimal from \(\nu\) to \(\mu\) because the quadratic cost is symmetric. Since \(\nu\) is absolutely continuous, Brenier uniqueness in that direction makes it \((\operatorname{id},R)_\#\nu\). Reversing the subgradient relation identifies \(R=\nabla u^*\) almost everywhere. Equality of these joint laws gives \[ R(T(x))=x\quad\mu\text{-a.e.},\qquad T(R(y))=y\quad\nu\text{-a.e.},\qquad R_\#\nu=\mu. \] Set \(s=R\circ S\). For Borel \(A\), \[ \mu(s^{-1}(A))=\mu(S^{-1}(R^{-1}(A))) =\nu(R^{-1}(A))=\mu(A). \] Thus \(s_\#\mu=\mu\). If \(N\) is the \(\nu\)-null set where \(T\circ R\ne\operatorname{id}\), then \(\mu(S^{-1}(N))=\nu(N)=0\). Outside that set, \[ T(s(x))=T(R(S(x)))=S(x), \] proving the factorization almost everywhere.

This is the nonlinear analogue of matrix polar decomposition: a rearrangement preserving the source law is followed by a convex-gradient deformation. Absolute continuity of the image is a sufficient nondegeneracy assumption for this proof; it must not be silently omitted.

Why absolute continuity matters

Example 9.7 (Why absolute continuity matters). If \(\mu=\delta_0\) and \(\nu=\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)\), the optimal plan cannot be induced by a map. More subtly, a source supported on a lower-dimensional surface may meet a multivalued part of \(\partial u\) on a set of positive \(\mu\)-mass. Absolute continuity removes this ambiguity because convex functions are differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere.

Exercises

convex gradients and gaussian maps

  1. Exercise 9.1. For centered Gaussian laws with covariances \(I\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(4,9)\), compute \(T\) and \(W_2^2\).

    Hint

    Use the Gaussian transport formula; diagonal covariance matrices reduce it to separate scalar calculations.

    Solution

    The map is \(T(x_1,x_2)=(2x_1,3x_2)\). The squared cost is \((2-1)^2+(3-1)^2=5\).

  2. Exercise 9.2. Why can averaging two optimal graph plans prove uniqueness, even though the average of two maps is not used?

    Hint

    Disintegrate the average of the two plans and apply the graph property at a fixed source point.

    Solution

    The average is a plan with conditional law \((\delta_{T_0(x)}+\delta_{T_1(x)})/2\). The graph property of every optimal plan forces these two atoms to coincide almost everywhere.
