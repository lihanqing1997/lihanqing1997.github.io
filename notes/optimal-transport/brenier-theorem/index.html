Learning goal. Explain how convex differentiability turns a plan into a unique map, and compute a Gaussian example.

For squared Euclidean distance, cyclical monotonicity becomes ordinary convexity. This identifies the optimal map as a gradient and gives a multidimensional replacement for monotone rearrangement.

Convex subgradients

Definition. For a proper lower-semicontinuous convex function \(u:\mathbb R^d\to(-\infty,\infty]\), its subdifferential at \(x\) is \[ \partial u(x)=\{y:u(z)\ge u(x)+y\cdot(z-x)\ \text{for every }z\}. \] If \(u\) is differentiable at \(x\), then \(\partial u(x)=\{\nabla u(x)\}\).

Rockafellar theorem. A set \(\Gamma\subset\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d\) is cyclically monotone for the pairing, meaning \[ \sum_i x_i\cdot y_i\ge\sum_i x_i\cdot y_{\sigma(i)}, \] if and only if it is contained in \(\partial u\) for some proper lower-semicontinuous convex \(u\).

For \(c(x,y)=\tfrac12|x-y|^2\), expanding the squares shows that \(c\)-cyclical monotonicity is exactly cyclical monotonicity of the pairing. The quadratic terms cancel under permutations.

Brenier's theorem

Brenier's theorem. Let \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\), and suppose \(\mu\) is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Then there exists a convex lower-semicontinuous \(u\) such that \[ T=\nabla u,\qquad T_\#\mu=\nu. \] The plan \((\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu\) is the unique optimal plan for the cost \(\tfrac12|x-y|^2\). Consequently \(T\) is the unique optimal map \(\mu\)-almost everywhere.

Proof. Existence of an optimal plan \(\pi\) follows from Existence and stability of optimal plans. Its support is \(c\)-cyclically monotone, so after expanding the quadratic cost it is cyclically monotone for \(x\cdot y\). Rockafellar's theorem gives a convex \(u\) with \(\operatorname{supp}\pi\subset\partial u\).

A convex function on \(\mathbb R^d\) is locally Lipschitz in the interior of its effective domain and therefore differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere by Rademacher's theorem. The first marginal is concentrated on \(\operatorname{dom}\partial u\subset\operatorname{dom}u\). Since it is absolutely continuous, this convex domain cannot be lower dimensional. It has nonempty interior, and its boundary has Lebesgue measure zero. Thus \(\mu\) is concentrated on interior differentiability points, where \(\partial u(x)=\{\nabla u(x)\}\). Disintegrating \(\pi=\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\pi_x(\mathrm{d}y)\), the support inclusion forces \(\pi_x=\delta_{\nabla u(x)}\) almost surely. Hence \(\pi=(\operatorname{id},\nabla u)_\#\mu\) and \((\nabla u)_\#\mu=\nu\).

If \(\widetilde\pi\) is another optimal plan, the average \((\pi+\widetilde\pi)/2\) is optimal and the same argument makes it a graph plan. But the conditional law of this average is \(\tfrac12\delta_{\nabla u(x)}+\tfrac12\widetilde\pi_x\), which can be a Dirac mass only when \(\widetilde\pi_x=\delta_{\nabla u(x)}\) almost surely. Thus the optimal plan is unique.

Converse optimality

Proposition. Let \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\). If \(u\) is convex, \(\nabla u\) exists \(\mu\)-almost everywhere, and \(T=\nabla u\) pushes \(\mu\) to \(\nu\), then \(T\) is optimal for quadratic cost.

Proof outline. Summing subgradient inequalities shows the graph is cyclically monotone for the pairing, hence for quadratic cost. The finite-second-moment quadratic sufficiency theorem then gives optimality; it is the quadratic specialization of Ambrosio–Gigli, §1.2. A Fenchel-duality proof is also possible, but must check integrability of the resulting potentials rather than assume it.

Inverse transport and Legendre duality

If both \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) are absolutely continuous, the reverse optimal map is \(\nabla u^*\), where \[ u^*(y)=\sup_x\{x\cdot y-u(x)\}. \] At differentiability points, \[ y=\nabla u(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=\nabla u^*(y). \] Thus the forward and reverse maps are inverse almost everywhere with respect to the corresponding measures.

Gaussian transport

Proposition. Let \(\mu=N(m_0,\Sigma_0)\), \(\nu=N(m_1,\Sigma_1)\), with positive-definite covariances. The Brenier map is \[ T(x)=m_1+A(x-m_0), \quad A=\Sigma_0^{-1/2} (\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2} \Sigma_0^{-1/2}, \] and \[ W_2^2(\mu,\nu) =|m_0-m_1|^2+ \operatorname{tr}\!\left( \Sigma_0+\Sigma_1 -2(\Sigma_0^{1/2}\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{1/2})^{1/2} \right). \]

Proof. The matrix \(A\) is symmetric positive definite, hence \(T=\nabla u\) for the convex quadratic \[ u(x)=m_1\cdot x+\tfrac12(x-m_0)^\top A(x-m_0). \] Direct multiplication gives \(A\Sigma_0A^\top=\Sigma_1\), so \(T_\#\mu=\nu\). The converse proposition proves optimality. Expanding \(\mathbb E|X-T(X)|^2\) and using the covariance identity yields the distance formula.

Polar factorization

Polar factorization with a nondegenerate image. Let \(\mu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\) be absolutely continuous, \(S\in L^2(\mu;\mathbb R^d)\), and suppose \(\nu=S_\#\mu\) is also absolutely continuous. If \(T=\nabla u\) is the Brenier map from \(\mu\) to \(\nu\), then there is a \(\mu\)-preserving map \(s\) such that \(S=T\circ s\) almost everywhere.

Proof. The reverse Brenier map \(R=\nabla u^*\) satisfies \(R_\#\nu=\mu\), \(T\circ R=\operatorname{id}\) \(\nu\)-almost everywhere. Set \(s=R\circ S\). Then \(s_\#\mu=R_\#\nu=\mu\) and \(T\circ s=T\circ R\circ S=S\) almost everywhere.

This is the nonlinear analogue of matrix polar decomposition: a rearrangement preserving the source law is followed by a convex-gradient deformation. Absolute continuity of the image is a sufficient nondegeneracy assumption for this proof; it must not be silently omitted.

Why absolute continuity matters

Example. If \(\mu=\delta_0\) and \(\nu=\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)\), the optimal plan cannot be induced by a map. More subtly, a source supported on a lower-dimensional surface may meet a multivalued part of \(\partial u\) on a set of positive \(\mu\)-mass. Absolute continuity removes this ambiguity because convex functions are differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere.

Sources and proof details. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport, §1.2, for cyclic monotonicity, Rockafellar, and quadratic sufficiency; §1.4 for optimal maps. Convex subgradients are reviewed in the convex optimization note.

Practice: convex gradients and gaussian maps

  1. For centered Gaussian laws with covariances \(I\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(4,9)\), compute \(T\) and \(W_2^2\).

    Hint and answer

    The map is \(T(x_1,x_2)=(2x_1,3x_2)\). The squared cost is \((2-1)^2+(3-1)^2=5\).

  2. Why can averaging two optimal graph plans prove uniqueness, even though the average of two maps is not used?

    Hint and answer

    The average is a plan with conditional law \((\delta_{T_0(x)}+\delta_{T_1(x)})/2\). The graph property of every optimal plan forces these two atoms to coincide almost everywhere.
