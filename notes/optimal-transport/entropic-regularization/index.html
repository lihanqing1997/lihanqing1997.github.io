Learning goal. Derive Sinkhorn scaling, recognize its entropy convention, and distinguish numerical convergence from regularization bias.

Entropy makes the transport objective strictly convex; in finite dimensions the optimizer also depends smoothly on positive marginal weights. Its optimizer has a multiplicative Gibbs form, and enforcing the two marginals reduces to alternating diagonal scalings. The regularization is therefore simultaneously variational, probabilistic, and algorithmic.

For the continuous results on this page, take compact metric spaces \(X,Y\) and finite continuous cost \(c\). For matrix scaling, discard zero-weight atoms and assume \(a_i,b_j>0\). These hypotheses keep the kernel strictly positive and bounded away from zero; unbounded costs and noncompact spaces need additional integrability assumptions.

The entropic transport problem

Definition 10.1 (The entropic transport problem). For \(\varepsilon>0\), define \[ \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)} \left\{ \int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi +\varepsilon\operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\mu\otimes\nu) \right\}. \] The reference \(\mu\otimes\nu\) makes the entropy term invariant under changes of coordinates and equal to the mutual information of a coupling.

Theorem 10.2 (Existence and uniqueness). Under the compact continuous-cost assumptions above, the entropic problem has a unique minimizer \(\pi_\varepsilon\).

Proof. Let \(R=\mu\otimes\nu\) and \(F_\varepsilon(\pi)=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+\varepsilon H(\pi\mid R)\). The product plan gives a finite comparison value: \[ \inf_{\Pi(\mu,\nu)}F_\varepsilon\le F_\varepsilon(R)=\int c\,\mathrm{d}R<\infty. \] We use the entropy variational formula \[ H(\pi\mid R)=\sup_{f\in C(X\times Y)}\left\{\int f\,\mathrm{d}\pi-\log\int e^f\,\mathrm{d}R\right\}. \] It makes entropy weakly lower semicontinuous, as a supremum of continuous affine functions of \(\pi\). This is a probability input; the entropy chapter derives the corresponding variational identity. A minimizing sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence \(\pi_{n_k}\Rightarrow\pi_*\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), and \[ \inf F_\varepsilon\le F_\varepsilon(\pi_*) \le\liminf_kF_\varepsilon(\pi_{n_k})=\inf F_\varepsilon. \] Thus a finite-objective minimizer exists.

If two distinct minimizers have densities \(r_0,r_1\) relative to \(R\), the set \(E=\{r_0\ne r_1\}\) has positive \(R\)-measure. Strict convexity of \(h(r)=r\log r-r+1\) gives \[ h\left(\tfrac12(r_0+r_1)\right)\le\tfrac12h(r_0)+\tfrac12h(r_1), \] with strict inequality on \(E\). Integrating and adding the linear cost yields \[ F_\varepsilon((\pi_0+\pi_1)/2)<\tfrac12F_\varepsilon(\pi_0)+\tfrac12F_\varepsilon(\pi_1)=\inf F_\varepsilon, \] a contradiction. Measures with infinite entropy cannot minimize the finite value.

Dual formulation

Theorem 10.3 (Entropic duality). Under the assumptions above, \[ \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\varphi,\psi} \left[ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu -\varepsilon\int \left\{ e^{(\varphi(x)+\psi(y)-c(x,y))/\varepsilon}-1 \right\} \,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y) \right]. \] At optimality, \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon}{\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\nu)}(x,y) = \exp\!\left(\frac{\varphi_\varepsilon(x)+\psi_\varepsilon(y)-c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right). \]

Proof. Put \(h(r)=r\log r-r+1\) for \(r\ge0\). Maximizing \(sr-\varepsilon h(r)\) gives the critical equation \(s-\varepsilon\log r=0\), so \[ \sup_{r\ge0}\{sr-\varepsilon h(r)\}=\varepsilon(e^{s/\varepsilon}-1), \qquad \varepsilon h(r)-sr+\varepsilon(e^{s/\varepsilon}-1)\ge0, \] with equality exactly at \(r=e^{s/\varepsilon}\). For a feasible density \(r=\mathrm{d}\pi/\mathrm{d}R\), \(R=\mu\otimes\nu\), let \(s=\varphi(x)+\psi(y)-c(x,y)\). The marginal constraints imply \[ \int r\,\mathrm{d}R=1,\qquad \int(\varphi(x)+\psi(y))r\,\mathrm{d}R =\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu=:J. \] Consequently the primal-minus-dual gap is \[ \begin{aligned} &\int cr\,\mathrm{d}R+\varepsilon H(\pi\mid R) -\left[J-\varepsilon\int(e^{s/\varepsilon}-1)\,\mathrm{d}R\right]\\ &\quad=\int\left[\varepsilon h(r)-sr+\varepsilon(e^{s/\varepsilon}-1)\right]\,\mathrm{d}R\ge0. \end{aligned} \] Infinite-entropy competitors have infinite primal value, so this proves weak duality for all competitors.

The scaling construction below, proved independently of duality, supplies continuous positive \(u,v\) with \(r(x,y)=u(x)e^{-c(x,y)/\varepsilon}v(y)\) having the prescribed marginals. Set \[ \varphi=\varepsilon\log u,\qquad\psi=\varepsilon\log v,\qquad r=e^{(\varphi+\psi-c)/\varepsilon}=e^{s/\varepsilon}. \] These quantities are bounded on the compact product, and the gap integrand is identically zero. Hence primal and dual values coincide and both are attained, with the stated density formula.

The Schrödinger system

Let \(K_\varepsilon(x,y)=e^{-c(x,y)/\varepsilon}\), \(u(x)=e^{\varphi_\varepsilon(x)/\varepsilon}\), and \(v(y)=e^{\psi_\varepsilon(y)/\varepsilon}\). Then \[ \pi_\varepsilon(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y) =u(x)K_\varepsilon(x,y)v(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\nu(\mathrm{d}y). \] The marginal constraints become \[ u(x)\int K_\varepsilon(x,y)v(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)=1, \qquad v(y)\int K_\varepsilon(x,y)u(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=1. \] This pair of nonlinear integral equations is the Schrödinger system.

Discrete matrix scaling

For discrete marginals \(a\in\Delta_m\), \(b\in\Delta_n\), let \(K_{ij}=e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}>0\). Absorbing the marginal weights into scaling vectors, the unique optimizer has form \[ P_\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v), \] with \[ u\odot(Kv)=a,\qquad v\odot(K^\top u)=b. \]

Proposition 10.4 (Sinkhorn iterations). Starting from \(v^{(0)}>0\), alternate \[ u^{(k+1)}=a\oslash(Kv^{(k)}), \qquad v^{(k+1)}=b\oslash(K^\top u^{(k+1)}), \] where \(\oslash\) is componentwise division. Every row update enforces the first marginal exactly, and every column update enforces the second.

A practical scaling loop

v = vector of ones
repeat:
    u = a / (K v)             # componentwise division
    v = b / (transpose(K) u)
    P = diag(u) K diag(v)
    residual = norm1(P ones - a) + norm1(transpose(P) ones - b)
until residual is below tolerance or the iteration limit is reached

Check both marginals after a complete sweep. Report the residual and whether the iteration limit was reached; a limit is not a convergence certificate. For small \(\varepsilon\), use the log-domain version below. A dense sweep costs \(O(mn)\) arithmetic and storing the dense kernel costs \(O(mn)\) memory; the number of sweeps depends on the kernel and accuracy.

The continuous factors above were defined relative to \(\mu\otimes\nu\). The discrete vectors here absorb \(a_i,b_j\). If \(f=\varepsilon\log u\), \(g=\varepsilon\log v\) are these absorbed log-scalings, the relative-entropy dual potentials are \(\varphi_i=f_i-\varepsilon\log a_i\), \(\psi_j=g_j-\varepsilon\log b_j\), up to opposite additive constants. This distinction matters when differentiating the value with respect to marginal weights.

Why Sinkhorn converges

Theorem 10.5 (Sinkhorn scaling theorem). If \(K\) has strictly positive entries and \(a,b\) are strictly positive probability vectors, there exist positive \(u,v\), unique up to \((u,v)\mapsto(\lambda u,\lambda^{-1}v)\), such that \(\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v)\in U(a,b)\). Sinkhorn iterates converge to this scaled matrix.

Proof, including the continuous kernel used above. Work first with a continuous kernel \(K\) on compact \(X\times Y\), with \(0<k_-\le K\le k_+\). Integrals below use the probability marginals \(\mu,\nu\). Set \[ (L_Kz)(x)=\log\int K(x,y)e^{z(y)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y),\qquad F(z)=-L_{K^\top}(-L_Kz). \] For a bounded function \(w\), write \(\operatorname{osc}w=\sup w-\inf w\). Differentiation under the integral, justified by boundedness, gives \[ D L_K(z)[w](x)=\int w(y)p_{x,z}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y),\qquad p_{x,z}(y)=\frac{K(x,y)e^{z(y)}}{\int K(x,y')e^{z(y')}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y')}. \] For every \(x,x'\), \(p_{x,z}\ge\eta p_{x',z}\), where \(\eta=(k_-/k_+)^2\). If \(\eta<1\), write \[ p_{x,z}=\eta p_{x',z}+(1-\eta)q_{x,x',z},\qquad q_{x,x',z}\ge0,\quad\int q_{x,x',z}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=1. \] The ratio estimate follows from \[ \frac{p_{x,z}(y)}{p_{x',z}(y)} =\frac{K(x,y)}{K(x',y)}\frac{\int K(x',w)e^{z(w)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(w)}{\int K(x,w)e^{z(w)}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(w)} \ge(k_-/k_+)^2=\eta. \] Thus the derivative difference is \((1-\eta)(\int wq_{x,x',z}\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\int wp_{x',z}\,\mathrm{d}\nu)\), whose absolute value is at most \((1-\eta)(\sup w-\inf w)\). When \(\eta=1\), the two densities coincide. This proves \[ |D L_K(z)[w](x)-D L_K(z)[w](x')| \le(1-\eta)\operatorname{osc}w. \] For the segment \(z_t=z'+t(z-z')\), boundedness of \(z,z',w,K\) justifies differentiation under the integral by dominated convergence. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives \[ L_Kz-L_Kz'=\int_0^1 DL_K(z_t)[z-z']\,\mathrm{d}t. \] Taking the oscillation gives \[ \operatorname{osc}(L_Kz-L_Kz')\le q\operatorname{osc}(z-z'),\qquad q=1-\eta<1. \] The transposed operator satisfies the same bound, so \(F\) is a contraction with factor \(q^2\) modulo additive constants.

Fix \(y_0\in Y\). On the complete space \(\{z\in C(Y):z(y_0)=0\}\), the oscillation is a norm equivalent to the uniform norm. The map \(z\mapsto F(z)-F(z)(y_0)\) is a contraction, hence has a unique fixed point. For this point \(F(z)=z+\kappa\). Set \(v=e^z\), \(u=1/(Kv)\). The two marginal densities are \[ u(x)(Kv)(x)=1,\qquad v(y)(K^\top u)(y)=e^{z(y)}e^{-F(z)(y)}=e^{-\kappa}. \] Integrating both marginals gives \(1=e^{-\kappa}\), hence \(\kappa=0\). This proves existence of the scaling and uniqueness modulo reciprocal constants. For the normalized iteration \(z_{n+1}=F(z_n)-F(z_n)(y_0)\), Banach's estimate gives \[ \|z_n-z_*\|_\infty\le\operatorname{osc}(z_n-z_*)\le q^{2n}\operatorname{osc}(z_0-z_*). \] Thus \(v_n=e^{z_n}\to v_*\) uniformly. The lower bound \(Kv_n\ge k_-\min v_n>0\) uniformly for large \(n\) gives \(u_{n+1}=1/(Kv_n)\to u_*\) uniformly. The unnormalized next column factor is \(v_{n+1}^{\mathrm{raw}}=e^{F(z_n)}\to e^{F(z_*)}=v_*\). Therefore \[ u_{n+1}Kv_n\longrightarrow u_*Kv_*,\qquad u_{n+1}Kv_{n+1}^{\mathrm{raw}}\longrightarrow u_*Kv_* \] uniformly. These are the row-updated and column-updated densities. Dividing \(v_{n+1}^{\mathrm{raw}}\) by a normalization constant and multiplying its paired \(u\)-factor by that same constant leaves the density unchanged.

For matrices, interpret the integrals as sums with weights \(a_i,b_j\). If \(u^{\rm rel},v^{\rm rel}\) denote these relative factors, the absorbed factors in the algorithm are \(u_i=a_i u_i^{\rm rel}\), \(v_j=b_j v_j^{\rm rel}\). The two updates are exactly the fixed-point iteration just proved. Normalization changes neither the matrix nor the iterates' marginal residuals, so the matrices converge to the unique scaling. The contraction constant can approach one when the kernel is poorly conditioned; this proof gives convergence without a uniform small-\(\varepsilon\) iteration bound.

Small-regularization limit

Theorem 10.6 (Zero-temperature limit). As \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), \[ \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu)\longrightarrow\operatorname{OT}_0(\mu,\nu). \] Every weak limit point of \(\pi_\varepsilon\) is an unregularized optimal plan. If several unregularized optimizers exist, \(\pi_\varepsilon\) selects the one minimizing \(\operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\mu\otimes\nu)\), when this selection problem is finite.

Proof. Let \(C_*=\operatorname{OT}_0(\mu,\nu)\), and take an optimal \(\pi_*\). Partition \(X,Y\) into finitely many Borel cells \(A_i,B_j\) of diameter at most \(\delta\), discarding zero-mass cells. With \(a_i=\mu(A_i)\), \(b_j=\nu(B_j)\), \(p_{ij}=\pi_*(A_i\times B_j)\), put \[ \pi^\delta=\sum_{ij}p_{ij}\frac{\mu|_{A_i}}{a_i}\otimes\frac{\nu|_{B_j}}{b_j}. \] For Borel \(E\subset X\), the row sums \(\sum_jp_{ij}=a_i\) give \[ \pi^\delta(E\times Y)=\sum_{ij}p_{ij}\frac{\mu(E\cap A_i)}{a_i} =\sum_i\mu(E\cap A_i)=\mu(E). \] The column calculation gives \(\nu\). Its density on each block is \(p_{ij}/(a_i b_j)\). Hence \[ H_\delta=\operatorname{KL}(\pi^\delta\|\mu\otimes\nu) =\sum_{ij:p_{ij}>0}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{a_i b_j}<\infty. \] The two plans assign the same mass to every block. Writing \(c^-_{ij},c^+_{ij}\) for the infimum and supremum on each block, both cost integrals belong to \([\sum p_{ij}c^-_{ij},\sum p_{ij}c^+_{ij}]\). Thus \[ \left|\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi^\delta-\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi_*\right| \le\sum_{ij}p_{ij}(c^+_{ij}-c^-_{ij})\le\omega_c(2\delta)\sum_{ij}p_{ij}=\omega_c(2\delta). \] Uniform continuity of \(c\) therefore gives \(|\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi^\delta-C_*|\le\omega_c(2\delta)\), where \(\omega_c(r)\to0\). Nonnegativity of entropy and comparison with \(\pi^\delta\) yield \[ C_*\le\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon \le C_*+\omega_c(2\delta)+\varepsilon H_\delta. \] First let \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) for fixed \(\delta\), then \(\delta\downarrow0\). The cost of \(\pi_\varepsilon\) lies between \(C_*\) and \(\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon\), so it converges to \(C_*\). Compactness and continuity of \(c\) make every limit plan optimal.

If an optimal plan \(\widehat\pi\) has finite entropy \(H(\widehat\pi)\), comparison gives \[ C_*+\varepsilon H(\pi_\varepsilon) \le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon+\varepsilon H(\pi_\varepsilon) \le C_*+\varepsilon H(\widehat\pi). \] Thus \(H(\pi_\varepsilon)\le H(\widehat\pi)\). For any convergent subsequence \(\pi_{\varepsilon_k}\Rightarrow\bar\pi\), lower semicontinuity gives \[ H(\bar\pi)\le\liminf_kH(\pi_{\varepsilon_k})\le H(\widehat\pi). \] This holds for every finite-entropy optimal competitor \(\widehat\pi\). Strict convexity of entropy on the convex optimal set makes this selected plan unique; compactness then gives convergence of the full family.

Large-regularization limit

Proposition 10.7 (Independence limit). In the compact setting, \(\pi_\varepsilon\to\mu\otimes\nu\) in total variation and \(\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon\to C_0=\int c\,\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\nu)\) as \(\varepsilon\to\infty\).

Proof. Put \(R=\mu\otimes\nu\), \(C_0=\int c\,\mathrm{d}R\), \(m=\min c\). Comparison with \(R\) gives \[ \int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon+\varepsilon H(\pi_\varepsilon\mid R)\le C_0, \qquad 0\le H(\pi_\varepsilon\mid R)\le\frac{C_0-m}{\varepsilon}\to0. \] Use the convention \(\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\sup_A|P(A)-Q(A)|\). Pinsker's inequality yields \[ \|\pi_\varepsilon-R\|_{\mathrm{TV}} \le\sqrt{H(\pi_\varepsilon\mid R)/2}\to0, \quad \left|\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon-C_0\right| \le2\|c\|_\infty\|\pi_\varepsilon-R\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\to0. \] Nonnegativity of entropy and the comparison bound now sandwich the full objective: \[ \int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon\le\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon\le C_0. \] Both endpoints tend to \(C_0\), so \(\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon\to C_0\). In particular \[ \varepsilon H(\pi_\varepsilon\mid R) =\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon-\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon\to0. \] This establishes convergence of the penalty as well as the plan.

Smooth dependence on the marginals

For fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) and positive kernels, dual potentials are unique modulo constants and vary smoothly with positive discrete marginals. The envelope theorem gives \[ \nabla_a\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon=\varphi_\varepsilon, \qquad \nabla_b\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon=\psi_\varepsilon \] in normalized coordinates. This smoothness is the main statistical and optimization advantage of regularization, but it becomes poorly conditioned as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\).

Log-domain stabilization

When \(\varepsilon\) is small, entries of \(K=e^{-C/\varepsilon}\) may underflow. Writing \(u=e^{f/\varepsilon}\), \(v=e^{g/\varepsilon}\) transforms scaling into log-sum-exp updates: \[ f_i=\varepsilon\log a_i-\varepsilon\log\sum_j \exp\!\left(\frac{g_j-C_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\right). \] Subtracting additive constants from \(f,g\) preserves the plan and prevents numerical overflow. This is the stable realization of the dual gauge invariance.

Regularization bias

\(\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu)\) is generally positive, so the regularized cost is not a distance and is biased toward diffuse couplings. Smaller \(\varepsilon\) reduces geometric bias but slows scaling and increases statistical and numerical sensitivity. The next page studies debiasing and separates the fully discrete and semi-discrete computational geometries.

Exercises

scaling and regularization

  1. Exercise 10.1. For \(a=b=(1/2,1/2)\), \(C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), and \(k=e^{-1/\varepsilon}\), find the entropic optimizer.

    Hint

    Use the symmetry of both marginals and the cost matrix to reduce the unknown scaling factors.

    Solution

    Symmetry and scaling give \(P_\varepsilon=\frac1{2(1+k)}\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\k&1\end{pmatrix}\). It approaches the diagonal plan as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) and the independent plan as \(\varepsilon\to\infty\).

  2. Exercise 10.2. Run one row update and one column update on a positive matrix. Why must you check both marginals before stopping?

    Hint

    Track what the second scaling operation does to the marginal corrected by the first operation.

    Solution

    The column update can disturb the row sums just enforced. A useful stopping residual is \(\|P\mathbf1-a\|_1+\|P^\top\mathbf1-b\|_1\). Small residual alone does not bound the bias from choosing positive \(\varepsilon\).
