Learning goal. Derive Sinkhorn scaling, recognize its entropy convention, and distinguish numerical convergence from regularization bias.

Entropy makes the transport objective strictly convex; in finite dimensions the optimizer also depends smoothly on positive marginal weights. Its optimizer has a multiplicative Gibbs form, and enforcing the two marginals reduces to alternating diagonal scalings. The regularization is therefore simultaneously variational, probabilistic, and algorithmic.

For the continuous results on this page, take compact metric spaces \(X,Y\) and finite continuous cost \(c\). For matrix scaling, discard zero-weight atoms and assume \(a_i,b_j>0\). These hypotheses keep the kernel strictly positive and bounded away from zero; unbounded costs and noncompact spaces need additional integrability assumptions.

The entropic transport problem

Definition. For \(\varepsilon>0\), define \[ \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)} \left\{ \int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi +\varepsilon\operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\mu\otimes\nu) \right\}. \] The reference \(\mu\otimes\nu\) makes the entropy term invariant under changes of coordinates and equal to the mutual information of a coupling.

Existence and uniqueness. Under the compact continuous-cost assumptions above, the entropic problem has a unique minimizer \(\pi_\varepsilon\).

Proof. Compactness of \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), lower semicontinuity of the cost integral, and lower semicontinuity of relative entropy give existence by the direct method. Relative entropy is strictly convex on densities with respect to \(\mu\otimes\nu\), while the cost is linear. Two distinct finite-objective minimizers would have a midpoint with strictly smaller entropy and the same averaged cost, contradicting minimality.

Dual formulation

Entropic duality. Under the assumptions above, \[ \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{\varphi,\psi} \left[ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu -\varepsilon\int \left\{ e^{(\varphi(x)+\psi(y)-c(x,y))/\varepsilon}-1 \right\} \,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y) \right]. \] At optimality, \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon}{\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\nu)}(x,y) = \exp\!\left(\frac{\varphi_\varepsilon(x)+\psi_\varepsilon(y)-c(x,y)}{\varepsilon}\right). \]

Proof. Write \(\pi=r\,\mu\otimes\nu\) and introduce multipliers \(\varphi,\psi\) for the marginals. Pointwise minimization in \(r\ge0\) uses the convex conjugate \[ \sup_{r\ge0}\{sr-\varepsilon(r\log r-r+1)\} =\varepsilon(e^{s/\varepsilon}-1). \] Substituting \(s=\varphi+\psi-c\) gives the dual. Equality in the conjugacy relation gives the exponential density of the optimizer.

The Schrödinger system

Let \(K_\varepsilon(x,y)=e^{-c(x,y)/\varepsilon}\), \(u(x)=e^{\varphi_\varepsilon(x)/\varepsilon}\), and \(v(y)=e^{\psi_\varepsilon(y)/\varepsilon}\). Then \[ \pi_\varepsilon(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y) =u(x)K_\varepsilon(x,y)v(y)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\nu(\mathrm{d}y). \] The marginal constraints become \[ u(x)\int K_\varepsilon(x,y)v(y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)=1, \qquad v(y)\int K_\varepsilon(x,y)u(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=1. \] This pair of nonlinear integral equations is the Schrödinger system.

Discrete matrix scaling

For discrete marginals \(a\in\Delta_m\), \(b\in\Delta_n\), let \(K_{ij}=e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}>0\). Absorbing the marginal weights into scaling vectors, the unique optimizer has form \[ P_\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v), \] with \[ u\odot(Kv)=a,\qquad v\odot(K^\top u)=b. \]

Sinkhorn iterations. Starting from \(v^{(0)}>0\), alternate \[ u^{(k+1)}=a\oslash(Kv^{(k)}), \qquad v^{(k+1)}=b\oslash(K^\top u^{(k+1)}), \] where \(\oslash\) is componentwise division. Every row update enforces the first marginal exactly, and every column update enforces the second.

A practical scaling loop

v = vector of ones
repeat:
    u = a / (K v)             # componentwise division
    v = b / (transpose(K) u)
    P = diag(u) K diag(v)
    residual = norm1(P ones - a) + norm1(transpose(P) ones - b)
until residual is below tolerance or the iteration limit is reached

Check both marginals after a complete sweep. Report the residual and whether the iteration limit was reached; a limit is not a convergence certificate. For small \(\varepsilon\), use the log-domain version below. A dense sweep costs \(O(mn)\) arithmetic and storing the dense kernel costs \(O(mn)\) memory; the number of sweeps depends on the kernel and accuracy.

The continuous factors above were defined relative to \(\mu\otimes\nu\). The discrete vectors here absorb \(a_i,b_j\). If \(f=\varepsilon\log u\), \(g=\varepsilon\log v\) are these absorbed log-scalings, the relative-entropy dual potentials are \(\varphi_i=f_i-\varepsilon\log a_i\), \(\psi_j=g_j-\varepsilon\log b_j\), up to opposite additive constants. This distinction matters when differentiating the value with respect to marginal weights.

Why Sinkhorn converges

Sinkhorn scaling theorem. If \(K\) has strictly positive entries and \(a,b\) are strictly positive probability vectors, there exist positive \(u,v\), unique up to \((u,v)\mapsto(\lambda u,\lambda^{-1}v)\), such that \(\operatorname{diag}(u)K\operatorname{diag}(v)\in U(a,b)\). Sinkhorn iterates converge to this scaled matrix.

Proof outline. In dual coordinates, each update exactly maximizes the strictly concave dual objective in one block of potentials. After fixing the additive gauge, positivity bounds the relevant dual level set. Block ascent and uniqueness then identify the limit; monotonicity alone would not prove convergence. Equivalently, each update is a Kullback--Leibler projection onto one affine marginal set. Alternating Bregman projections converge to the unique intersection minimizer. A quantitative proof uses contraction of the positive kernel in Hilbert's projective metric.

Small-regularization limit

Zero-temperature limit. As \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), \[ \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\nu)\longrightarrow\operatorname{OT}_0(\mu,\nu). \] Every weak limit point of \(\pi_\varepsilon\) is an unregularized optimal plan. If several unregularized optimizers exist, \(\pi_\varepsilon\) selects the one minimizing \(\operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\mu\otimes\nu)\), when this selection problem is finite.

Proof. Since the entropy term is nonnegative, \(\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon\ge\operatorname{OT}_0\). Evaluate the regularized objective at a finite-entropy optimal plan, or use product couplings within increasingly fine partitions to approximate an optimal plan by finite-entropy plans, to obtain the limsup inequality. Uniform continuity of the cost controls the partition approximation error. Compactness gives limit points of \(\pi_\varepsilon\); lower semicontinuity of the cost makes them optimal. Comparing two unregularized optimizers at order \(\varepsilon\) gives the entropy selection rule.

Large-regularization limit

As \(\varepsilon\to\infty\), minimizing mutual information dominates the cost. The unique zero-information coupling is \(\mu\otimes\nu\), so \(\pi_\varepsilon\Rightarrow\mu\otimes\nu\) under mild conditions. Entropic transport therefore interpolates between optimal geometric dependence and independence.

Smooth dependence on the marginals

For fixed \(\varepsilon>0\) and positive kernels, dual potentials are unique modulo constants and vary smoothly with positive discrete marginals. The envelope theorem gives \[ \nabla_a\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon=\varphi_\varepsilon, \qquad \nabla_b\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon=\psi_\varepsilon \] in normalized coordinates. This smoothness is the main statistical and optimization advantage of regularization, but it becomes poorly conditioned as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\).

Log-domain stabilization

When \(\varepsilon\) is small, entries of \(K=e^{-C/\varepsilon}\) may underflow. Writing \(u=e^{f/\varepsilon}\), \(v=e^{g/\varepsilon}\) transforms scaling into log-sum-exp updates: \[ f_i=\varepsilon\log a_i-\varepsilon\log\sum_j \exp\!\left(\frac{g_j-C_{ij}}{\varepsilon}\right). \] Subtracting additive constants from \(f,g\) preserves the plan and prevents numerical overflow. This is the stable realization of the dual gauge invariance.

Regularization bias

\(\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu,\mu)\) is generally positive, so the regularized cost is not a distance and is biased toward diffuse couplings. Smaller \(\varepsilon\) reduces geometric bias but slows scaling and increases statistical and numerical sensitivity. The next page studies debiasing and separates the fully discrete and semi-discrete computational geometries.

Sources and proof details. Peyré–Cuturi, Computational optimal transport, Chapter 4, for entropy conventions, convergence proofs for matrix scaling, and stable algorithms.

Practice: scaling and regularization

  1. For \(a=b=(1/2,1/2)\), \(C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), and \(k=e^{-1/\varepsilon}\), find the entropic optimizer.

    Hint and answer

    Symmetry and scaling give \(P_\varepsilon=\frac1{2(1+k)}\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\k&1\end{pmatrix}\). It approaches the diagonal plan as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) and the independent plan as \(\varepsilon\to\infty\).

  2. Run one row update and one column update on a positive matrix. Why must you check both marginals before stopping?

    Hint and answer

    The column update can disturb the row sums just enforced. A useful stopping residual is \(\|P\mathbf1-a\|_1+\|P^\top\mathbf1-b\|_1\). Small residual alone does not bound the bias from choosing positive \(\varepsilon\).
