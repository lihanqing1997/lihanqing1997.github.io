Learning goal. Turn an entropy bound into a transport bound and concentration for Lipschitz observables.

Transport inequalities convert information divergence into geometric distance. Through Kantorovich duality they become exponential bounds for Lipschitz observables, and through gradient flows they connect entropy, Fisher information, curvature, and concentration.

Relative entropy and transport inequalities

Definition. For \(\nu,\mu\in\mathcal P(X)\), \[ \operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\int\log\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right)\mathrm{d}\nu, &\nu\ll\mu,\\ +\infty,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \] A measure \(\mu\) satisfies \(T_1(C)\) if \[ W_1(\nu,\mu)\le\sqrt{2C\,\operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu)} \] for every \(\nu\), and satisfies \(T_2(C)\) if \[ W_2^2(\nu,\mu)\le2C\,\operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu). \]

Since \(W_1\le W_2\), \(T_2(C)\) implies \(T_1(C)\). Pinsker's inequality, \[ \|\nu-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2\le\frac12\operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu), \] is an analogous comparison in total variation, but it does not encode the geometry of the underlying space.

Entropy variational formula

Donsker--Varadhan formula. For \(\nu\ll\mu\), \[ \operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu) =\sup_f\left\{\int f\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\log\int e^f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right\}, \] where the supremum may be taken over bounded measurable \(f\).

Proof. Write \(h=\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}\mu\). For bounded measurable \(f\), the probability \(\mu_f=e^f\mu/\mu(e^f)\) satisfies \[ 0\le\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\mu_f) =\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\mu)-\nu f+\log\mu(e^f), \] with the inequality still valid when the entropy is infinite. Equivalently, \[ \nu f-\log\mu(e^f)\le H(\nu\mid\mu)\quad(\forall f\text{ bounded measurable}), \] so the supremum is at most the entropy. For the reverse bound set \(f_M=\max(-M,\min(\log h,M))\), with \(\log0=-\infty\). Then \(e^{f_M}\to h\) \(\mu\)-almost everywhere and \(e^{f_M}\le h+1\); dominated convergence gives \(\mu(e^{f_M})\to1\). On \(h\le1\), \(h|\log h|\le1/e\), so the negative parts of \(f_M\) converge in \(L^1(\nu)\). On \(h>1\), their positive parts increase to \(\log h\), so monotone convergence applies, also when the result is infinite. Explicitly, \[ \nu f_M=\int_{h>1}h\min(\log h,M)\,\mathrm{d}\mu -\int_{0<h\le1}h\min(|\log h|,M)\,\mathrm{d}\mu \longrightarrow\int h\log h\,\mathrm{d}\mu. \] The first integral uses monotone convergence, the second dominated convergence; no \(\infty-\infty\) occurs because the negative part is at most \(1/e\). Since \(\log\mu(e^{f_M})\to\log1=0\), \[ \sup_f\{\nu f-\log\mu(e^f)\}\ge\lim_M\{\nu f_M-\log\mu(e^{f_M})\}=H(\nu\mid\mu). \] This is the reverse bound, including when the entropy is infinite.

\(T_1\) and sub-Gaussian observables

Bobkov--Götze characterization. For \(C>0\) and \(\mu\in\mathcal P_1(X)\), the measure \(\mu\) satisfies \(T_1(C)\) if and only if every \(1\)-Lipschitz \(f\) satisfies \[ \log\int e^{\lambda(f-\mu f)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu \le\frac{C\lambda^2}{2} \qquad\text{for every }\lambda\in\mathbb R. \]

Proof. For bounded measurable \(g\), the same tilted-law calculation gives the Gibbs formula \[ \log\mu(e^g)=\sup_\nu\{\nu g-\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\mu)\}; \] indeed, for \(\mu_g=e^g\mu/\mu(e^g)\), \[ \nu g-H(\nu\mid\mu)=\log\mu(e^g)-H(\nu\mid\mu_g)\le\log\mu(e^g), \] and equality holds at \(\nu=\mu_g\). If \(\mu\in\mathcal P_1\), boundedness of \(g\) gives \(\mu_g\in\mathcal P_1\), so the supremum can be restricted to \(\nu\in\mathcal P_1\).

Assume \(T_1(C)\) and let \(f\) be bounded and \(1\)-Lipschitz. For every finite-entropy \(\nu\in\mathcal P_1\), Kantorovich–Rubinstein and \(T_1\) give \(|\nu f-\mu f|\le\sqrt{2CH}\), where \(H=\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\mu)\). Therefore \[ \log\mu e^{\lambda(f-\mu f)} \le\sup_{H\ge0}\{|\lambda|\sqrt{2CH}-H\} =\frac{C\lambda^2}{2}. \] The scalar maximization follows by putting \(z=\sqrt H\): \[ |\lambda|\sqrt{2C}\,z-z^2 =\frac{C\lambda^2}{2}-\left(z-|\lambda|\sqrt{C/2}\right)^2 \le\frac{C\lambda^2}{2}. \] For an unbounded Lipschitz \(f\), use \(f_R=\max(-R,\min(f,R))\). It is still \(1\)-Lipschitz, and \(\mu f_R\to\mu f\) by \(\mu\in\mathcal P_1\). Since \(|f_R|\le|f|\in L^1(\mu)\), dominated convergence gives the convergence of the means, and Fatou gives \[ \mu e^{\lambda(f-\mu f)} \le\liminf_R\mu e^{\lambda(f_R-\mu f_R)}\le e^{C\lambda^2/2}. \]

Conversely, the entropy variational inequality gives, for \(\lambda>0\), \[ \nu f-\mu f\le\frac{H}{\lambda}+\frac{C\lambda}{2}. \] For \(0<H<\infty\), choose \(\lambda=\sqrt{2H/C}\); for \(H=0\), let \(\lambda\downarrow0\). Taking the supremum over bounded \(1\)-Lipschitz tests gives \(W_1\le\sqrt{2CH}\), using truncations in Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality. If a finite-entropy competitor's first moment is not initially known, apply the same inequality to \(f_R=\min(d(x,x_0),R)\) first; the estimate with \(\lambda=1\) gives \[ \nu f_R\le\mu f_R+H+C/2\le\mu d(x,x_0)+H+C/2. \] Monotone convergence as \(R\uparrow\infty\) proves \(\nu d(x,x_0)<\infty\). Dominated convergence now permits truncation of any Lipschitz \(f\), and Kantorovich–Rubinstein applies. Infinite entropy makes the assertion vacuous.

Concentration. Under \(T_1(C)\), every \(1\)-Lipschitz \(f\) satisfies \[ \mu(f-\mu f\ge r)\le e^{-r^2/(2C)}. \] For \(r\ge0\) and \(\lambda>0\), Markov's inequality and the exponential-moment bound give \[ \mu(f-\mu f\ge r)\le e^{-\lambda r}\mu e^{\lambda(f-\mu f)} \le\exp(-\lambda r+C\lambda^2/2). \] For \(r>0\), choose \(\lambda=r/C\), obtaining \(\exp(-r^2/(2C))\). For \(r=0\), the bound is simply \(\mu(f-\mu f\ge0)\le1\).

Talagrand's Gaussian \(T_2\) inequality

Gaussian transport inequality. For the standard Gaussian measure \(\gamma=N(0,I_d)\) and every \(\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\), \[ W_2^2(\nu,\gamma)\le2\operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\gamma). \]

Proof. We give a complete proof for \(\nu=T_\#\gamma\), where \(T=\operatorname{id}+\nabla\chi\), \(\chi\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^d)\), and \(I+D^2\chi\) is positive definite everywhere. Then \(T\) is a global smooth diffeomorphism (it is strongly monotone and equals the identity outside a compact set), is a convex gradient, and is optimal. Its image density \(f\) relative to \(\gamma\) is positive and smooth. All nonzero integrands below are supported in a compact set, which justifies the integrations by parts. A proof for all finite-entropy laws, using the already stated displacement-convexity theorem, follows after this calculation. The Jacobian identity gives \[ f(T(x))e^{-|T(x)|^2/2}\det DT(x)=e^{-|x|^2/2}, \] hence \[ \log f(T(x)) =\frac{|T(x)|^2-|x|^2}{2}-\log\det DT(x). \] For the positive eigenvalues \(a_1(x),\ldots,a_d(x)\) of \(DT(x)\), \[ \log\det DT(x)=\sum_{j=1}^d\log a_j(x) \le\sum_{j=1}^d(a_j(x)-1)=\operatorname{tr}(DT(x)-I). \] Thus \[ \log\det DT\le\operatorname{tr}(DT-I). \] Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\gamma) &=\int\log f(T(x))\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x)\\ &\ge\frac12\int|T-x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\gamma +\int x\cdot(T-x)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma -\int\operatorname{tr}(DT-I)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma. \end{aligned} \] Write \(b=T-\operatorname{id}=\nabla\chi\), which is compactly supported, and let \(g\) be the Gaussian density. Since \(\partial_jg=-x_jg\), ordinary integration by parts gives \[ \int x_jb_jg\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int b_j\partial_jg\,\mathrm{d}x =\int(\partial_jb_j)g\,\mathrm{d}x. \] Summing over \(j\) gives \[ \int x\cdot(T-x)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma =\int\operatorname{div}(T-x)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma =\int\operatorname{tr}(DT-I)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma. \] The last two terms cancel. Since \(T\) is optimal, \[ \operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\gamma) \ge\frac12\int|T-x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\gamma =\frac12W_2^2(\nu,\gamma). \]

Proof for all \(\nu\in\mathcal P_2\), using displacement convexity. If \(H(\nu)=\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\gamma)=+\infty\), the assertion is immediate. Otherwise \(\nu\) has a density and its entropy is well defined, since \(\nu\in\mathcal P_2\). Write \[ H(\eta)=\operatorname{Ent}(\eta)+\tfrac12\int|x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\eta+\tfrac d2\log(2\pi). \] McCann's theorem from the displacement-convexity chapter makes the first term displacement convex, and the exact quadratic potential calculation makes the second term displacement \(1\)-convex. Hence, along the geodesic from \(\gamma\) to \(\nu\), \[ 0\le H(\nu_t)\le tH(\nu)-\tfrac12t(1-t)W_2^2(\gamma,\nu), \] using \(H(\gamma)=0\) and nonnegativity of relative entropy. Rearranging and dividing by \(t>0\) gives \[ H(\nu)\ge\frac{H(\nu_t)}t+\frac{1-t}{2}W_2^2(\gamma,\nu) \ge\frac{1-t}{2}W_2^2(\gamma,\nu). \] The distance is finite because both laws lie in \(\mathcal P_2\). Taking \(t\downarrow0\) yields \(H(\nu)\ge W_2^2(\gamma,\nu)/2\). The external input is precisely the general McCann theorem already stated; the smooth proof above separately explains the cancellation behind it.

Logarithmic Sobolev inequality and \(T_2\)

Otto–Villani implication (external theorem on Euclidean space). For a probability law \(\mu=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\) with smooth positive density, if \(\mu\) satisfies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality \[ \operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu) \le\frac C2 I(\nu\,\|\,\mu), \] where \[ I(\nu\,\|\,\mu)=\int\left|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right|^2\,\mathrm{d}\nu, \] then \(\mu\) satisfies \(T_2(C)\).

Proof of the flow criterion. The precise input needed from entropy-flow theory is a curve \(\nu_t\), continuous at \(t=0\), with \(E(t)=\operatorname{KL}(\nu_t\|\mu)\) locally absolutely continuous on \((0,\infty)\), continuous at zero with \(E(0)<\infty\), \[ -E'(t)=I(t),\qquad |\dot\nu_t|\le\sqrt{I(t)},\qquad E(t)\le CI(t)/2. \] Under these assumptions we prove the implication completely. The inequalities imply \(E'+2E/C\le0\). Hence \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(e^{2t/C}E(t))\le0, \qquad E(t)\le E(s)e^{-2(t-s)/C}\quad(0<s<t). \] Continuity of \(E\) at zero gives \(E(t)\le E(0)e^{-2t/C}\). On any interval where \(E>0\), \[ \sqrt{I(t)}\le\sqrt{C/2}\,\frac{I(t)}{\sqrt{E(t)}} =-\sqrt{2C}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sqrt{E(t)}. \] Thus, for \(0<s<t\) while \(E\) is positive, \[ \int_s^t|\dot\nu_r|\,\mathrm{d}r \le\int_s^t\sqrt{I(r)}\,\mathrm{d}r \le\sqrt{2C}(\sqrt{E(s)}-\sqrt{E(t)}). \] First let \(s\downarrow0\), using continuity of \(E\) and monotone convergence of the nonnegative integrals, then let \(t\uparrow\infty\). If \(E\) reaches zero, the law is \(\mu\) and the estimate continues with zero remaining length. We obtain \[ \int_0^\infty|\dot\nu_t|\,\mathrm{d}t\le\sqrt{2CE(0)}. \] For \(t>s\), the same bound gives \[ W_2(\nu_s,\nu_t)\le\int_s^t|\dot\nu_r|\,\mathrm{d}r\le\sqrt{2CE(s)}\to0\quad(s\to\infty). \] Completeness yields a \(W_2\)-limit \(\eta\). But Pinsker gives \(\|\nu_t-\mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le\sqrt{E(t)/2}\to0\). Both imply weak convergence, whose limit is unique, so \(\eta=\mu\). Consequently \(W_2(\nu_0,\mu)\le\sqrt{2CE(0)}\). Constructing the entropy flow and extending this argument to every finite-entropy initial law are the external ingredients in the general Otto–Villani theorem. The displayed length argument does not assume \(W_2\)-convergence to equilibrium in advance.

The HWI inequality

HWI inequality (general external theorem). Let \(\mu=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\), with \(V\in C^2\), \(0<Z<\infty\), and \(D^2V\ge\kappa I\). For \(\nu\in\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\) with finite relative entropy and finite Fisher information (defined using the weak gradient), \[ \operatorname{KL}(\nu\,\|\,\mu) \le W_2(\nu,\mu)\sqrt{I(\nu\,\|\,\mu)} -\frac{\kappa}{2}W_2^2(\nu,\mu). \]

Derivation when the first variation is justified. Put \(H(\eta)=\operatorname{KL}(\eta\|\mu)\), \(D=W_2(\nu,\mu)\), and let \(T\) be the optimal map from \(\nu\) to \(\mu\). Assume \(\nu\) has a positive smooth density, finite Fisher information, and the geodesic \(\nu_t=((1-t)\operatorname{id}+tT)_\#\nu\) admits the differentiation and integration by parts \[ \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}H(\nu_t)\right|_{t=0+} =\int\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\cdot(T-x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu. \tag{1} \] These are explicit smooth-calculus hypotheses, not an assertion for arbitrary laws. Displacement \(\kappa\)-convexity and \(H(\mu)=0\) give \[ \frac{H(\nu_t)-H(\nu)}t\le-H(\nu)-\frac\kappa2(1-t)D^2. \] Starting from \[ H(\nu_t)\le(1-t)H(\nu)+tH(\mu)-\tfrac\kappa2t(1-t)D^2, \] subtract \(H(\nu)\) and divide by \(t>0\), obtaining the displayed difference quotient. Its limit exists by the explicit first-variation assumption (1). Let \(t\downarrow0\) and use (1). Rearranging and applying Cauchy–Schwarz yields \[ H(\nu)\le-\int\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\cdot(T-x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\frac\kappa2D^2 \le\sqrt{I(\nu\|\mu)}D-\frac\kappa2D^2. \] Here the Cauchy–Schwarz bound is \[ \left|\int\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\cdot(T-x)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right| \le\left(\int\left|\nabla\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right|^2\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{1/2} \left(\int|T-x|^2\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{1/2} =\sqrt I\,D. \] Both factors are finite by hypothesis. The general HWI theorem replaces (1) by the entropy subgradient inequality; see the transport-inequality reference below.

Tensorization

If each \(\mu_i\) satisfies \(T_2(C_i)\) on \(X_i\), then the product measure satisfies \(T_2(\max_iC_i)\) for the squared product metric. The proof disintegrates a competitor coordinate by coordinate, applies the one-coordinate inequalities conditionally, and uses the chain rule for relative entropy. This dimension-free tensorization is a central reason transport inequalities yield concentration for high-dimensional product measures.

Proof for two factors, then induction. Disintegrate a finite-entropy competitor as \(\nu(\mathrm{d}x_1,\mathrm{d}x_2)=\nu_1(\mathrm{d}x_1)\nu_{x_1}(\mathrm{d}x_2)\). Couple \(X_1\sim\nu_1\) to \(Y_1\sim\mu_1\) optimally. Given \(X_1=x_1\), couple \(X_2\sim\nu_{x_1}\) to \(Y_2\sim\mu_2\) optimally, independently of \(Y_1\). A measurable choice of conditional optimal plans exists on Polish spaces; equivalently use measurable nearly optimal plans and let their error decrease to zero. If \(\pi_1(\mathrm{d}x_1,\mathrm{d}y_1)\) is the first coupling and \(Q_{x_1}(\mathrm{d}x_2,\mathrm{d}y_2)\) the conditional coupling, the joint law is \(\Lambda=\pi_1Q_{x_1}\). Its \(X\)-marginal is \(\nu_1\nu_{x_1}=\nu\). For Borel \(A_1,A_2\), \[ \Lambda(Y_1\in A_1,Y_2\in A_2) =\int\mathbf1_{A_1}(y_1)\underbrace{Q_{x_1}(X_2\times A_2)}_{\mu_2(A_2)}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_1 =\mu_1(A_1)\mu_2(A_2). \] Thus its \(Y\)-marginal is the product law. Thus \[ \begin{aligned} W_2^2(\nu,\mu_1\otimes\mu_2) &\le W_2^2(\nu_1,\mu_1)+\int W_2^2(\nu_{x_1},\mu_2)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_1(x_1)\\ &\le2C_1H(\nu_1\|\mu_1)+2C_2\int H(\nu_{x_1}\|\mu_2)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_1(x_1)\\ &\le2\max(C_1,C_2)H(\nu\|\mu_1\otimes\mu_2), \end{aligned} \] To check the entropy step, write \(h=\mathrm{d}\nu/\mathrm{d}(\mu_1\otimes\mu_2)\), \(h_1(x_1)=\int h(x_1,x_2)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_2\). For \(h_1>0\), the conditional density is \(h_{x_1}=h/h_1\), and \(\log h=\log h_1+\log h_{x_1}\) \(\nu\)-almost everywhere. Integration gives \[ H(\nu\mid\mu_1\otimes\mu_2) =\int h_1\log h_1\,\mathrm{d}\mu_1 +\int h_1(x_1)\left[\int h_{x_1}\log h_{x_1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_2\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mu_1 =H(\nu_1\mid\mu_1)+\int H(\nu_{x_1}\mid\mu_2)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_1. \] Each negative density-log-density part is bounded in integral by \(1/e\); truncation and monotone convergence of positive parts justify the identity also in the extended-value sense. If the entropy is infinite the bound is immediate. Repeating the two-factor argument proves the finite-product statement.

Sources and proof details. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport, §3, for the gradient-flow framework, and Villani, Optimal transport: old and new, for transport inequalities. The Gaussian inequality is proved using the stated McCann theorem; general Otto–Villani and HWI extensions are external results; their precise smooth calculations and the complete conditional flow argument are given above.

Practice: constants and sharpness

  1. Check Gaussian \(T_2\) for \(\nu=N(a,I)\) against \(\gamma=N(0,I)\).

    Hint and answer

    \(W_2^2=|a|^2\) and \(\operatorname{KL}(\nu\|\gamma)=|a|^2/2\). Equality shows the displayed constant is sharp.

  2. Given \(\log\mathbb E e^{\lambda(f-\mathbb Ef)}\le C\lambda^2/2\), derive the upper-tail bound.

    Hint and answer

    Markov gives \(\exp(-\lambda r+C\lambda^2/2)\). Choosing \(\lambda=r/C\) gives \(\exp(-r^2/(2C))\).
