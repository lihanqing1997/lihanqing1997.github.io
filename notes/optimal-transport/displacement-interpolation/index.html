Learning goal. Construct a geodesic from a coupling and distinguish displacement from mixing densities.

Linear interpolation of measures creates and destroys mass at fixed locations. Wasserstein interpolation instead follows particles along shortest paths. This produces the intrinsic geodesics of the probability-measure space.

Constant-speed geodesics

Definition. A curve \(t\mapsto\mu_t\) in a metric space is a constant-speed geodesic from \(\mu_0\) to \(\mu_1\) if \[ W_p(\mu_s,\mu_t)=|t-s|W_p(\mu_0,\mu_1) \] for all \(s,t\in[0,1]\).

Assume first that \(X=\mathbb R^d\). If \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu_0,\mu_1)\), define \[ e_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty,\qquad \mu_t=(e_t)_\#\pi. \] Each pair \((x,y)\) is moved along the Euclidean segment joining its endpoints.

Displacement interpolation theorem. If \(\pi\) is \(p\)-optimal between \(\mu_0,\mu_1\), then \[ \mu_t=((1-t)\operatorname{pr}_1+t\operatorname{pr}_2)_\#\pi \] is a constant-speed \(W_p\)-geodesic.

Proof. Put \(F_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty\), \(D=W_p(\mu_0,\mu_1)\). The law \(\gamma_{s,t}=(F_s,F_t)_\#\pi\) has marginals \(\mu_s,\mu_t\), and \[ \begin{aligned} W_p(\mu_s,\mu_t)^p &\le\int|F_s(x,y)-F_t(x,y)|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi\\ &=|t-s|^p\int|x-y|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi =|t-s|^pD^p. \end{aligned} \] For \(0\le s\le t\le1\), triangle inequality gives the matching lower bound: \[ \begin{aligned} W_p(\mu_s,\mu_t) &\ge D-W_p(\mu_0,\mu_s)-W_p(\mu_t,\mu_1)\\ &\ge D-sD-(1-t)D=(t-s)D. \end{aligned} \] Combining bounds yields \(W_p(\mu_s,\mu_t)=|t-s|D\); symmetry handles \(s>t\).

Interpolation induced by a map

Corollary. If the optimal plan is induced by \(T\), then \[ \mu_t=T_{t\#}\mu_0,\qquad T_t=(1-t)\operatorname{id}+tT. \] For quadratic transport with \(\mu_0\) absolutely continuous, Brenier's theorem makes this the unique geodesic whenever the optimal plan is unique.

Translation. If \(T(x)=x+a\), then \(\mu_t=(x\mapsto x+ta)_\#\mu_0\). The entire distribution moves rigidly at constant speed.

Geodesic base spaces and dynamical plans

Definition. Let \(\operatorname{Geo}(X)\) be the set of constant-speed minimizing geodesics \(\gamma:[0,1]\to X\), and let \(e_t(\gamma)=\gamma_t\). A probability measure \(\boldsymbol\pi\) on \(\operatorname{Geo}(X)\) is a dynamical optimal plan if \[ (e_0,e_1)_\#\boldsymbol\pi \] is an optimal coupling of its endpoint marginals.

Geodesic-space theorem. If \(X\) is a complete separable geodesic space, then \((\mathcal P_p(X),W_p)\) is geodesic. Every dynamical optimal plan generates a geodesic by \[ \mu_t=(e_t)_\#\boldsymbol\pi. \] For \(1<p<\infty\), every \(W_p\)-geodesic admits such a representation. This converse is not asserted for \(p=1\): mixture curves can also be \(W_1\)-geodesics.

Proof. The measurable-selection theorem for Polish spaces supplies a universally measurable choice of a minimizing geodesic for each endpoint pair: \(\operatorname{Geo}(X)\) is a closed subset of \(C([0,1];X)\), and its continuous endpoint map is onto \(X\times X\). Universal measurability suffices after completing the endpoint coupling. If \(S(x,y)\) denotes this choice and \(\pi\) is an optimal endpoint coupling, define \(\boldsymbol\pi=S_\#\pi\). Then \[ (e_0,e_1)_\#\boldsymbol\pi=\pi,\qquad \int d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))^p\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\pi =|t-s|^p\int d(x,y)^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] This is the required dynamical plan. For \(D=W_p(\mu_0,\mu_1)\), its marginals satisfy \(W_p(\mu_s,\mu_t)\le(t-s)D\). Adding the bounds on \([0,s],[s,t],[t,1]\) and using the endpoint triangle inequality forces equality, proving existence.

For the converse, assume \(p>1\) and take a given constant-speed geodesic. At dyadic times \(t_i=i2^{-n}\), glue optimal successive couplings to a joint law of \(X_i\). Let \(A_i=d(X_{i-1},X_i)\). Then \[ D\le\|d(X_0,X_{2^n})\|_p\le\left\|\sum_iA_i\right\|_p \le\sum_i\|A_i\|_p=D. \] All inequalities are equalities. Equality in the pointwise triangle inequality gives \(d(X_0,X_{2^n})=\sum_iA_i\) almost surely. Put \(N=2^n\). If \(D>0\), all \(\|A_i\|_p=D/N\). Equality in Minkowski and strict convexity of the \(L^p\) unit ball imply \[ \frac{A_i}{\|A_i\|_p}=\frac{A_j}{\|A_j\|_p}\quad\text{a.s. for all }i,j. \] Indeed, a convex average of distinct unit vectors has norm strictly less than one. Thus all \(A_i\) are equal; since their sum is \(d(X_0,X_N)\), \[ A_i=N^{-1}d(X_0,X_N)\quad\text{a.s.} \] For \(i<j\), upper and lower triangle bounds give \[ \begin{aligned} d(X_i,X_j)&\le\sum_{r=i+1}^j A_r,\\ d(X_i,X_j)&\ge d(X_0,X_N)-\sum_{r=1}^iA_r-\sum_{r=j+1}^NA_r =\sum_{r=i+1}^jA_r. \end{aligned} \] Substitution yields \[ d(X_i,X_j)=|t_i-t_j|\,d(X_0,X_{2^n}) \] for every pair of dyadic indices. The endpoint coupling is optimal as well. If \(D=0\), all these variables coincide and the conclusion is immediate.

To pass to all dyadic times, view each finite joint law as a law on the countable product indexed by all dyadic times, filling missing coordinates with independent variables of their prescribed marginals. These laws are tight: for a given \(\delta>0\), choose a compact set for the \(k\)-th coordinate with failure probability at most \(\delta2^{-k}\), and use the compact product and the union bound. If \(\mathbb D=\{s_1,s_2,\ldots\}\) lists the dyadic times and \(K_k\subset X\) has \(\mu_{s_k}(K_k^c)\le\delta2^{-k}\), then every product-space law \(P_n\) satisfies \[ P_n\left(\left(\prod_kK_k\right)^c\right)\le\sum_kP_n(X_{s_k}\notin K_k) \le\sum_k\delta2^{-k}=\delta. \] The countable compact product is compact. Prokhorov therefore gives a subsequence \(P_{n_j}\Rightarrow P\). Each finite distance equality is a closed constraint and holds for all sufficiently fine grids, so Portmanteau preserves it. The endpoint cost is at most \(D^p\) by lower semicontinuity and at least \(D^p\) by its marginals, hence remains optimal. Almost every dyadic sample is therefore a constant-speed isometry, scaled by its endpoint distance. Completeness of \(X\) extends it uniquely to a continuous geodesic. Evaluation at dyadic times makes this extension measurable into \(C([0,1];X)\); its path law is the required dynamical plan. For \(s_k\in\mathbb D\) with \(s_k\to t\), every lifted path has \(\gamma(s_k)\to\gamma(t)\). For bounded continuous \(f\), dominated convergence gives \[ \int f(\gamma(t))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\pi =\lim_k\int f(\gamma(s_k))\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol\pi =\lim_k\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{s_k}=\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_t, \] where the last step uses \(W_p\)-continuity of the given curve. This identifies every time marginal.

Displacement versus mixture interpolation

The ordinary convex mixture \[ \bar\mu_t=(1-t)\mu_0+t\mu_1 \] leaves part of the mass at the source and creates the remainder at the target. By contrast, displacement interpolation locates the mass between its endpoints.

Two Dirac masses. For \(\mu_0=\delta_0\), \(\mu_1=\delta_1\), \[ \bar\mu_t=(1-t)\delta_0+t\delta_1, \qquad \mu_t=\delta_t. \] Only the second curve describes one particle moving continuously from \(0\) to \(1\).

Gaussian geodesics

Proposition. For nondegenerate Gaussian measures, let \(T(x)=m_1+A(x-m_0)\) be the Brenier map from Quadratic transport and Brenier's theorem. Then the \(W_2\)-geodesic is Gaussian: \[ \mu_t=N(m_t,\Sigma_t), \] where \[ m_t=(1-t)m_0+tm_1,\qquad \Sigma_t=((1-t)I+tA)\Sigma_0((1-t)I+tA)^\top. \]

Proof. Let \(X=m_0+Z\), where \(\mathbb EZ=0\), \(\mathbb EZZ^\top=\Sigma_0\), and set \(B_t=(1-t)I+tA\). Then \[ T_t(X)=(1-t)(m_0+Z)+t(m_1+AZ) =\underbrace{(1-t)m_0+tm_1}_{m_t}+B_tZ. \] This affine image is Gaussian, and its mean and covariance are \[ \mathbb ET_t(X)=m_t+B_t\mathbb EZ=m_t, \qquad \mathbb E[(T_t(X)-m_t)(T_t(X)-m_t)^\top] =B_t\mathbb E[ZZ^\top]B_t^\top=B_t\Sigma_0B_t^\top. \] The displacement interpolation theorem identifies this family as the \(W_2\)-geodesic.

Uniqueness and branching

A unique endpoint coupling and unique base-space geodesics imply a unique displacement interpolation. Nonuniqueness can arise either from several optimal couplings or from branching geodesics in \(X\). In \(\mathbb R^d\) with quadratic cost and absolutely continuous source, Brenier's theorem removes the first ambiguity and straight lines remove the second. On branching metric spaces, the same mass can follow several shortest paths.

Curvature visible in probability space

Although Euclidean particles travel along straight lines, \(\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R^d)\) is not a Hilbert space when \(d\ge2\). Distances between displacement geodesics reflect the interaction of their transport maps. This nonlinear geometry is precisely why ordinary convexity must later be replaced by displacement convexity.

Sources and proof details. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport, §2.2, for dynamical plans and the \(p>1\) converse representation.

Practice: moving mass versus mixing it

  1. For endpoints \(\delta_0,\delta_1\), compute the distance along the mixture curve for \(W_1\) and \(W_2\).

    Hint and answer

    Between times \(s,t\), mass \(|t-s|\) crosses distance one. Thus \(W_1=|t-s|\), but \(W_2=\sqrt{|t-s|}\). The mixture is a \(W_1\)-geodesic, not a constant-speed \(W_2\)-geodesic.

  2. Find the midpoint between \(N(0,1)\) and \(N(0,9)\) in \(W_2\).

    Hint and answer

    Interpolate standard deviations, not variances: the midpoint is \(N(0,4)\). An equal density mixture is not Gaussian.
