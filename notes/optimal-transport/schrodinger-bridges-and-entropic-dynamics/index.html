Learning goal. Reduce a path-space entropy problem to its endpoints and distinguish diffusion drift from current velocity.

A Schrödinger bridge asks for the most likely random evolution connecting two unlikely endpoint distributions under a reference diffusion. It is a path-space entropy projection whose endpoint reduction is entropic transport and whose zero-noise limit is classical optimal transport.

The path-space problem

Definition 20.1 (The path-space problem). Let \(R^\varepsilon\) be a reference Markov law on path space \(\Omega=C([0,1];\mathbb R^d)\), typically Brownian motion with generator \(\tfrac\varepsilon2\Delta\). For prescribed endpoints \(\mu_0,\mu_1\), the Schrödinger problem is \[ \inf_{P\in\mathcal P(\Omega)} \left\{ \operatorname{KL}(P\,\|\,R^\varepsilon): P_0=\mu_0,\ P_1=\mu_1 \right\}, \] where \(P_t=(e_t)_\#P\).

The minimizer changes the reference dynamics as little as possible in relative entropy while enforcing both endpoint laws. It is not conditioned on two fixed points, but on two full marginal distributions.

Reduction to a static entropy projection

Theorem 20.2 (Static reduction). Let \(R^\varepsilon_{01}\) be the joint endpoint law of the reference process. Then \[ \inf_{\substack{P:P_0=\mu_0\\P_1=\mu_1}} \operatorname{KL}(P\,\|\,R^\varepsilon) = \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu_0,\mu_1)} \operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,R^\varepsilon_{01}). \] If \(\pi^\star\) solves the static problem with finite value, the dynamic minimizer is \[ P^\star(\mathrm{d}\omega) =\int R^\varepsilon(\mathrm{d}\omega\mid X_0=x,X_1=y) \,\pi^\star(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y). \]

Proof. Write \(R=R^\varepsilon\), \(Z=(X_0,X_1)\). First suppose \(P\ll R\), with density \(H\). Disintegrate \(R=R^zR_{01}(\mathrm{d}z)\) and define \[ h(z)=\int H(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}R^z(\omega),\qquad k_z(\omega)=H(\omega)/h(z)\quad\text{when }h(z)>0. \] Then \(P_{01}=hR_{01}\), \(P^z=k_zR^z\), because for bounded measurable \(a,b\), \[ \int a(Z)b(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}P =\int a(z)\left[\int b(\omega)H(\omega)\,\mathrm{d}R^z\right]\,\mathrm{d}R_{01}(z) =\int a(z)\left[\int b\,\mathrm{d}P^z\right]\,\mathrm{d}P_{01}(z). \] The set \(h=0\) is \(P_{01}\)-null, so arbitrary conditional definitions there do not matter. On a \(P\)-full set, \(H=h(Z)k_Z\), hence \(\log H=\log h(Z)+\log k_Z\). Conditional integration yields \[ \begin{aligned} H(P\mid R) &=\int\log H\,\mathrm{d}P\\ &=\int\log h\,\mathrm{d}P_{01}+\int\left[\int\log k_z\,\mathrm{d}P^z\right]\,\mathrm{d}P_{01}(z)\\ &=H(P_{01}\mid R_{01})+\int H(P^z\mid R^z)\,\mathrm{d}P_{01}(z). \end{aligned} \] The negative parts are integrable: \(\int_{h<1}h|\log h|\,\mathrm{d}R_{01}\le1/e\), and \(\int_{k_z<1}k_z|\log k_z|\,\mathrm{d}R^z\le1/e\) for each \(z\). Truncation and monotone convergence of positive parts therefore justify the identity even when the entropy is infinite. If \(P\not\ll R\), its infinite value cannot improve any finite competitor; the lower inequality also follows from entropy monotonicity under the endpoint map.

Since conditional entropies are nonnegative, every dynamic competitor satisfies \(H(P\mid R)\ge H(P_{01}\mid R_{01})\). Conversely, for an endpoint law \(\pi=hR_{01}\) of finite entropy, define \[ P_\pi(\mathrm{d}\omega)=\int R^z(\mathrm{d}\omega)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}z). \] Its endpoint law is \(\pi\), its density relative to \(R\) is \(h(Z)\), and \[ H(P_\pi\mid R)=\int\log h(Z)\,\mathrm{d}P_\pi =\int\log h\,\mathrm{d}\pi=H(\pi\mid R_{01}). \] Taking infima gives both inequalities and hence equality. If the static infimum is infinite, the dynamic lower bound is already infinite, so equality still holds. At finite optimal value, the nonnegative conditional term vanishes exactly when \(P^z=R^z\) for \(\pi\)-almost every \(z\), proving the asserted bridge representation.

Relation to entropic optimal transport

Fix Brownian motion with generator \(\varepsilon\Delta/2\) and initial probability density \(r_0>0\). Its endpoint density is \[ r_0(x)(2\pi\varepsilon)^{-d/2} \exp\{-|x-y|^2/(2\varepsilon)\}. \] If \(\mu_i=\rho_i\,\mathrm{d}x\) have finite entropy and the following integrals are finite, then for \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu_0,\mu_1)\), \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{KL}(\pi\|R^\varepsilon_{01}) ={}&\operatorname{KL}(\pi\|\mu_0\otimes\mu_1) +\frac1{2\varepsilon}\int|x-y|^2\,\mathrm{d}\pi\\ &+\operatorname{KL}(\mu_0\|r_0\,\mathrm{d}x) +\operatorname{Ent}(\mu_1) +\frac d2\log(2\pi\varepsilon). \end{aligned} \] Only the first two terms vary with the coupling. Multiplying by \(\varepsilon\) therefore gives exactly the entropic problem with cost \(|x-y|^2/2\), up to fixed endpoint constants. The Brownian initial law must be specified: “Brownian motion with Lebesgue initial law” would be a sigma-finite reference, not a probability law.

Schrödinger potentials

In the strictly positive finite-state model, entropy duality gives the unique optimizer in the form \[ \pi^\star(x,y)=f(x)g(y)R^\varepsilon_{01}(x,y), \] with positive factors unique up to reciprocal scaling. Enforcing marginals gives \[ f(x)\mathbb E_R[g(X_1)\mid X_0=x] =\frac{\mu_0(x)}{R_0(x)},\qquad g(y)\mathbb E_R[f(X_0)\mid X_1=y] =\frac{\mu_1(y)}{R_1(y)}. \] These are the Schrödinger equations; alternating them is Sinkhorn scaling with the reference endpoint matrix as kernel. Analogous factorizations hold for diffusion references under additional absolute-continuity and integrability hypotheses. We use such a factorization in the smooth calculations below; the analytic existence theory is in Léonard's survey, §§2–4.

Entropic interpolation

The marginals \(\mu_t=P_t^\star\) form the entropic interpolation. For a Markov reference and \(P^\star=f(X_0)g(X_1)R\), past and future are conditionally independent given \(X_t\). Consequently \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_t}{\mathrm{d}R_t} =\mathbb E_R[f(X_0)\mid X_t]\, \mathbb E_R[g(X_1)\mid X_t]. \] For the Brownian reference above, let \(H_t\) be convolution with the heat kernel of variance \(\varepsilon t\). Define the Lebesgue-density factors \[ a_t=H_t(f r_0),\qquad b_t=H_{1-t}g. \] Then \(\rho_t=a_tb_t\), \(\partial_ta_t=\varepsilon\Delta a_t/2\), and \(\partial_tb_t=-\varepsilon\Delta b_t/2\). These factors differ from the conditional-expectation factors when the initial reference density is nonconstant.

Forward, backward, and current velocities

For smooth positive factors, the Brownian bridge's forward drift and backward drift (in the forward-time convention) are \[ b_t^+=\varepsilon\nabla\log b_t,\qquad b_t^-=-\varepsilon\nabla\log a_t. \] Their average and half-difference are \[ v_t=\frac\varepsilon2\nabla\log(b_t/a_t),\qquad u_t=\frac\varepsilon2\nabla\log\rho_t. \] The forward drift solves a Fokker–Planck equation with diffusion; the current velocity solves the continuity equation without a diffusion term. Indeed, \[ \partial_t(a_tb_t)=\frac\varepsilon2(b_t\Delta a_t-a_t\Delta b_t) =-\nabla\cdot(\rho_tv_t). \] Mixing reference-relative factors with Lebesgue densities would give an incorrect backward drift.

Dynamic action with Fisher information

Theorem 20.3 (Entropic dynamic formulation (smooth finite-action setting)). Up to endpoint-dependent entropy constants, the Brownian Schrödinger problem is equivalent to minimizing \[ \int_0^1\int \left\{ \frac{1}{2\varepsilon}|v_t|^2 +\frac{\varepsilon}{8}|\nabla\log\rho_t|^2 \right\} \rho_t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t \] subject to the continuity equation and endpoint constraints.

Proof using the drift-entropy identity. The stochastic input is the finite-energy Girsanov identity: for an admissible controlled law with forward drift \(b^+\), diffusion coefficient \(\sqrt\varepsilon I\), and initial law \(\mu_0\), \[ \operatorname{KL}(P\|R^\varepsilon) =\operatorname{KL}(\mu_0\|r_0\,\mathrm{d}x) +\frac1{2\varepsilon}\int_0^1\int|b_t^+|^2\rho_t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t. \tag{1} \] Here admissibility means that the controlled diffusion exists, is absolutely continuous with respect to the reference law, and its change-of-drift density satisfies the Girsanov identity; bounded smooth drifts with the usual exponential-martingale integrability are a sufficient case. The extension to general finite-action laws is an external result in Léonard, §4.

Assume smooth positive densities, finite kinetic and Fisher integrals, and either periodic boundary conditions or decay making the following integrations by parts valid. The forward Fokker–Planck equation is \(\partial_t\rho=-\nabla\cdot(\rho b^+)+\varepsilon\Delta\rho/2\). Define \(v=b^+-\varepsilon\nabla\log\rho/2\). Since \(\rho\nabla\log\rho=\nabla\rho\), \[ \rho v=\rho b^+-\tfrac\varepsilon2\nabla\rho, \qquad-\nabla\cdot(\rho v)=-\nabla\cdot(\rho b^+)+\tfrac\varepsilon2\Delta\rho=\partial_t\rho. \] Thus it satisfies the continuity equation. Pointwise expansion gives \[ \frac{|b^+|^2}{2\varepsilon}\rho =\frac{|v|^2}{2\varepsilon}\rho+\frac\varepsilon8|\nabla\log\rho|^2\rho +\frac12v\cdot\nabla\rho. \] The entropy chain rule and continuity equation yield \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{Ent}(\rho_t) =\int(1+\log\rho_t)(-\nabla\cdot(\rho_tv_t))\,\mathrm{d}x =\int v_t\cdot\nabla\rho_t\,\mathrm{d}x. \] Cauchy–Schwarz first in space, then in time, gives \[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1\left|\int v_t\cdot\nabla\rho_t\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\,\mathrm{d}t &\le\int_0^1\left(\int|v_t|^2\rho_t\right)^{1/2} \left(\int|\nabla\log\rho_t|^2\rho_t\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t\\ &\le\left(\int_0^1\int|v_t|^2\rho_t\right)^{1/2} \left(\int_0^1\int|\nabla\log\rho_t|^2\rho_t\right)^{1/2}<\infty. \end{aligned} \] Hence integration of the entropy derivative gives the finite endpoint difference. Combining with (1) gives exactly \[ \operatorname{KL}(P\|R^\varepsilon) =\operatorname{KL}(\mu_0\|r_0\,\mathrm{d}x) +\tfrac12[\operatorname{Ent}(\rho_1)-\operatorname{Ent}(\rho_0)] +\int_0^1\int\left(\frac{|v|^2}{2\varepsilon}+\frac\varepsilon8|\nabla\log\rho|^2\right)\rho. \] The first two terms depend only on endpoints. Conversely, an admissible smooth continuity-equation pair defines the forward drift by the same substitution, so minimization over corresponding admissible pairs gives the claimed smooth formulation. This calculation keeps all constants and the stochastic input explicit.

Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman structure

The logarithmic potentials \(\phi_t=\varepsilon\log b_t\) and \(\widehat\phi_t=\varepsilon\log a_t\) satisfy viscous Hamilton--Jacobi equations. For example, \[ \partial_t\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi_t|^2 +\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta\phi_t=0 \] Here \(b_t\) solves the backward heat equation, fixing the sign of the Laplacian. As \(\varepsilon\to0\), the viscosity term disappears and the equation approaches the Hamilton--Jacobi equation of Wasserstein geodesics.

Zero-noise limit

Theorem 20.4 (A concrete small-noise limit). Suppose \(\mu_0,\mu_1\) have bounded densities supported in compact subsets of \(\mathbb R^d\), and take a strictly positive Gaussian initial reference density \(r_0\). Then \[ \lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0} \varepsilon\min_{P:P_i=\mu_i}\operatorname{KL}(P\|R^\varepsilon) =\tfrac12W_2^2(\mu_0,\mu_1). \] The optimal endpoint couplings converge weakly to the unique quadratic optimal plan. Optimal path laws converge weakly to its straight-line dynamical plan.

Proof. Bounded compactly supported densities have finite Lebesgue entropy: their positive entropy parts are bounded and their negative parts satisfy \(|r\log r|\le1/e\) on a finite-volume support. The Gaussian \(r_0\) has bounded logarithm on that support, so \(\operatorname{KL}(\mu_0\|r_0\,\mathrm{d}x)<\infty\). To verify the endpoint constants, write \(\mu_i=\rho_i\,\mathrm{d}x\), \(a=\mathrm{d}\pi/\mathrm{d}(\mu_0\otimes\mu_1)\), and \(c=|x-y|^2/2\). On a \(\pi\)-full set, \[ \log\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}R^\varepsilon_{01}}(x,y) =\log a(x,y)+\log\frac{\rho_0(x)}{r_0(x)}+\log\rho_1(y) +\tfrac d2\log(2\pi\varepsilon)+c(x,y)/\varepsilon. \] Integrating, multiplying by \(\varepsilon\), and using the endpoint marginals gives \[ \varepsilon H(\pi\mid R^\varepsilon_{01}) =\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+\varepsilon H(\pi\mid\mu_0\otimes\mu_1) +\varepsilon\left[H(\mu_0\mid r_0\,\mathrm{d}x)+\operatorname{Ent}(\mu_1)+\tfrac d2\log(2\pi\varepsilon)\right]. \] The last bracket is independent of \(\pi\). Static reduction and minimization therefore give \[ \varepsilon\inf_P\operatorname{KL}(P\|R^\varepsilon) =\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\mu_0,\mu_1) +\varepsilon\left[\operatorname{KL}(\mu_0\|r_0\,\mathrm{d}x)+\operatorname{Ent}(\mu_1)+\frac d2\log(2\pi\varepsilon)\right]. \] For cost \(|x-y|^2/2\), compact entropic convergence makes the first term tend to \(W_2^2/2\); the bracket multiplied by \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero. Every endpoint limit is optimal, and Brenier's uniqueness identifies it with \(\pi_*\). Compactness then gives convergence of the whole family \(\pi_\varepsilon\).

For the path limit use independent endpoints \((X,Y)\sim\pi_\varepsilon\) and a standard Brownian bridge \(\beta_t=B_t-tB_1\). Static reduction says the optimal path has law \[ \gamma_t=(1-t)X+tY+\sqrt\varepsilon\,\beta_t. \] Doob's \(L^2\) inequality for the submartingale \(|B_t|\) gives \(\mathbb E\sup_{t\le1}|B_t|^2\le4\mathbb E|B_1|^2=4d\). Therefore \[ \mathbb E\sup_{t\le1}|\beta_t|^2 \le2\mathbb E\sup_{t\le1}|B_t|^2+2\mathbb E|B_1|^2\le8d+2d=10d. \] For the straight path \(\ell_{X,Y}(t)=(1-t)X+tY\), \[ \mathbb E\|\gamma-\ell_{X,Y}\|_\infty =\sqrt\varepsilon\,\mathbb E\|\beta\|_\infty \le\sqrt\varepsilon\,(\mathbb E\|\beta\|_\infty^2)^{1/2}\le\sqrt{10d\varepsilon}. \] For every bounded \(1\)-Lipschitz functional \(G\) on path space with the uniform metric, \[ \left|\mathbb EG(\gamma)-\int G(t\mapsto(1-t)x+ty)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_\varepsilon(x,y)\right| \le\sqrt{10d\varepsilon}. \] The endpoint-to-path map satisfies \[ \|\ell_{x,y}-\ell_{x',y'}\|_\infty \le|x-x'|+|y-y'|, \] so it is continuous from endpoint space into path space, so weak convergence of \(\pi_\varepsilon\) passes the second term to the straight-path law of \(\pi_*\). Bounded Lipschitz tests determine weak convergence on this Polish path space. This proves the full asserted path limit.

Gaussian bridges

For Gaussian endpoint laws and a linear Gaussian reference process, the Schrödinger bridge remains Gaussian. Its mean solves a deterministic minimum-energy interpolation, while its covariance solves coupled matrix Riccati equations. In the zero-noise limit these equations reduce to the Gaussian Wasserstein geodesic from Geodesics and displacement interpolation.

What the bridge adds to transport

Classical transport chooses deterministic least-action motion whenever a map exists. A Schrödinger bridge retains stochastic fluctuations around the most likely motion, remains smooth for positive noise, and naturally incorporates a reference dynamics. This makes it a genuine dynamical theory rather than merely a computational regularization.

Exercises

bridges and deterministic limits

  1. Exercise 20.1. Why does the optimal path law retain the reference conditional bridge for each endpoint pair?

    Hint

    Use the entropy decomposition conditional on the endpoints and identify its nonnegative remainder.

    Solution

    The conditional relative-entropy term in the chain rule is nonnegative and is zero precisely when the conditional laws coincide.

  2. Exercise 20.2. For \(\rho_t=a_tb_t\) with the two heat equations stated above, verify the continuity equation for \(v_t\).

    Hint

    Differentiate the product of the two heat solutions and expand the divergence of their proposed flux.

    Solution

    Expand \(\partial_t(a_tb_t)\) and \(-\nabla\cdot[\varepsilon(a_t\nabla b_t-b_t\nabla a_t)/2]\). The mixed gradient terms cancel.
