Learning goal. Use potentials to give lower bounds and turn complementary slackness into an optimality certificate.

The primal problem chooses how mass moves. The dual problem assigns a value to mass at each endpoint. If the combined endpoint value never exceeds the cost of a journey, then its expected value is a lower bound on every transport plan. At optimality this inequality becomes equality exactly along the journeys actually used.

Dual feasible potentials

Definition 7.1 (Dual feasible potentials). A pair of measurable functions \((\varphi,\psi)\) is dual feasible for the cost \(c\) if \[ \varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y) \] for every \((x,y)\). We use continuous potentials on compact spaces, or explicitly integrable potentials when the spaces are noncompact; feasibility must not merely hold under one chosen coupling. Its dual value is \[ J(\varphi,\psi)=\int_X\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_Y\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu. \] The Kantorovich dual value is \[ D_c(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\varphi\oplus\psi\le c}J(\varphi,\psi). \]

Proposition 7.2 (Weak duality). For every \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) and every feasible \((\varphi,\psi)\), \[ J(\varphi,\psi)\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] Hence \(D_c(\mu,\nu)\le K_c(\mu,\nu)\).

Proof. Since \(\pi_X=\mu\), \(\pi_Y=\nu\), the pushforward integral identity gives \[ \int\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi=\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu, \qquad \int\psi(y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi=\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu. \] These integrals are finite for the integrable feasible potentials under consideration. Hence \[ \begin{aligned} J(\varphi,\psi) &=\int(\varphi(x)+\psi(y))\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y)\\ &\le\int c(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y) \qquad\text{because }\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y). \end{aligned} \] For each fixed \(\pi\), take the supremum over feasible potentials; then take the infimum over \(\pi\): \[ D_c\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi\quad(\forall\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D_c\le K_c. \]

Strong duality

Theorem 7.3 (Kantorovich duality on compact spaces). If \(X,Y\) are compact metric spaces and \(c\) is finite and continuous, then \[ \min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi =\sup_{\varphi\in C(X),\,\psi\in C(Y),\,\varphi+\psi\le c} \left\{\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}. \] The supremum is attained; its normalization is discussed below.

Proof. Let \(Z=X\times Y\), \(V=C(X)\times C(Y)\), and define \[ L(\pi,\varphi,\psi)=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\pi_X)+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}(\nu-\pi_Y), \quad \pi\in\mathcal P(Z). \] If \(\pi_X\ne\mu\), some \(f\in C(X)\) has \(a=\int f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\pi_X)\ne0\). Taking \(\varphi=tf\operatorname{sgn}(a)\), \(\psi=0\), gives \(L=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+t|a|\to\infty\). The other marginal is treated identically. Therefore \[ \sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in V}L(\pi,\varphi,\psi) =\begin{cases}\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi,&\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu),\\+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \] The set \(\mathcal P(Z)\) is compact convex in the weak topology, \(V\) is convex, and \(L\) is continuous affine in both variables. Sion's minimax theorem (the convex-analysis input) gives \[ \begin{aligned} K_c&=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal P(Z)}\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in V}L =\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in V}\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal P(Z)}L\\ &=\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in V}\left[J(\varphi,\psi)+\min_{(x,y)\in Z}(c(x,y)-\varphi(x)-\psi(y))\right]. \end{aligned} \] The last equality holds because integrating a continuous function against a probability is at least its minimum, with equality for a Dirac mass at a minimizer. Write that minimum as \(m\). Then \[ \varphi(x)+m+\psi(y)\le c(x,y),\qquad J(\varphi+m,\psi)=J(\varphi,\psi)+m\le D_c. \] Thus \(K_c\le D_c\), and weak duality gives equality. Attainment is proved below by a normalized compactness argument.

On Polish spaces, nonnegative lower-semicontinuous costs also admit strong duality, using bounded continuous feasible potentials for the value formula. Dual attainment does not follow in that generality. The noncompact extension uses approximation and separation; see Ambrosio–Gigli, §1.3. In particular, equality of optimal values and existence of maximizing potentials are different claims.

Complementary slackness

Theorem 7.4 (Optimality certificate). Assume strong duality, as in the compact continuous-cost theorem above. Let \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) and let \((\varphi,\psi)\) be dual feasible. If both values are finite, then \(\pi\) and \((\varphi,\psi)\) are primal and dual optimal if and only if \[ \varphi(x)+\psi(y)=c(x,y) \qquad\text{for }\pi\text{-almost every }(x,y). \]

Proof. Work under strong-duality hypotheses, in particular the compact continuous-cost setting above. The slack \(s=c-\varphi-\psi\) is nonnegative, and finite primal and dual values give \[ \int s\,\mathrm{d}\pi=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi-J(\varphi,\psi). \] If \(s=0\) \(\pi\)-almost everywhere, weak duality and feasibility give the sandwich \[ J(\varphi,\psi)\le D_c\le K_c\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi=J(\varphi,\psi). \] Every inequality is equality, proving both optimalities. Conversely, optimality and strong duality imply \(\int s\,\mathrm{d}\pi=K_c-D_c=0\). For each integer \(n\ge1\), \[ 0=\int s\,\mathrm{d}\pi\ge n^{-1}\pi(s\ge n^{-1}),\qquad \{s>0\}=\bigcup_{n\ge1}\{s\ge n^{-1}\}. \] Each set in the union has measure zero, so \(\pi(s>0)=0\), which is the contact identity.

This criterion converts a global optimization problem into a geometric support condition. One does not need to compare a candidate plan with every competitor; it suffices to find potentials touching the cost on its support.

Dual attainment and normalization

Proposition 7.5 (Dual attainment and normalization). If \(X,Y\) are compact and \(c\) is continuous, the dual supremum is attained. Potentials are nonunique because \((\varphi+a,\psi-a)\) has the same feasibility and value. One may normalize, for example, by fixing \(\varphi(x_0)=0\).

Proof. Let \((\varphi_n,\psi_n)\) be continuous feasible pairs with \(J(\varphi_n,\psi_n)\to D_c\). Set \[ f_n(x)=\inf_y(c(x,y)-\psi_n(y)),\qquad g_n(y)=\inf_x(c(x,y)-f_n(x)). \] Feasibility gives \(f_n\ge\varphi_n\); the inequality \(f_n(x)\le c(x,y)-\psi_n(y)\) gives \(g_n\ge\psi_n\). Also \(f_n+g_n\le c\). Thus \[ J(\varphi_n,\psi_n)\le J(f_n,g_n)\le D_c,\qquad J(f_n,g_n)\to D_c. \] For any families \((a_z),(b_z)\), \(|\inf_z a_z-\inf_z b_z|\le\sup_z|a_z-b_z|\). Therefore \[ |f_n(x)-f_n(x')|\le\sup_y|c(x,y)-c(x',y)|, \quad |g_n(y)-g_n(y')|\le\sup_x|c(x,y)-c(x,y')|. \] Replace \((f_n,g_n)\) by \((f_n-f_n(x_0),g_n+f_n(x_0))\), preserving both the objective and \(g_n=f_n^c\). With \(M=\|c\|_\infty\), \[ f_n(x_0)=0,\qquad |f_n(x)|\le2M,\qquad -3M\le c(x,y)-f_n(x)\le3M,\qquad |g_n(y)|\le3M. \] Uniform continuity of \(c\) makes the displayed moduli tend to zero independently of \(n\). Arzelà–Ascoli, applied successively to the two families, gives one subsequence with \[ \|f_{n_k}-f\|_\infty+\|g_{n_k}-g\|_\infty\to0. \] Pointwise limits give \(f(x)+g(y)\le c(x,y)\). Moreover \[ |J(f_{n_k},g_{n_k})-J(f,g)| \le\|f_{n_k}-f\|_\infty+\|g_{n_k}-g\|_\infty\to0. \] Hence \(J(f,g)=D_c\), proving attainment with \(f(x_0)=0\).

Kantorovich--Rubinstein duality

Theorem 7.6 (Kantorovich--Rubinstein theorem). On a Polish metric space \((X,d)\), for \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_1(X)\), \[ W_1(\mu,\nu) =\sup_{\operatorname{Lip}(f)\le1} \left\{\int_X f\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_X f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}. \]

Proof. Normalize Lipschitz functions by \(f(x_0)=0\); then \(|f(x)|\le d(x,x_0)\), so both integrals are finite. For every coupling, \[ \int f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)=\int(f(x)-f(y))\,\mathrm{d}\pi\le\int d(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] Taking the supremum over 1-Lipschitz \(f\) and the infimum over \(\pi\) gives \(S(\mu,\nu)\le W_1(\mu,\nu)\), where \(S\) denotes the claimed supremum.

Finite support. On a finite set \(D\) containing both supports, finite Kantorovich duality gives feasible vectors \(\varphi,\psi\). Define \[ f(x)=\min_{y\in D}\{d(x,y)-\psi(y)\},\qquad x\in D. \] The triangle inequality gives \(f(x)\le d(x,x')+f(x')\), and swapping \(x,x'\) gives \(|f(x)-f(x')|\le d(x,x')\). Feasibility and the choice \(y=x\) give \[ \varphi(x)\le f(x)\le-\psi(x),\qquad J(\varphi,\psi)\le\int f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu). \] Thus finite duality proves \(W_1=S\) on \(D\). The extension \[ \widetilde f(x)=\min_{z\in D}\{f(z)+d(x,z)\},\qquad x\in X, \] is 1-Lipschitz by the same inequality and equals \(f\) on \(D\), so the finite-support formula also holds with test functions on \(X\).

General measures. Use the finite quantizations from the Wasserstein separability proof: \(\mu_n=q_{n\#}\mu\), \(\nu_n=q_{n\#}\nu\), with \[ e_n:=\int d(x,q_n(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\int d(y,q_n(y))\,\mathrm{d}\nu(y)\to0. \] The metric triangle inequality gives \(|W_1(\mu_n,\nu_n)-W_1(\mu,\nu)|\le e_n\). Uniformly over all 1-Lipschitz \(f\), \[ \left|\int f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu_n-\nu_n)-\int f\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\nu)\right|\le e_n. \] Taking suprema yields \(|S(\mu_n,\nu_n)-S(\mu,\nu)|\le e_n\). The finite-support equality therefore passes to the limit: \[ W_1(\mu,\nu)=\lim_nW_1(\mu_n,\nu_n)=\lim_nS(\mu_n,\nu_n)=S(\mu,\nu). \]

Potentials as sensitivity derivatives

Dual potentials also measure the first-order response of the optimal value to changes in the marginals. Formally, if \(\mu_t=\mu+t\sigma\) and \(\nu_t=\nu+t\tau\) preserve total mass, an optimal pair \((\varphi,\psi)\) gives \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}K_c(\mu_t,\nu_t)\bigg|_{t=0} =\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\tau \] whenever differentiability holds. This fact later drives statistical limit theory and numerical optimization.

Exercises

finding touching potentials

  1. Exercise 7.1. For \(\mu=\delta_0\), \(\nu=\delta_a\) on \(\mathbb R\), find a 1-Lipschitz function certifying \(W_1=|a|\).

    Hint

    Choose the sign of a linear function so that its difference between the two atoms is positive.

    Solution

    For \(a\ne0\), take \(f(x)=-\operatorname{sign}(a)x\) in \(\mu f-\nu f\). It achieves \(|a|\); for \(a=0\), take zero.

  2. Exercise 7.2. A feasible plan has cost 3 and a feasible dual pair has value 2.9. What is certified?

    Hint

    Use weak duality to place the unknown optimum between the two feasible objective values.

    Solution

    The optimum lies in \([2.9,3]\), and the plan is at most 0.1 suboptimal. A small primal–dual gap certifies objective accuracy, not proximity of the plan to a unique optimizer.
