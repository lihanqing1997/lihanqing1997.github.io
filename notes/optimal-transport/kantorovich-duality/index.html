Learning goal. Use potentials to give lower bounds and turn complementary slackness into an optimality certificate.

The primal problem chooses how mass moves. The dual problem assigns a value to mass at each endpoint. If the combined endpoint value never exceeds the cost of a journey, then its expected value is a lower bound on every transport plan. At optimality this inequality becomes equality exactly along the journeys actually used.

Dual feasible potentials

Definition. A pair of measurable functions \((\varphi,\psi)\) is dual feasible for the cost \(c\) if \[ \varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y) \] for every \((x,y)\). We use continuous potentials on compact spaces, or explicitly integrable potentials when the spaces are noncompact; feasibility must not merely hold under one chosen coupling. Its dual value is \[ J(\varphi,\psi)=\int_X\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_Y\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu. \] The Kantorovich dual value is \[ D_c(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\varphi\oplus\psi\le c}J(\varphi,\psi). \]

Weak duality. For every \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) and every feasible \((\varphi,\psi)\), \[ J(\varphi,\psi)\le\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] Hence \(D_c(\mu,\nu)\le K_c(\mu,\nu)\).

Proof. Using the marginal constraints, \[ J(\varphi,\psi) =\int_{X\times Y}\{\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y) \le\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y). \] Taking the supremum on the left and the infimum on the right proves the claim.

Strong duality

Kantorovich duality on compact spaces. If \(X,Y\) are compact metric spaces and \(c\) is finite and continuous, then \[ \min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi =\sup_{\varphi\in C(X),\,\psi\in C(Y),\,\varphi+\psi\le c} \left\{\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}. \] The supremum is attained; its normalization is discussed below.

Proof. Let \(\mathcal P(X\times Y)\) be all probability measures on the compact product, and set \[ L(\pi,\varphi,\psi)=\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi+ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}(\mu-\pi_X)+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}(\nu-\pi_Y). \] The supremum over continuous potentials is \(\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi\) when the marginals are correct and \(+\infty\) otherwise: a continuous test function detects any incorrect marginal and can be scaled. Sion's minimax theorem applies because the probability-measure set is compact convex in the weak topology and \(L\) is continuous affine in each variable. Thus \[ K_c=\sup_{\varphi,\psi}\left[J(\varphi,\psi)+ \min_{x,y}\{c(x,y)-\varphi(x)-\psi(y)\}\right]. \] For a fixed pair let the displayed minimum be \(m\). Replacing \(\varphi\) by \(\varphi+m\) gives a feasible pair with objective \(J+m\). Hence \(K_c\le D_c\); weak duality gives equality.

On Polish spaces, nonnegative lower-semicontinuous costs also admit strong duality, using bounded continuous feasible potentials for the value formula. Dual attainment does not follow in that generality. The noncompact extension uses approximation and separation; see Ambrosio–Gigli, §1.3. In particular, equality of optimal values and existence of maximizing potentials are different claims.

Complementary slackness

Optimality certificate. Let \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) and let \((\varphi,\psi)\) be dual feasible. If both values are finite, then \(\pi\) and \((\varphi,\psi)\) are primal and dual optimal if and only if \[ \varphi(x)+\psi(y)=c(x,y) \qquad\text{for }\pi\text{-almost every }(x,y). \]

Proof. The nonnegative slack \(s=c-\varphi-\psi\) satisfies \[ \int s\,\mathrm{d}\pi =\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi-J(\varphi,\psi). \] If the equality holds \(\pi\)-almost surely, the primal and dual values coincide, so weak duality makes both optimal. Conversely, if both are optimal, strong duality makes the right-hand side zero. A nonnegative measurable function with zero integral vanishes almost surely.

This criterion converts a global optimization problem into a geometric support condition. One does not need to compare a candidate plan with every competitor; it suffices to find potentials touching the cost on its support.

Dual attainment and normalization

Proposition. If \(X,Y\) are compact and \(c\) is continuous, the dual supremum is attained. Potentials are nonunique because \((\varphi+a,\psi-a)\) has the same feasibility and value. One may normalize, for example, by fixing \(\varphi(x_0)=0\).

Proof. Take continuous feasible pairs \((\varphi_n,\psi_n)\) with objectives tending to the dual value. Define \[ f_n(x)=\inf_y(c(x,y)-\psi_n(y)),\qquad g_n(y)=\inf_x(c(x,y)-f_n(x)). \] Then \(f_n\ge\varphi_n\), \(g_n\ge\psi_n\), and \(f_n+g_n\le c\). For all \(x,x',y,y'\), \[ |f_n(x)-f_n(x')|\le\sup_y|c(x,y)-c(x',y)|,\quad |g_n(y)-g_n(y')|\le\sup_x|c(x,y)-c(x,y')|. \] Normalize \(f_n(x_0)=0\) by opposite constant shifts. If \(M=\|c\|_\infty\), the first bound gives \(\|f_n\|_\infty\le2M\); since \(g_n=f_n^c\), also \(\|g_n\|_\infty\le3M\). Uniform continuity of \(c\) makes both families equicontinuous. Arzelà–Ascoli gives a common subsequence converging uniformly to \((f,g)\). The inequality \(f+g\le c\) passes pointwise to the limit, while \[ \left|\int f_n\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\le\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0 \] and likewise for \(g_n\). The objectives still tend to the supremum, so the limit attains it.

Kantorovich--Rubinstein duality

Kantorovich--Rubinstein theorem. On a Polish metric space \((X,d)\), for \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_1(X)\), \[ W_1(\mu,\nu) =\sup_{\operatorname{Lip}(f)\le1} \left\{\int_X f\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_X f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}. \]

Proof. If \(f\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz, then \(f(x)-f(y)\le d(x,y)\), so \((f,-f)\) is dual feasible and weak duality gives the lower bound. For the reverse direction, start from any feasible \((\varphi,\psi)\) and define \[ f(x)=\inf_y\{d(x,y)-\psi(y)\}. \] The triangle inequality shows that \(f\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz. Feasibility gives \(\varphi\le f\), while the definition gives \(f(y)\le-\psi(y)\) by choosing the same point in the infimum. Hence \[ \int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu \le\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int f\,\mathrm{d}\nu. \] Taking the supremum and invoking Kantorovich duality proves the formula.

Potentials as sensitivity derivatives

Dual potentials also measure the first-order response of the optimal value to changes in the marginals. Formally, if \(\mu_t=\mu+t\sigma\) and \(\nu_t=\nu+t\tau\) preserve total mass, an optimal pair \((\varphi,\psi)\) gives \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}K_c(\mu_t,\nu_t)\bigg|_{t=0} =\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\tau \] whenever differentiability holds. This fact later drives statistical limit theory and numerical optimization.

Sources and proof details. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport, §1.3, for separation, noncompact duality, and attainment conditions. See also Fenchel duality.

Practice: finding touching potentials

  1. For \(\mu=\delta_0\), \(\nu=\delta_a\) on \(\mathbb R\), find a 1-Lipschitz function certifying \(W_1=|a|\).

    Hint and answer

    For \(a\ne0\), take \(f(x)=-\operatorname{sign}(a)x\) in \(\mu f-\nu f\). It achieves \(|a|\); for \(a=0\), take zero.

  2. A feasible plan has cost 3 and a feasible dual pair has value 2.9. What is certified?

    Hint and answer

    The optimum lies in \([2.9,3]\), and the plan is at most 0.1 suboptimal. A small primal–dual gap certifies objective accuracy, not proximity of the plan to a unique optimizer.
