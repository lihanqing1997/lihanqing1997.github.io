Learning goal. Separate plan existence, map existence, and regularity, and recognize the extra geometry needed for general costs.

Existence of a transport map is not the same as regularity. Even smooth positive densities can produce a discontinuous map when the domains or the cost have the wrong geometry. This page separates the Euclidean quadratic theory from the structural conditions needed for general costs.

Twist and nondegeneracy

Definition. A \(C^2\) cost \(c:\Omega\times\Lambda\to\mathbb R\) satisfies the twist condition if, for every \(x\), the map \[ y\longmapsto D_xc(x,y) \] is injective. It is nondegenerate if \(\det D^2_{xy}c(x,y)\ne0\) throughout the domain. Under twist, define the \(c\)-exponential \(Y(x,p)\) by \[ D_xc(x,Y(x,p))=p. \]

Map induced by a differentiable potential. Let \((\varphi,\psi)\) be optimal dual potentials and suppose \(\varphi\) is differentiable at \(x\). If \((x,y)\) belongs to the contact set, then \[ D\varphi(x)=D_xc(x,y). \] Under twist, \(y=Y(x,D\varphi(x))\) is unique.

Proof. Fix a contact pair \((x,y)\), with \(x\) an interior differentiability point of \(\varphi\), and set \(F(z)=c(z,y)-\psi(y)-\varphi(z)\). Feasibility and contact mean \[ F(z)\ge0\quad(\forall z),\qquad F(x)=0. \] For every vector \(a\) and small \(h\) with \(x+ha\) in the domain, \[ \frac{F(x+ha)-F(x)}h\ge0\quad(h>0),\qquad \frac{F(x+ha)-F(x)}h\le0\quad(h<0). \] Differentiability makes both limits equal \((D_xc(x,y)-D\varphi(x))\cdot a\); it is therefore zero. Taking the coordinate vectors gives \(D\varphi(x)=D_xc(x,y)\). Under twist, \(y\mapsto D_xc(x,y)\) is injective, so two contact destinations would satisfy \[ D_xc(x,y_1)=D\varphi(x)=D_xc(x,y_2)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_1=y_2. \] If \(\varphi\) is differentiable source-almost everywhere, disintegration of an optimal plan supported on contact forces its conditional law to be the Dirac mass at this unique destination. The measurable Dirac-kernel criterion from Chapter 1 then gives a transport map.

The generated Jacobian equation

Differentiate \(D\varphi(x)=D_xc(x,T(x))\): \[ D^2\varphi(x) =D^2_{xx}c(x,T(x))+D^2_{xy}c(x,T(x))DT(x). \] Nondegeneracy therefore gives \[ DT=(D^2_{xy}c)^{-1}\{D^2\varphi-D^2_{xx}c\}. \] Combining its determinant with \(f(x)=g(T(x))|\det DT(x)|\) yields a generated Jacobian equation. Quadratic cost has \(D^2_{xy}c=-I\) and reduces, after the usual change of potential, to Monge--Ampère.

Interior regularity for quadratic cost

Caffarelli interior regularity (external theorem). Let \(\Omega,\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^d\) be bounded open convex domains. Suppose \[ 0<\lambda\le f,g\le\Lambda_0<\infty \] on their supports, and let \(u\) be the Brenier potential. Then \(u\) is strictly convex and \(u\in C^{1,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\) for some \(\alpha>0\) depending only on the dimension and density bounds, on compact subsets of the source interior. If \(f,g\in C^{k,\alpha}\) and are bounded away from zero, then \(u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}\).

Analytic input. The multidimensional statement is an external regularity theorem. Density bounds control Monge–Ampère mass, convex target geometry gives strict convexity and interior localization, and section estimates give Hölder control of the gradient. Smooth-density conclusions additionally use the regularity theory for the equation; they do not follow by applying uniformly elliptic estimates before a Hessian bound has been established. See Santambrogio, Chapter 1. The one-dimensional counterpart admits the complete elementary proof below.

One-dimensional regularity with explicit bounds. Let \(f\in C^{k,\alpha}([a,b])\), \(g\in C^{k,\alpha}([c,d])\), \(k\ge0\), \(0<\alpha<1\), have equal positive mass, and suppose \(0<\lambda\le f,g\le M\). Their monotone map satisfies \[ T=G^{-1}\circ F\in C^{k+1,\alpha}([a,b]),\qquad T'=\frac{f}{g\circ T},\qquad \frac\lambda M\le T'\le\frac M\lambda. \] Its potential \(u(x)=\int_a^xT(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\) belongs to \(C^{k+2,\alpha}\).

Proof. Put \(F(x)=\int_a^xf(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), \(G(y)=\int_c^yg(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\), and let \(M_0\) be their common total mass. Positivity gives \[ G:[c,d]\to[0,M_0]\text{ bijective},\qquad G'=g\ge\lambda>0. \] The inverse-function theorem therefore gives \(T=G^{-1}\circ F\in C^1\). Differentiating \(G(T(x))=F(x)\) gives \[ g(T(x))T'(x)=f(x),\qquad T'(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(T(x))},\qquad \lambda/M\le T'(x)\le M/\lambda. \] In particular \(T\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz with \(L=M/\lambda\). Denoting Hölder seminorms by \([\cdot]_\alpha\), for \(k=0\) we have \[ \begin{aligned} |T'(x)-T'(x')| &\le\frac{|f(x)-f(x')|}{\lambda} +\frac{M}{\lambda^2}|g(T(x))-g(T(x'))|\\ &\le\left(\frac{[f]_\alpha}{\lambda}+\frac{M[g]_\alpha L^\alpha}{\lambda^2}\right)|x-x'|^\alpha. \end{aligned} \] Thus \(T\in C^{1,\alpha}\).

For higher regularity, suppose \(1\le j\le k\) and \(T\in C^{j,\alpha}\). Repeated chain rule has the explicit finite form \[ (g\circ T)^{(j)} =\sum_{m_1+2m_2+\cdots+jm_j=j} \frac{j!}{m_1!\cdots m_j!}\, (g^{(m_1+\cdots+m_j)}\circ T) \prod_{\ell=1}^j\left(\frac{T^{(\ell)}}{\ell!}\right)^{m_\ell}. \] Every factor is Hölder continuous; bounded products remain Hölder because \([ab]_\alpha\le\|a\|_\infty[b]_\alpha+\|b\|_\infty[a]_\alpha\). Thus \(h=g\circ T\in C^{j,\alpha}\). Since \(h\ge\lambda\), the reciprocal \(r=1/h\) satisfies the recursion obtained from \((hr)^{(j)}=0\): \[ r^{(j)}=-\frac1h\sum_{\ell=1}^j\binom j\ell h^{(\ell)}r^{(j-\ell)}. \] Induction gives \(r\in C^{j,\alpha}\), and the product rule gives \(T'=fr\in C^{j,\alpha}\), hence \(T\in C^{j+1,\alpha}\). Starting from the base case proves the assertion through \(j=k\). Finally \(u'=T\), so \(u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}\).

Global boundary regularity

Global regularity (external theorem, smooth uniformly convex setting). If \(\Omega,\Lambda\) are uniformly convex domains with sufficiently smooth boundaries, \(f,g\in C^{k,\alpha}\) are positive and bounded away from zero, and their masses agree, then the Brenier potential satisfies \[ u\in C^{k+2,\alpha}(\overline\Omega) \] for \(k\ge0\), \(0<\alpha<1\), and \(C^{k+2,\alpha}\) uniformly convex boundaries, up to an additive constant, and \(\nabla u:\overline\Omega\to\overline\Lambda\) is a diffeomorphism.

A statement of the boundary theorem is Chen–Figalli, Theorem 1.1; higher derivatives follow under the displayed higher smoothness assumptions. Its proof requires oblique boundary estimates for the second boundary condition \(\nabla u(\Omega)=\Lambda\). Ordinary Dirichlet theory is not applicable because the boundary values of \(u\) are not prescribed.

What can go wrong

Nonconvex target. If the target has separated components, nearby source points may be sent to different components. The resulting optimal map can jump across an interface even when both densities are smooth. Convexity of the target prevents this geometric tearing in the quadratic theory.

Vanishing density. If \(g\) approaches zero, the equation \(\det D^2u=f/g(\nabla u)\) can force unbounded Hessian eigenvalues. Positivity and two-sided density bounds are therefore genuine ellipticity assumptions.

The Ma--Trudinger--Wang curvature

For a general cost, twist and nondegeneracy produce a map and an equation but do not guarantee continuity. Define \[ A_{ij}(x,p)=D^2_{x_ix_j}c(x,Y(x,p)). \] With the sign convention used here, the Ma--Trudinger--Wang tensor is \[ \operatorname{MTW}_{(x,p)}(\xi,\eta) =-\frac{\partial^2 A_{ij}}{\partial p_k\partial p_l} \xi_i\xi_j\eta_k\eta_l, \qquad \xi\cdot\eta=0. \] The weak MTW condition requires this quantity to be nonnegative; the strong condition requires a positive lower bound proportional to \(|\xi|^2|\eta|^2\).

Regularity roadmap (qualitative). Under smoothness, twist, nondegeneracy, suitable \(c\)-convexity of the domains, positive smooth densities, and an MTW condition, optimal maps are locally Hölder or smoother according to the strength of the assumptions. Failure of the weak MTW condition permits smooth positive data for which the optimal map is discontinuous.

The tensor measures how \(c\)-support functions bend relative to one another. Quadratic Euclidean cost has zero MTW curvature. On a Riemannian manifold, the squared geodesic distance connects this tensor to sectional curvature, while the cut locus creates an additional obstruction to smoothness.

Transport on manifolds

For \(c(x,y)=\tfrac12d_M(x,y)^2\), the optimal map takes the form \[ T(x)=\exp_x(-\nabla\varphi(x)) \] for the dual convention \(\varphi+\psi\le c\), since \(D_x(d_M(x,y)^2/2)=-\log_x y\) away from the cut locus. Regularity requires that optimal pairs avoid the cut locus, because the squared distance ceases to be smooth there. Curvature, injectivity radius, and \(c\)-convexity of the target jointly replace Euclidean convexity.

A practical hierarchy of conclusions

The assumptions should be read in layers. Lower semicontinuity gives an optimal plan. Twist plus differentiability gives an optimal map. Positive density bounds and convexity yield continuity and interior regularity for quadratic cost. Smooth uniformly convex boundaries give global regularity. For general costs, MTW-type curvature is the additional geometric condition controlling continuity of the map.

Practice: assumptions that prevent tearing

  1. Check twist and nondegeneracy for \(c(x,y)=|x-y|^2/2\).

    Hint and answer

    \(D_xc=x-y\) is injective in \(y\), and \(D^2_{xy}c=-I\) is invertible. Also \(A(x,p)=I\), so the MTW tensor is zero.

  2. Why does a disconnected target suggest discontinuities even for smooth positive densities on each component?

    Hint and answer

    A continuous map from a connected source has connected image. A map transporting positive mass onto both separated target components must separate source regions; continuity across their interface is obstructed.
