Learning goal. Compute transport by quantiles and explain how order replaces a multidimensional optimization.

On the real line, order determines optimality. Pairing equal quantile levels produces a coupling with no crossings, and every crossing can be uncrossed without increasing a convex distance cost. This yields explicit formulas unavailable in higher dimensions.

For this chapter, \(\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R)\) denotes laws with finite \(p\)-th moment, and \[ W_p(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int|x-y|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi\right)^{1/p}. \] The preceding chapters established its metric and convergence properties; here order on the real line makes the optimization explicit.

Distribution and quantile functions

Definition. For \(\mu\in\mathcal P(\mathbb R)\), define \[ F_\mu(x)=\mu((-\infty,x]),\qquad F_\mu^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F_\mu(x)\ge u\},\quad 0<u<1. \] The generalized inverse \(F_\mu^{-1}\) is nondecreasing and left-continuous.

Quantile representation. If \(U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)\), then \(F_\mu^{-1}(U)\sim\mu\).

Proof. For every \(x\), \[ F_\mu^{-1}(u)\le x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\le F_\mu(x). \] Therefore \[ \mathbb P(F_\mu^{-1}(U)\le x)=\mathbb P(U\le F_\mu(x))=F_\mu(x), \] which identifies the distribution.

The quantile coupling

Definition. The monotone or comonotone coupling of \(\mu,\nu\) is \[ \pi^\uparrow=(F_\mu^{-1},F_\nu^{-1})_\#\lambda_{(0,1)}. \] If \(\mu\) has no atoms, the monotone transport map is \[ T(x)=F_\nu^{-1}(F_\mu(x)), \qquad T_\#\mu=\nu. \]

When \(\mu\) has atoms, the formula \(F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu\) may fail to split them and need not push \(\mu\) to \(\nu\). The quantile coupling remains valid because the auxiliary uniform variable supplies the required randomization inside jumps.

The uncrossing inequality

Monge inequality. Let \(h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be convex. If \(x_1\le x_2\) and \(y_1\le y_2\), then \[ h(x_1-y_1)+h(x_2-y_2) \le h(x_1-y_2)+h(x_2-y_1). \]

Proof. Set \(a=x_1-y_2\), \(b=x_1-y_1\), \(c=x_2-y_2\), \(d=x_2-y_1\). Then \(a\le b,c\le d\) and \(b+c=a+d\). If \(a=d\), all four numbers coincide. Otherwise let \(\theta=(b-a)/(d-a)\in[0,1]\). Thus \[ b=(1-\theta)a+\theta d,\qquad c=\theta a+(1-\theta)d. \] Convexity gives \(h(b)\le(1-\theta)h(a)+\theta h(d)\) and \(h(c)\le\theta h(a)+(1-\theta)h(d)\). Adding proves the inequality. If both orders are strict and \(h\) is strictly convex, then \(0<\theta<1\), and both inequalities are strict.

The right-hand side pairs the smaller source with the larger target and the larger source with the smaller target. The lemma says that removing such a crossing cannot increase any convex displacement cost.

Optimality of monotone rearrangement

Monotone rearrangement theorem. Let \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R)\). For \(c(x,y)=|x-y|^p\), the quantile coupling minimizes the cost. For \(p>1\) it is the unique optimal coupling. If in addition \(\mu\) has no atoms, it is induced by \(T=F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu\).

Proof. Write \(Q_\mu=F_\mu^{-1}\). We first prove optimality and then uniqueness.

1. Finite laws. For two laws with \(N\) equal-weight atoms, Birkhoff's theorem reduces the minimum to a permutation. If a permutation has an inversion, interchange the two target indices. The Monge inequality decreases or preserves the cost; the number of inversions decreases when adjacent inverted indices are interchanged. After finitely many interchanges the sorted matching remains. Rational masses reduce to this case by repeating atoms with a common denominator.

For arbitrary finite laws, fix a coupling matrix \(P\). Choose nonnegative rational matrices \(P^{(k)}\to P\) entrywise with total mass one. Their row and column sums \(a^{(k)},b^{(k)}\) converge to the original masses. The associated quantile functions converge pointwise outside the finitely many limiting cumulative-mass breakpoints. Their values lie in the same finite supports. Dominated convergence and the rational case therefore give \[ \int_0^1|Q_a-Q_b|^p\,\mathrm{d}u =\lim_k\int_0^1|Q_{a^{(k)}}-Q_{b^{(k)}}|^p\,\mathrm{d}u \le\lim_k\sum_{ij}P^{(k)}_{ij}|x_i-y_j|^p =\sum_{ij}P_{ij}|x_i-y_j|^p. \]

2. General laws and the limit. Let \(q_n\) clip the real line to \([-n,n]\) and then round down to a multiple of \(2^{-n}\). It is nondecreasing, has finite range, and \[ |q_n(x)-x|\le 2^{-n}+|x|\mathbf1_{\{|x|>n\}}. \] Hence \(q_n\to\operatorname{id}\) in \(L^p(\mu)\) and \(L^p(\nu)\), by the finite moments and dominated convergence on the tails. A nondecreasing function of a uniform variable is the quantile of its own law almost everywhere; thus the quantiles of \(q_{n\#}\mu,q_{n\#}\nu\) are \(q_n\circ Q_\mu,q_n\circ Q_\nu\). For any \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), the finite case gives \[ \|q_n(Q_\mu)-q_n(Q_\nu)\|_{L^p(0,1)} \le\|q_n(x)-q_n(y)\|_{L^p(\pi)}. \] Each side converges to the corresponding unquantized norm: the norm of its difference is at most the sum of the two quantization errors. Taking limits proves the desired bound for every \(\pi\); the quantile coupling attains it.

3. Uniqueness when \(p>1\). Suppose the support of an optimal plan contains \((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2)\) with \(x_1<x_2\) and \(y_1>y_2\). Strict uncrossing and continuity provide disjoint neighborhoods \(U_1,U_2\), of positive masses \(m_i\), on which the cost saving is at least \(\delta>0\). Put \(\gamma_i=\pi|_{U_i}/m_i\), and take independent pairs \((X_i,Y_i)\sim\gamma_i\). For \(0<\varepsilon\le\min(m_1,m_2)\), replace \(\varepsilon(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)\) by the laws \(\varepsilon\mathcal L(X_1,Y_2)+\varepsilon\mathcal L(X_2,Y_1)\). This preserves both marginals, remains nonnegative, and lowers the cost by at least \(\varepsilon\delta\), a contradiction.

The support is therefore monotone. Set \(H(x,y)=\pi(( -\infty,x]\times(-\infty,y])\). The sets \(\{X\le x,Y>y\}\) and \(\{X>x,Y\le y\}\) cannot both have positive mass, since that would give a crossing. Their masses are \(F_\mu(x)-H(x,y)\) and \(F_\nu(y)-H(x,y)\). Consequently \[ H(x,y)=\min(F_\mu(x),F_\nu(y)), \] which determines exactly the quantile coupling. Finally, if \(\mu\) is atomless, continuity of \(F_\mu\) gives \(F_\mu(X)\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)\) and \(Q_\mu(F_\mu(X))=X\) almost surely. Thus the coupling is the graph of \(Q_\nu\circ F_\mu\).

The same ordering principle extends to other convex displacement costs with suitable integrability. We use the power-cost version so every displayed value is finite. For \(p=1\), uniqueness can fail: if all source points lie to the left of all target points, every coupling has the same linear cost.

Explicit Wasserstein formulas

Corollary. For \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R)\), \[ W_p^p(\mu,\nu) =\int_0^1|F_\mu^{-1}(u)-F_\nu^{-1}(u)|^p\,\mathrm{d}u. \] For compactly supported measures, \[ W_\infty(\mu,\nu) =\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{0<u<1} |F_\mu^{-1}(u)-F_\nu^{-1}(u)|. \]

Proof of the \(W_\infty\) formula. For any coupling \(\pi\), \(W_p\le\|x-y\|_{L^\infty(\pi)}\). The quantile formula and monotone convergence of \(L^p\)-norms on a probability space give \(\lim_{p\to\infty}W_p=\|Q_\mu-Q_\nu\|_\infty\). Taking the infimum over \(\pi\) gives one inequality; using the quantile coupling gives the other.

Gaussian laws. If \(\mu=N(m_0,\sigma_0^2)\) and \(\nu=N(m_1,\sigma_1^2)\) with \(\sigma_0,\sigma_1>0\), then \[ T(x)=m_1+\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0}(x-m_0), \qquad W_2^2(\mu,\nu)=(m_0-m_1)^2+(\sigma_0-\sigma_1)^2. \] Indeed, with \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), the quantile coupling is \(X=m_0+\sigma_0Z\), \(Y=m_1+\sigma_1Z\). Therefore \[ \mathbb E|X-Y|^2=(m_0-m_1)^2+2(m_0-m_1)(\sigma_0-\sigma_1)\mathbb EZ+(\sigma_0-\sigma_1)^2\mathbb EZ^2, \] and \(\mathbb EZ=0\), \(\mathbb EZ^2=1\) give the formula.

Empirical measures. If \[ \mu_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i},\qquad \nu_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{y_i}, \] and \(x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}\), \(y_{(1)}\le\cdots\le y_{(n)}\), then \[ W_p^p(\mu_n,\nu_n)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n|x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}|^p. \] Thus one-dimensional empirical transport reduces to sorting.

A running example: uniform dilation

From a map to an optimality certificate. Take \(\mu=\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]\) and \(\nu=\operatorname{Unif}[0,2]\). Equal quantiles give \(T(x)=2x\). For cost \(c(x,y)=|x-y|^2/2\), its cost is \(1/6\). The potentials \[ \varphi(x)=-x^2/2,\qquad\psi(y)=y^2/4 \] satisfy \[ c(x,y)-\varphi(x)-\psi(y)=(x-y/2)^2\ge0. \] Equality holds on \(y=2x\), and their integrals sum to \(-1/6+1/3=1/6\). Thus the same example has a quantile solution, a convex-gradient map, and a matching primal–dual certificate. Later its interpolation is uniform on \([0,1+t]\), with velocity \(v_t(z)=z/(1+t)\).

Geodesics on the line

The quantile representation also linearizes Wasserstein geodesics: \[ F_{\mu_t}^{-1}(u)=(1-t)F_{\mu_0}^{-1}(u)+tF_{\mu_1}^{-1}(u). \] Consequently \(\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R)\) embeds isometrically as the closed convex cone of nondecreasing functions in \(L^2(0,1)\). This special Hilbertian description explains why one-dimensional barycenters and asymptotics are unusually explicit.

Practice: quantiles, atoms, and sorting

  1. Find \(T\) and \(W_2^2\) from \(\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]\) to \(\operatorname{Unif}[0,2]\).

    Hint and answer

    The quantiles are \(u\) and \(2u\). Thus \(T(x)=2x\) and \(W_2^2=\int_0^1u^2\,\mathrm{d}u=1/3\).

  2. For source \((\delta_0+\delta_1)/2\) and target \((\delta_2+\delta_3)/2\), compare the two matchings for \(p=1\) and \(p=2\).

    Hint and answer

    For \(p=1\), both costs are two. For \(p=2\), sorting costs four, while the crossing matching costs five. Strict convexity distinguishes the latter case.
