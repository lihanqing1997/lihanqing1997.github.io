Learning goal. Compute transport by quantiles and explain how order replaces a multidimensional optimization.

On the real line, order determines optimality. Pairing equal quantile levels produces a coupling with no crossings, and every crossing can be uncrossed without increasing a convex distance cost. This yields explicit formulas unavailable in higher dimensions.

For this chapter, \(\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R)\) denotes laws with finite \(p\)-th moment, and \[ W_p(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int|x-y|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi\right)^{1/p}. \] The preceding chapters established its metric and convergence properties; here order on the real line makes the optimization explicit.

Distribution and quantile functions

Definition. For \(\mu\in\mathcal P(\mathbb R)\), define \[ F_\mu(x)=\mu((-\infty,x]),\qquad F_\mu^{-1}(u)=\inf\{x:F_\mu(x)\ge u\},\quad 0<u<1. \] The generalized inverse \(F_\mu^{-1}\) is nondecreasing and left-continuous.

Quantile representation. If \(U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)\), then \(F_\mu^{-1}(U)\sim\mu\).

Proof. Put \(F=F_\mu\), \(Q=F_\mu^{-1}\). For \(u\in(0,1)\), the set \(A_u=\{t:F(t)\ge u\}\) is nonempty and bounded below, since \(F(-\infty)=0\), \(F(+\infty)=1\). If \(u\le F(x)\), then \(x\in A_u\), hence \(Q(u)\le x\). Conversely, if \(Q(u)\le x\), for every \(k\) some \(t_k\in A_u\) satisfies \(t_k<Q(u)+1/k\le x+1/k\). Therefore \[ F(x+1/k)\ge F(t_k)\ge u,\qquad F(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}F(x+1/k)\ge u, \] where the limit uses right-continuity. We have proved \[ \{u:Q(u)\le x\}=(0,F(x)]\cap(0,1). \] This also proves measurability of \(Q\). For \(U\sim\lambda_{(0,1)}\), \[ \mathbb P(Q(U)\le x)=\lambda((0,F(x)]\cap(0,1))=F(x)=\mu((-\infty,x]). \] The half-lines determine a Borel probability on \(\mathbb R\), so \(Q_\#\lambda=\mu\).

The quantile coupling

Definition. The monotone or comonotone coupling of \(\mu,\nu\) is \[ \pi^\uparrow=(F_\mu^{-1},F_\nu^{-1})_\#\lambda_{(0,1)}. \] If \(\mu\) has no atoms, the monotone transport map is \[ T(x)=F_\nu^{-1}(F_\mu(x)), \qquad T_\#\mu=\nu. \]

When \(\mu\) has atoms, the formula \(F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu\) may fail to split them and need not push \(\mu\) to \(\nu\). The quantile coupling remains valid because the auxiliary uniform variable supplies the required randomization inside jumps.

The uncrossing inequality

Monge inequality. Let \(h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be convex. If \(x_1\le x_2\) and \(y_1\le y_2\), then \[ h(x_1-y_1)+h(x_2-y_2) \le h(x_1-y_2)+h(x_2-y_1). \]

Proof. Define \[ a=x_1-y_2,\quad b=x_1-y_1,\quad c=x_2-y_2,\quad d=x_2-y_1. \] The order assumptions give \(a\le b,c\le d\), and direct addition gives \(b+c=a+d\). If \(a=d\), all four coincide and the assertion is equality. Otherwise set \(\theta=(b-a)/(d-a)\in[0,1]\); then \[ b=(1-\theta)a+\theta d,\qquad c=\theta a+(1-\theta)d. \] By the definition of convexity, \[ \begin{aligned} h(b)+h(c) &\le[(1-\theta)h(a)+\theta h(d)]+[\theta h(a)+(1-\theta)h(d)]\\ &=h(a)+h(d). \end{aligned} \] Substituting \(a,b,c,d\) gives the desired inequality. If \(x_1<x_2\), \(y_1<y_2\), then \[ 0<\theta=\frac{y_2-y_1}{(x_2-x_1)+(y_2-y_1)}<1. \] For strictly convex \(h\), both convexity inequalities are strict.

The right-hand side pairs the smaller source with the larger target and the larger source with the smaller target. The lemma says that removing such a crossing cannot increase any convex displacement cost.

Optimality of monotone rearrangement

Monotone rearrangement theorem. Let \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R)\). For \(c(x,y)=|x-y|^p\), the quantile coupling minimizes the cost. For \(p>1\) it is the unique optimal coupling. If in addition \(\mu\) has no atoms, it is induced by \(T=F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu\).

Proof. Write \(Q_\eta=F_\eta^{-1}\).

1. Finite laws. Sort equal-weight atoms as \(x_1\le\cdots\le x_N\), \(y_1\le\cdots\le y_N\). By Birkhoff, a minimizing coupling can be chosen as a permutation. If adjacent positions satisfy \(\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)\), interchange these two values to obtain \(\sigma'\). The Monge inequality gives \[ \begin{aligned} &|x_i-y_{\sigma'(i)}|^p+|x_{i+1}-y_{\sigma'(i+1)}|^p\\ &\hspace{1em}\le |x_i-y_{\sigma(i)}|^p+|x_{i+1}-y_{\sigma(i+1)}|^p. \end{aligned} \] The inversion count \(\#\{i<j:\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\}\) falls by one. After finitely many steps it is zero, giving the sorted matching. Repeating atoms reduces rational masses to equal weights.

For arbitrary finite masses and any coupling matrix \(P\), choose rational probability matrices \(P^{(k)}\to P\) entrywise. For example, enumerate its \(M\) entries, round the first \(M-1\) down to multiples of \(1/k\), and assign the remaining mass to the last entry. Their row and column masses \(a^{(k)},b^{(k)}\) converge to \(a,b\). Outside the finitely many limiting cumulative-mass breakpoints, \[ Q_{a^{(k)}}(u)\to Q_a(u),\qquad Q_{b^{(k)}}(u)\to Q_b(u). \] All values lie in fixed finite supports, so the integrands are bounded by \(\max_{i,j}|x_i-y_j|^p\). Dominated convergence gives \[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^1|Q_a-Q_b|^p\,\mathrm{d}u &=\lim_k\int_0^1|Q_{a^{(k)}}-Q_{b^{(k)}}|^p\,\mathrm{d}u\\ &\le\lim_k\sum_{ij}P^{(k)}_{ij}|x_i-y_j|^p =\sum_{ij}P_{ij}|x_i-y_j|^p. \end{aligned} \]

2. General laws. Let \[ q_n(x)=2^{-n}\left\lfloor2^n\max(-n,\min(x,n))\right\rfloor. \] This nondecreasing map has finite range and satisfies \[ |q_n(x)-x|\le2^{-n}+|x|\mathbf1_{\{|x|>n\}}, \quad \int|q_n(x)-x|^p\,\mathrm{d}\eta \le2^{p-1}\left(2^{-np}+\int_{|x|>n}|x|^p\,\mathrm{d}\eta\right)\to0 \] for \(\eta=\mu,\nu\); the tail tends to zero by dominated convergence, dominated by \(|x|^p\in L^1(\eta)\). A nondecreasing measurable \(h\) on \((0,1)\) equals the quantile of \(h_\#\lambda\) almost everywhere: both have initial intervals as sublevel sets, of the same lengths, so these sets agree almost everywhere for every rational level; their countable union identifies the functions. Apply this to \(h=q_n\circ Q_\eta\). For any \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), the finite-law result gives \[ \|q_n(Q_\mu)-q_n(Q_\nu)\|_{L^p(0,1)} \le\|q_n(x)-q_n(y)\|_{L^p(\pi)}. \] Put \(e_n(\eta)=\|q_n-\operatorname{id}\|_{L^p(\eta)}\to0\). The reverse triangle inequality and the quantile representation imply \[ \begin{aligned} \left|\|q_n(Q_\mu)-q_n(Q_\nu)\|_p-\|Q_\mu-Q_\nu\|_p\right|&\le e_n(\mu)+e_n(\nu),\\ \left|\|q_n(x)-q_n(y)\|_{L^p(\pi)}-\|x-y\|_{L^p(\pi)}\right|&\le e_n(\mu)+e_n(\nu). \end{aligned} \] Taking limits yields \(\|Q_\mu-Q_\nu\|_p\le\|x-y\|_{L^p(\pi)}\) for every coupling. The quantile coupling attains the left side, proving optimality.

3. Uniqueness for \(p>1\). If the support of an optimal \(\pi\) contains \(x_1<x_2\), \(y_1>y_2\), strict uncrossing and continuity give disjoint bounded neighborhoods \(U_1,U_2\), \(m_i=\pi(U_i)>0\), and \(\delta>0\) such that \[ |a_1-b_1|^p+|a_2-b_2|^p-|a_1-b_2|^p-|a_2-b_1|^p\ge\delta \] for \((a_i,b_i)\in U_i\). Put \(\gamma_i=\pi|_{U_i}/m_i\), \(Q=\gamma_1\otimes\gamma_2\), and let \(\beta_1,\beta_2\) be the \(Q\)-laws of \((a_1,b_2)\), \((a_2,b_1)\). For \(0<\varepsilon\le\min(m_1,m_2)\), \[ \pi'=\pi-\varepsilon(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)+\varepsilon(\beta_1+\beta_2)\ge0. \] Its source marginals are unchanged because \((\beta_i)_X=(\gamma_i)_X\), and its target marginals are unchanged because \((\beta_1)_Y=(\gamma_2)_Y\), \((\beta_2)_Y=(\gamma_1)_Y\). Thus \(\pi'\) is feasible, but \[ \int|x-y|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi'-\int|x-y|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi \le-\varepsilon\delta<0, \] a contradiction. The support is monotone.

Write \(H(x,y)=\pi(( -\infty,x]\times(-\infty,y])\). The two crossing regions cannot both have positive mass. Their masses give \[ (F_\mu(x)-H(x,y))(F_\nu(y)-H(x,y))=0,\qquad H(x,y)\le\min(F_\mu(x),F_\nu(y)). \] Therefore \(H(x,y)=\min(F_\mu(x),F_\nu(y))\). This is exactly the quantile joint distribution, since \[ \lambda\{u:Q_\mu(u)\le x,\ Q_\nu(u)\le y\} =\lambda\{u:u\le\min(F_\mu(x),F_\nu(y))\}. \] Joint distribution functions determine the plan, proving uniqueness.

4. The map when \(\mu\) is atomless. Then \(F_\mu\) is continuous. The inverse definition and continuity give, for \(0<u<1\), \[ F_\mu(Q_\mu(u))\ge u,\qquad F_\mu(Q_\mu(u)-1/k)<u\quad(k\ge1), \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_\mu(Q_\mu(u))=u. \] With \(X=Q_\mu(U)\), \(Y=Q_\nu(U)\), it follows that \[ Y=Q_\nu(U)=Q_\nu(F_\mu(X))=T(X)\quad\text{a.s.} \] Thus \(\pi^\uparrow=(\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu\). Define \(T\) arbitrarily where \(F_\mu(x)\notin(0,1)\); that set has \(\mu\)-measure zero.

The same ordering principle extends to other convex displacement costs with suitable integrability. We use the power-cost version so every displayed value is finite. For \(p=1\), uniqueness can fail: if all source points lie to the left of all target points, every coupling has the same linear cost.

Explicit Wasserstein formulas

Corollary. For \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R)\), \[ W_p^p(\mu,\nu) =\int_0^1|F_\mu^{-1}(u)-F_\nu^{-1}(u)|^p\,\mathrm{d}u. \] For compactly supported measures, \[ W_\infty(\mu,\nu) =\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{0<u<1} |F_\mu^{-1}(u)-F_\nu^{-1}(u)|. \]

Proof of the \(W_\infty\) formula. By definition \(W_\infty=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\|x-y\|_{L^\infty(\pi)}\). Put \(h=|Q_\mu-Q_\nu|\), which is bounded under compact support. For every coupling and finite \(p\), \[ \|h\|_{L^p(0,1)}=W_p(\mu,\nu) \le\|x-y\|_{L^p(\pi)}\le\|x-y\|_{L^\infty(\pi)}. \] If \(M=\|h\|_\infty\), then \(\|h\|_p\le M\). For every \(0\le a<M\), \(\delta_a=\lambda(h>a)>0\), and \[ \|h\|_p\ge a\delta_a^{1/p}\longrightarrow a. \] Letting \(a\uparrow M\) proves \(\|h\|_p\to M\). Thus \(M\le\|x-y\|_{L^\infty(\pi)}\) for every \(\pi\), and \(M\le W_\infty\). Conversely the quantile coupling satisfies \(\|x-y\|_{L^\infty(\pi^\uparrow)}=M\), so \(W_\infty\le M\).

Gaussian laws. If \(\mu=N(m_0,\sigma_0^2)\) and \(\nu=N(m_1,\sigma_1^2)\) with \(\sigma_0,\sigma_1>0\), then \[ T(x)=m_1+\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_0}(x-m_0), \qquad W_2^2(\mu,\nu)=(m_0-m_1)^2+(\sigma_0-\sigma_1)^2. \] Indeed, with \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), the quantile coupling is \(X=m_0+\sigma_0Z\), \(Y=m_1+\sigma_1Z\). Therefore \[ \mathbb E|X-Y|^2=(m_0-m_1)^2+2(m_0-m_1)(\sigma_0-\sigma_1)\mathbb EZ+(\sigma_0-\sigma_1)^2\mathbb EZ^2, \] and \(\mathbb EZ=0\), \(\mathbb EZ^2=1\) give the formula.

Empirical measures. If \[ \mu_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{x_i},\qquad \nu_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{y_i}, \] and \(x_{(1)}\le\cdots\le x_{(n)}\), \(y_{(1)}\le\cdots\le y_{(n)}\), then \[ W_p^p(\mu_n,\nu_n)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n|x_{(i)}-y_{(i)}|^p. \] Thus one-dimensional empirical transport reduces to sorting.

A running example: uniform dilation

From a map to an optimality certificate. Take \(\mu=\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]\) and \(\nu=\operatorname{Unif}[0,2]\). Equal quantiles give \(T(x)=2x\). For cost \(c(x,y)=|x-y|^2/2\), its cost is \(1/6\). The potentials \[ \varphi(x)=-x^2/2,\qquad\psi(y)=y^2/4 \] satisfy \[ c(x,y)-\varphi(x)-\psi(y)=(x-y/2)^2\ge0. \] Equality holds on \(y=2x\), and their integrals sum to \(-1/6+1/3=1/6\). Thus the same example has a quantile solution, a convex-gradient map, and a matching primal–dual certificate. Later its interpolation is uniform on \([0,1+t]\), with velocity \(v_t(z)=z/(1+t)\).

Geodesics on the line

The quantile representation also linearizes Wasserstein geodesics: \[ F_{\mu_t}^{-1}(u)=(1-t)F_{\mu_0}^{-1}(u)+tF_{\mu_1}^{-1}(u). \] Consequently \(\mathcal P_2(\mathbb R)\) embeds isometrically as the closed convex cone of nondecreasing functions in \(L^2(0,1)\). This special Hilbertian description explains why one-dimensional barycenters and asymptotics are unusually explicit.

Practice: quantiles, atoms, and sorting

  1. Find \(T\) and \(W_2^2\) from \(\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]\) to \(\operatorname{Unif}[0,2]\).

    Hint and answer

    The quantiles are \(u\) and \(2u\). Thus \(T(x)=2x\) and \(W_2^2=\int_0^1u^2\,\mathrm{d}u=1/3\).

  2. For source \((\delta_0+\delta_1)/2\) and target \((\delta_2+\delta_3)/2\), compare the two matchings for \(p=1\) and \(p=2\).

    Hint and answer

    For \(p=1\), both costs are two. For \(p=2\), sorting costs four, while the crossing matching costs five. Strict convexity distinguishes the latter case.
