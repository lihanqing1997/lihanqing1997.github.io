Learning goal. Prove the metric triangle inequality by gluing and distinguish weak convergence from convergence of transport costs.

A Wasserstein distance does more than assign a transport cost. It equips a space of probability measures with a metric that simultaneously records weak convergence and control of tails. The geometry developed later rests on this topology.

Definition and finiteness

Definition. Let \((X,d)\) be Polish and \(1\le p<\infty\). For \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(X)\), define \[ W_p(\mu,\nu) =\left( \inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)} \int_{X\times X}d(x,y)^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y) \right)^{1/p}. \] An optimizer exists because \(d^p\) is continuous and nonnegative.

Finiteness. For any base point \(x_0\), \[ W_p(\mu,\nu)^p \le 2^{p-1}\left( \int d(x,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu +\int d(y,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\nu \right). \]

Proof. Use the product coupling \(\mu\otimes\nu\) and the inequality \(d(x,y)^p\le2^{p-1}\{d(x,x_0)^p+d(y,x_0)^p\}\).

The metric axioms

Theorem. \(W_p\) is a metric on \(\mathcal P_p(X)\).

Proof. Nonnegativity and symmetry are immediate. If \(W_p(\mu,\nu)=0\), an optimal plan \(\pi\) satisfies \(d(x,y)=0\) almost surely, so it is concentrated on the diagonal and its two marginals coincide.

For the triangle inequality, choose \(\pi_{12}\in\Pi(\mu_1,\mu_2)\) and \(\pi_{23}\in\Pi(\mu_2,\mu_3)\), and glue them to \(\gamma\) on \(X^3\). Its \((1,3)\)-marginal is a coupling of \(\mu_1,\mu_3\). Minkowski's inequality gives \[ \begin{aligned} W_p(\mu_1,\mu_3) &\le\left(\int d(x_1,x_3)^p\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\right)^{1/p}\\ &\le\left(\int d(x_1,x_2)^p\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\right)^{1/p} +\left(\int d(x_2,x_3)^p\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\right)^{1/p}. \end{aligned} \] Taking infima over the two plans proves \(W_p(\mu_1,\mu_3)\le W_p(\mu_1,\mu_2)+W_p(\mu_2,\mu_3)\).

Elementary comparisons

Proposition. If \(1\le p\le q\) and \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_q(X)\), then \[ W_p(\mu,\nu)\le W_q(\mu,\nu). \] Also, \[ W_p(\mu,\delta_{x_0})^p=\int d(x,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x). \]

Proof. For every coupling, the \(L^p\)-norm of \(d(x,y)\) is at most its \(L^q\)-norm; take infima. A coupling with \(\delta_{x_0}\) is unique, namely \(\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\delta_{x_0}(\mathrm{d}y)\), giving the second formula.

On a space of finite diameter \(D\), interpolation gives \(W_q^q\le D^{q-p}W_p^p\). On unbounded spaces no reverse comparison is possible without stronger moment bounds.

Characterization of \(W_p\)-convergence

Topology theorem. For \(\mu_n,\mu\in\mathcal P_p(X)\), the following are equivalent:

(i) \(W_p(\mu_n,\mu)\to0\).

(ii) \(\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu\) and, for one base point \(x_0\), \[ \int d(x,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n(x) \to \int d(x,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x). \]

(iii) \(\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu\) and the \(p\)-th moments are uniformly integrable: \[ \lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_n \int_{\{d(x,x_0)>R\}}d(x,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n(x)=0. \]

Proof. Write \(r(x)=d(x,x_0)\).

(i) implies (ii). Choose couplings \((X_n,X)\) with \(\mathbb E d(X_n,X)^p\le W_p(\mu_n,\mu)^p+1/n\). They may be put on one probability space by disintegrating each over the common law of \(X\) and taking the product of the conditional kernels. Then \(d(X_n,X)\to0\) in probability, so \(X_n\Rightarrow X\). Moreover, \[ \left|\|r(X_n)\|_p-\|r(X)\|_p\right| \le\|r(X_n)-r(X)\|_p\le\|d(X_n,X)\|_p\longrightarrow0. \] Taking \(p\)-th powers yields convergence of moments.

(ii) implies (iii) and (i). The Skorokhod representation theorem for Polish spaces gives variables with these laws and \(X_n\to X\) almost surely. Put \(Y_n=r(X_n)^p\), \(Y=r(X)^p\). Then \(Y_n\to Y\) almost surely and \(\mathbb EY_n\to\mathbb EY\). Since \(\min(Y_n,Y)\le Y\), dominated convergence gives \[ \mathbb E|Y_n-Y|=\mathbb EY_n+\mathbb EY-2\mathbb E\min(Y_n,Y)\longrightarrow0. \] An \(L^1\)-convergent sequence is uniformly integrable, which is (iii). Also \[ 0\le d(X_n,X)^p\le2^{p-1}(Y_n+Y). \] The right side is uniformly integrable and the left side tends to zero almost surely. Vitali's theorem gives \(\mathbb E d(X_n,X)^p\to0\), hence (i).

(iii) implies (ii). For \(f_R(x)=\min(r(x)^p,R)\), weak convergence gives \(\int f_R\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n\to\int f_R\,\mathrm{d}\mu\). The errors satisfy \[ 0\le\int(r^p-f_R)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n \le\int_{\{r^p>R\}}r^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n, \] uniformly vanishing as \(R\to\infty\). The same tail bound for \(\mu\) follows from its finite moment. First let \(n\to\infty\), then \(R\to\infty\). This proves (ii).

Completeness and separability

Theorem. If \((X,d)\) is complete and separable, then \((\mathcal P_p(X),W_p)\) is complete and separable.

Proof. Completeness. From a Cauchy sequence extract \(\mu_{n_k}\) with \(W_p(\mu_{n_k},\mu_{n_{k+1}})\le2^{-k}\). Optimal successive couplings can be glued on a single space: disintegrate them into transition kernels and use the countable product construction for kernels. Thus there are \(X_k\sim\mu_{n_k}\) with \(\|d(X_k,X_{k+1})\|_p\le2^{-k}\). By Tonelli and \(\|\cdot\|_1\le\|\cdot\|_p\), \[ \mathbb E\sum_{k=1}^\infty d(X_k,X_{k+1})\le\sum_{k=1}^\infty2^{-k}<\infty. \] The sum is finite almost surely, so \(X_k\) is Cauchy almost surely. Completeness of \(X\) gives a measurable limit \(X_\infty\). For each \(k\), Fatou followed by Minkowski gives \[ \|d(X_k,X_\infty)\|_p \le\liminf_{\ell\to\infty}\|d(X_k,X_\ell)\|_p \le\sum_{j=k}^\infty2^{-j}=2^{1-k}. \] In particular, \(\|d(X_\infty,x_0)\|_p\le\|d(X_1,x_0)\|_p+1<\infty\). Thus its law belongs to \(\mathcal P_p\) and is the \(W_p\)-limit of the subsequence. For the whole sequence use \(W_p(\mu_n,\mathcal L(X_\infty))\le W_p(\mu_n,\mu_{n_k})+2^{1-k}\), first choosing \(k\) and then \(n\).

Separability. Enumerate a countable dense subset as \(D=\{x_1,x_2,\ldots\}\). Let \(q_N(x)\) be a nearest point among \(x_1,\ldots,x_N\), breaking ties by the smallest index. These maps are Borel, \(d(x,q_N(x))\to0\), and \(d(x,q_N(x))\le d(x,x_1)\). Dominated convergence gives \[ W_p(\mu,q_{N\#}\mu)^p\le\int d(x,q_N(x))^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu\longrightarrow0. \] On a fixed finite support of diameter \(D_N\), changing the weight vector from \(a\) to \(b\) costs at most \(D_N^p\|a-b\|_1/2\): keep the common mass at each point and couple the remaining equal masses arbitrarily. Rational weights therefore approximate \(q_{N\#}\mu\). All finite rationally weighted laws on \(D\) form the required countable dense set.

Relative compactness

Compactness criterion. A family \(\mathcal K\subset\mathcal P_p(X)\) is relatively compact in \(W_p\) if it is tight and its \(p\)-th moments are uniformly integrable. On a proper metric space, a uniform bound on a moment of order \(q>p\) implies these conditions.

Proof. Tightness and Prokhorov give a weakly convergent subsequence from any sequence in \(\mathcal K\). Uniform integrability passes to the limit through bounded continuous truncations of \(r^p\) and implies convergence of moments, by the same truncation argument as above. The topology theorem upgrades this subsequence to \(W_p\)-convergence. If \(\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal K}\int r^q\,\mathrm{d}\mu=M<\infty\), then \(\mu(r>R)\le M/R^q\) and \(\int_{r>R}r^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu\le M/R^{q-p}\). On a proper space closed balls are compact, so these are respectively tightness and uniform integrability.

The extra tail condition is essential. Weak compactness alone permits a small amount of mass to escape to infinity while carrying nonvanishing \(p\)-transport cost, as the example on Existence and stability of optimal plans showed.

Bounded moments are not uniform integrability. On \(\mathbb R\), let \[ \mu_n=(1-n^{-2})\delta_0+n^{-2}\delta_n. \] Then \(\mu_n\Rightarrow\delta_0\) and \(\int x^2\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n=1\), but \(W_2^2(\mu_n,\delta_0)=1\). Every subsequence has the same weak limit, so none converges in \(W_2\). A second-moment bound gives tightness on Euclidean space, not \(W_2\)-compactness.

The \(W_\infty\) distance

For bounded-support measures one may also define \[ W_\infty(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)} \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{(x,y)\sim\pi}d(x,y). \] It minimizes the largest displacement rather than an average displacement. It is the limit of \(W_p\) as \(p\to\infty\) under suitable boundedness assumptions, but its topology and geodesics can be more rigid.

Sources and proof details. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport, §2.1, for topology, completeness, and compactness.

Practice: tails and topology

  1. For \(\mu_n=(1-n^{-q})\delta_0+n^{-q}\delta_n\), when does \(W_p(\mu_n,\delta_0)\to0\)?

    Hint and answer

    The \(p\)-th power is \(n^{p-q}\). It tends to zero exactly when \(q>p\), although weak convergence holds for every \(q>0\).

  2. Why does a uniform \((p+\eta)\)-moment bound imply uniformly integrable \(p\)-th moments?

    Hint and answer

    On \(|x|>R\), \(|x|^p\le R^{-\eta}|x|^{p+\eta}\). Integrate and take the supremum over the family.
