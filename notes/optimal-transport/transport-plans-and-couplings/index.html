Learning goal. Construct pushforwards and finite couplings; distinguish moving an atom from splitting it. Use compactness and disintegration to prepare the existence and map theorems that follow.

Optimal transport compares two distributions by placing them on a common probability space. Before a cost is minimized, one must understand the three operations that make this possible: moving a measure through a map, joining two measures by a coupling, and composing couplings through a common marginal.

Probability measures on Polish spaces

Unless explicitly specialized, \(X\) and \(Y\) are Polish spaces (their topology admits a complete separable metric) with their Borel \(\sigma\)-fields. This assumption is broad enough for Euclidean spaces, separable Banach spaces, manifolds, path spaces, and countable discrete spaces, while still providing tightness and regular conditional probabilities.

Definition. Write \(\mathcal P(X)\) for the Borel probability measures on \(X\). If \(d_X\) is a complete compatible metric and \(p\ge1\), define \[ \mathcal P_p(X)=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal P(X):\int_X d_X(x,x_0)^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)<\infty\right\}. \] The definition does not depend on the base point \(x_0\), because \(d_X(x,x_1)^p\le 2^{p-1}\{d_X(x,x_0)^p+d_X(x_0,x_1)^p\}\).

Weak convergence \(\mu_n\Rightarrow\mu\) means \(\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_n\to\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\) for every bounded continuous \(f\). Moment conditions will later strengthen weak convergence into Wasserstein convergence.

Pushforward measures

Definition. If \(T:X\to Y\) is Borel measurable and \(\mu\in\mathcal P(X)\), the pushforward \(T_\#\mu\in\mathcal P(Y)\) is \[ (T_\#\mu)(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B)),\qquad B\in\mathcal B(Y). \] Equivalently, \(T_\#\mu\) is the law of \(T(U)\) whenever \(U\sim\mu\).

Change-of-variables identity. For every nonnegative Borel function \(g:Y\to[0,\infty]\), and for every \(T_\#\mu\)-integrable \(g\), \[ \int_Y g(y)\,\mathrm{d}(T_\#\mu)(y)=\int_X g(T(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x). \]

Proof. Put \(\nu=T_\#\mu\). We extend the defining identity from sets to functions in four steps.

1. Indicators. For \(B\in\mathcal B(Y)\), \(\mathbf1_B(T(x))=\mathbf1_{T^{-1}(B)}(x)\). Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \int_Y\mathbf1_B\,\mathrm{d}\nu &=\nu(B)=\mu(T^{-1}(B))\\ &=\int_X\mathbf1_{T^{-1}(B)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu =\int_X\mathbf1_B\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu. \end{aligned} \]

2. Nonnegative simple functions. For \(s=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\mathbf1_{B_j}\), where \(a_j\ge0\) and \(B_j\) are Borel, finite linearity gives \[ \begin{aligned} \int_Ys\,\mathrm{d}\nu &=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\int_Y\mathbf1_{B_j}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\\ &=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\int_X\mathbf1_{B_j}\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu =\int_Xs\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu. \end{aligned} \]

3. Nonnegative Borel functions. For \(g:Y\to[0,\infty]\), set \[ g_n(y)=2^{-n}\left\lfloor2^n\min\{g(y),n\}\right\rfloor. \] Each \(g_n\) is a nonnegative Borel simple function. The dyadic grids are nested, so \[ 0\le g_n\le g_{n+1},\qquad 0\le\min\{g,n\}-g_n<2^{-n}. \] Consequently \(g_n\uparrow g\) pointwise on \(Y\) and \(g_n\circ T\uparrow g\circ T\) pointwise on \(X\). The compositions are measurable because \(T\) is measurable. The monotone convergence theorem applies to both sequences: \[ \begin{aligned} \int_Yg\,\mathrm{d}\nu &=\lim_n\int_Yg_n\,\mathrm{d}\nu &&\text{(monotone convergence)}\\ &=\lim_n\int_Xg_n\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu &&\text{(Step 2)}\\ &=\int_Xg\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu &&\text{(monotone convergence).} \end{aligned} \] These equalities are valid even when the common value is \(+\infty\).

4. Integrable signed functions. Suppose \(\int_Y|g|\,\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty\). Step 3 first gives \[ \int_X|g\circ T|\,\mathrm{d}\mu =\int_X|g|\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu =\int_Y|g|\,\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty. \] Thus both sides of the desired identity are defined. With \(g^+=\max(g,0)\), \(g^-=\max(-g,0)\), all integrals in the following subtraction are finite: \[ \begin{aligned} \int_Yg\,\mathrm{d}\nu &=\int_Yg^+\,\mathrm{d}\nu-\int_Yg^-\,\mathrm{d}\nu\\ &=\int_Xg^+\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_Xg^-\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu\\ &=\int_X(g^+-g^-)\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu =\int_Xg\circ T\,\mathrm{d}\mu. \end{aligned} \]

Example. If \(X=Y=\mathbb R^d\), \(T(x)=Ax+b\), and \(U\sim N(m,\Sigma)\), then \(T_\#N(m,\Sigma)=N(Am+b,A\Sigma A^\top)\). If \(\mu\) has density \(f\) and \(T\) is a \(C^1\)-diffeomorphism, then \(T_\#\mu\) has density \(g(y)=f(T^{-1}y)|\det DT(T^{-1}y)|^{-1}\). The Monge--Ampère equation will arise by applying this formula to \(T=\nabla\varphi\).

Couplings and transport plans

Definition. A coupling of \(\mu\in\mathcal P(X)\) and \(\nu\in\mathcal P(Y)\) is a probability measure \(\pi\in\mathcal P(X\times Y)\) whose marginals are \(\mu\) and \(\nu\): \[ (\operatorname{pr}_X)_\#\pi=\mu, \qquad (\operatorname{pr}_Y)_\#\pi=\nu. \] The set of all such measures is denoted \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\). In optimal transport, a coupling is also called a transport plan.

The set \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) is never empty because it contains the independent coupling \(\mu\otimes\nu\). A coupling records dependence while leaving the two marginal laws fixed. Thus choosing a transport plan is exactly choosing which source points are paired with which target points.

Proposition. If \(X,Y\) are Polish, then \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) is convex and tight. It is also closed under weak convergence; consequently it is weakly compact.

Proof. For \(\pi_0,\pi_1\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) and \(t\in[0,1]\), put \(\pi_t=(1-t)\pi_0+t\pi_1\). For Borel \(A\subset X\), \(B\subset Y\), \[ \pi_t(A\times Y)=(1-t)\mu(A)+t\mu(A)=\mu(A),\qquad \pi_t(X\times B)=\nu(B). \] Hence \(\pi_t\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), proving convexity.

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), tightness of the two probabilities on Polish spaces gives compact \(K_X,K_Y\) with \(\mu(K_X^c),\nu(K_Y^c)\le\varepsilon/2\). Uniformly over \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), \[ \begin{aligned} \pi((K_X\times K_Y)^c) &\le\pi(K_X^c\times Y)+\pi(X\times K_Y^c)\\ &=\mu(K_X^c)+\nu(K_Y^c)\le\varepsilon. \end{aligned} \] Since \(K_X\times K_Y\) is compact, this is tightness of the entire family.

If \(\pi_n\Rightarrow\pi\), then for every \(f\in C_b(X)\), \(f\circ\operatorname{pr}_X\in C_b(X\times Y)\), and \[ \int_Xf\,\mathrm{d}(\operatorname{pr}_X)_\#\pi =\int_{X\times Y}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi =\lim_n\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_n =\int_Xf\,\mathrm{d}\mu. \] Bounded continuous functions determine Borel probabilities on Polish spaces, so \((\operatorname{pr}_X)_\#\pi=\mu\). The identical computation with \(g\in C_b(Y)\) gives the second marginal \(\nu\). Thus the family is weakly closed. Prokhorov's theorem makes a tight family relatively weakly compact; closedness makes \(\Pi(\mu,\nu)\) weakly compact.

Maps, kernels, and disintegration

A map sends all mass located at \(x\) to one point. A general plan may split that mass among several destinations. Disintegration makes this distinction exact.

Disintegration theorem (probability input). For every \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), there exists a \(\mu\)-almost surely unique measurable probability kernel \(x\mapsto\pi_x\in\mathcal P(Y)\) such that \[ \pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\pi_x(\mathrm{d}y), \qquad \nu(B)=\int_X\pi_x(B)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x). \] Moreover, \(\pi\) is induced by a measurable map \(T:X\to Y\), meaning \(\pi=(\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu\), if and only if \(\pi_x=\delta_{T(x)}\) for \(\mu\)-almost every \(x\).

The kernel \(\pi_x\) is the conditional law of the destination given the source \(x\). Deterministic plans are therefore precisely the couplings whose conditional laws are Dirac masses.

Verification of the map criterion. Suppose \(\pi=(\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu\). For Borel \(A\subset X,B\subset Y\), \[ \begin{aligned} \pi(A\times B) &=\int_X\mathbf1_A(x)\mathbf1_B(T(x))\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\\ &=\int_A\delta_{T(x)}(B)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x). \end{aligned} \] The kernel \(x\mapsto\delta_{T(x)}\) is measurable, so uniqueness in the disintegration theorem implies \(\pi_x=\delta_{T(x)}\) for \(\mu\)-almost every \(x\).

Conversely, write \(k(x)=\pi_x\), viewed as a measurable map into \(\mathcal P(Y)\). The map \(\iota:Y\to\mathcal P(Y)\), \(\iota(y)=\delta_y\), is continuous and injective. The standard-Borel inverse theorem says that \(D=\iota(Y)\) is Borel and \(\iota^{-1}:D\to Y\) is measurable. Thus \(E=k^{-1}(D)\) is measurable; the Dirac assumption says \(\mu(E)=1\). Fix \(y_0\in Y\) and define \[ T(x)=\begin{cases}\iota^{-1}(k(x)),&x\in E,\\y_0,&x\notin E.\end{cases} \] This map is measurable. For every Borel rectangle, \[ \begin{aligned} \pi(A\times B) &=\int_Ak(x)(B)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\\ &=\int_A\delta_{T(x)}(B)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x) =((\operatorname{id},T)_\#\mu)(A\times B). \end{aligned} \] The two probability measures agree on the rectangle \(\pi\)-system, which generates \(\mathcal B(X\times Y)\); the uniqueness theorem for measures gives equality everywhere. Disintegration and the standard-Borel inverse theorem are the probability inputs used here.

Example. Let \(\mu=\delta_0\) and \(\nu=\tfrac12(\delta_{-1}+\delta_1)\). There is one coupling, \(\pi=\delta_0\otimes\nu\), and its conditional law at \(0\) is \(\nu\). No map \(T\) can satisfy \(T_\#\mu=\nu\), because the image of a Dirac mass under a map is another Dirac mass. This is the simplest reason the Kantorovich relaxation is needed.

The gluing lemma

The triangle inequality for Wasserstein distance and the composition of stochastic transports both rely on the following construction.

Gluing lemma. Let \(\pi_{12}\in\Pi(\mu_1,\mu_2)\) and \(\pi_{23}\in\Pi(\mu_2,\mu_3)\) on Polish spaces \(X_1,X_2,X_3\). There exists \(\gamma\in\mathcal P(X_1\times X_2\times X_3)\) whose \((1,2)\)-marginal is \(\pi_{12}\) and whose \((2,3)\)-marginal is \(\pi_{23}\).

Proof. Disintegration over the common marginal provides probability kernels \(K_{21},K_{23}\) with \[ \pi_{12}(\mathrm{d}x_1,\mathrm{d}x_2)=\mu_2(\mathrm{d}x_2)K_{21}(x_2,\mathrm{d}x_1),\qquad \pi_{23}(\mathrm{d}x_2,\mathrm{d}x_3)=\mu_2(\mathrm{d}x_2)K_{23}(x_2,\mathrm{d}x_3). \] Define the product-kernel measure by its nonnegative integrals: \[ \int f\,\mathrm{d}\gamma =\int_{X_2}\int_{X_1}\int_{X_3} f(x_1,x_2,x_3)\,K_{23}(x_2,\mathrm{d}x_3)K_{21}(x_2,\mathrm{d}x_1)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_2(x_2). \] Measurability and countable additivity follow from the kernel integration theorem and monotone convergence. In particular, \[ \gamma(X_1\times X_2\times X_3) =\int_{X_2}K_{21}(x_2,X_1)K_{23}(x_2,X_3)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_2=1. \] For Borel \(A_i\subset X_i\), the marginal identities are \[ \begin{aligned} \gamma(A_1\times A_2\times X_3) &=\int_{A_2}K_{21}(x_2,A_1)\underbrace{K_{23}(x_2,X_3)}_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_2 =\pi_{12}(A_1\times A_2),\\ \gamma(X_1\times A_2\times A_3) &=\int_{A_2}\underbrace{K_{21}(x_2,X_1)}_{1}K_{23}(x_2,A_3)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_2 =\pi_{23}(A_2\times A_3). \end{aligned} \] Agreement on rectangles determines both marginals. The construction makes \(x_1,x_3\) conditionally independent given \(x_2\).

A finite coupling polytope

Example. If \(X=\{x_1,x_2\}\), \(Y=\{y_1,y_2\}\), \(\mu=(a,1-a)\), and \(\nu=(b,1-b)\), every coupling has matrix \[ P(t)= \begin{pmatrix} t & a-t\\ b-t & 1-a-b+t \end{pmatrix}, \] \[ \max(0,a+b-1)\le t\le\min(a,b). \] The interval of admissible \(t\)'s is the two-by-two transport polytope. A linear transport cost selects one of its endpoints unless the cost is parallel to the interval. This finite picture already contains convexity, nonuniqueness, and the distinction between marginals and dependence.

Practice: couplings and mass splitting

  1. For \(a=b=1/2\), write all two-by-two couplings. Which are graph plans?

    Hint and answer

    The parameter is \(0\le t\le1/2\). At the two endpoints the conditional destinations are deterministic; every interior value splits both atoms.

  2. Let \(X\) be uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(T(x)=x^2\). Find the density of \(T(X)\).

    Hint and answer

    For \(0<y<1\), the distribution function is \(\sqrt y\), so the density is \(1/(2\sqrt y)\). Its integral is one; a pushforward need not preserve a uniform density.
