Learning goal. Derive the Jacobian equation and explain why the target image condition is part of the problem.

Brenier's theorem turns quadratic transport into a convex potential \(u\). When the measures have densities, the pushforward condition becomes a nonlinear partial differential equation for \(u\). Its determinant measures how the gradient map changes volume.

Derivation from change of variables

Jacobian equation. Let \(\mu=f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) on a domain \(\Omega\), \(\nu=g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\) on \(\Lambda\), and suppose \(T:\Omega\to\Lambda\) is a \(C^1\)-diffeomorphism with \(T_\#\mu=\nu\). Then \[ f(x)=g(T(x))|\det DT(x)| \] for almost every \(x\). If \(T=\nabla u\) with \(u\) convex, \(D^2u>0\), and \(g>0\) almost everywhere on \(\Lambda\), then \[ \boxed{\det D^2u(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(\nabla u(x))}}, \qquad \nabla u(\Omega)=\Lambda. \]

Proof. The pushforward identity gives, for bounded Borel \(h\), \[ \int_\Lambda h(y)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_\Omega h(T(x))f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x. \] The ordinary change-of-variables theorem for the diffeomorphism \(T\) also gives \[ \int_\Lambda h(y)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y =\int_\Omega h(T(x))g(T(x))|\det DT(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x. \] For any Borel \(A\subset\Omega\), take \(h=\mathbf1_A\circ T^{-1}\); this is Borel since \(T^{-1}\) is continuous. Subtraction yields \[ \int_A\bigl[f(x)-g(T(x))|\det DT(x)|\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}x=0\quad(\forall A). \] Both functions are integrable densities. Choosing the sets where their difference is positive and negative proves equality almost everywhere. If \(T=\nabla u\), then \(DT=D^2u>0\), so \(|\det DT|=\det D^2u\). At points where \(g(\nabla u)>0\), division gives \[ \det D^2u=\frac{f}{g\circ\nabla u}. \] Finally \(T:\Omega\to\Lambda\) is onto by hypothesis, hence \(\nabla u(\Omega)=\Lambda\). This is the transport image condition.

Ellipticity and convexity

The operator \(u\mapsto\det D^2u\) is elliptic only on convex functions. Indeed, differentiating the determinant at a positive-definite matrix \(A\) in direction \(H\) gives \[ D(\det)_A[H]=\operatorname{cof}(A):H =\det(A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}H), \] whose coefficient matrix \(\operatorname{cof}(A)\) is positive definite. Convexity is therefore both the transport structure and the ellipticity condition.

Linearized Monge--Ampère operator. At a smooth strictly convex solution \(u\), the linearization of \(\det D^2u=F\) is \[ L_uv=\operatorname{cof}(D^2u):D^2v. \] Moreover, each row of \(\operatorname{cof}(D^2u)\) is divergence free, so \[ L_uv=\nabla\cdot(\operatorname{cof}(D^2u)\nabla v). \]

Proof. Assume \(u\in C^3\), \(v\in C^2\), and write \(C=\operatorname{cof}(D^2u)\). For \(A=D^2u\), \(B=D^2v\), the Leibniz polynomial gives \[ \begin{aligned} \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}h}\det(A+hB)\right|_{h=0} &=\sum_{\sigma\in S_d}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \sum_{k=1}^d B_{k,\sigma(k)}\prod_{i\ne k}A_{i,\sigma(i)}\\ &=\sum_{k,j}\operatorname{cof}(A)_{kj}B_{kj}=\sum_{ij}C_{ij}v_{ij}. \end{aligned} \] The second equality collects the terms having \(\sigma(k)=j\); their remaining signed product is precisely the cofactor. For \(d\ge2\), the cofactor has the alternating-symbol expression \[ C_{ij}=\frac1{(d-1)!}\sum_{i_2,\ldots,i_d}\sum_{j_2,\ldots,j_d} \epsilon_{i i_2\cdots i_d}\epsilon_{j j_2\cdots j_d}\prod_{k=2}^d u_{i_kj_k}. \] In \(\sum_i\partial_i C_{ij}\), a derivative hitting the \(k\)-th factor produces \(u_{i i_k j_k}\). Summing over \(i,i_k\) gives zero: this third derivative is symmetric under exchanging the two indices, whereas the first alternating symbol changes sign and all other factors remain unchanged. For the sum \(S_k\) of terms in which the derivative hits factor \(k\), relabel the dummy indices \(i\leftrightarrow i_k\). Then \[ \epsilon_{i i_2\cdots i_k\cdots i_d}\mapsto-\epsilon_{i i_2\cdots i_k\cdots i_d}, \qquad u_{i i_k j_k}=u_{i_k i j_k}, \] while the remaining factors do not involve those two indices. Thus \(S_k=-S_k\), so \(S_k=0\). Summing the zero terms proves proving \(\sum_i\partial_iC_{ij}=0\). In \(d=1\), \(C=1\) and the identity is immediate. The product rule now gives \[ \sum_{ij}\partial_i(C_{ij}\partial_jv)=\sum_{ij}C_{ij}v_{ij}+\sum_j\left(\sum_i\partial_iC_{ij}\right)\partial_jv=L_uv. \] For a Hessian the cofactor matrix is symmetric, so column and row divergence are equivalent.

Alexandrov solutions

Optimal potentials need not be twice differentiable. The correct weak formulation uses the geometry of the subgradient rather than distributional determinants.

Definition. For a convex \(u:\Omega\to\mathbb R\), its Monge--Ampère measure is \[ M_u(E)=\mathcal L^d(\partial u(E)), \qquad E\subset\Omega\ \text{Borel}. \] We call \(u\) an Alexandrov solution of \(\det D^2u=\rho\) if \(M_u(E)=\int_E\rho(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\) for every Borel \(E\).

Consistency. If \(\Omega\) is convex and open and \(u\in C^2(\Omega)\) is strictly convex, then \[ M_u(E)=\int_E\det D^2u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x. \]

Proof. On the convex domain \(\Omega\), differentiability gives \(\partial u(x)=\{\nabla u(x)\}\). Strict convexity makes the gradient injective: if \(x\ne y\) and \(\nabla u(x)=\nabla u(y)=a\), strict supporting-plane inequalities give \[ u(y)>u(x)+a\cdot(y-x),\qquad u(x)>u(y)+a\cdot(x-y), \] whose sum is the contradiction \(0>0\). Thus \(\nabla u\) is injective. It is locally Lipschitz because \(u\in C^2\). The area formula, applied on compact subsets and then by monotone exhaustion of \(\Omega\), gives \[ \int_E|\det D(\nabla u)(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x =\int_{\mathbb R^d}\#\{x\in E:\nabla u(x)=y\}\,\mathrm{d}y =\mathcal L^d(\nabla u(E)). \] The multiplicity is exactly one on the image and zero elsewhere. Since \(D^2u\) is positive semidefinite, its determinant is nonnegative. Hence \[ M_u(E)=\mathcal L^d(\partial u(E))=\mathcal L^d(\nabla u(E)) =\int_E\det D^2u\,\mathrm{d}x. \]

Transport formulation of weak solutions

Weighted Alexandrov formulation (external weak change-of-variables theorem). Let \(\Omega,\Lambda\) be bounded convex open domains, and let \(f,g\) be densities on them bounded above and below by positive constants, with equal mass. The Brenier potential has the transport boundary condition and satisfies \[ \int_{\partial u(E)}g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\int_E f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \quad(E\subset\Omega\text{ Borel}), \] with \(g\) extended by zero outside \(\Lambda\). This weighted subgradient-image identity is the weak meaning of \(g(\nabla u)\det D^2u=f\). Together with \(\partial u(\Omega)\subset\overline\Lambda\) and the full-mass image condition, it characterizes the corresponding convex transport potential up to constants.

The weighted identity avoids evaluating a density at a multivalued subgradient. Its justification uses almost-everywhere invertibility of the forward and reverse Brenier maps and the convex-target localization theory. For a rigorous treatment see Ambrosio–Gigli, §1.4. Under smoothness it reduces to the change-of-variables calculation above. The equation and the target boundary condition must be kept together: a local volume constraint alone does not choose the destination domain.

One-dimensional and radial examples

One dimension. The equation is \[ u''(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(u'(x))}. \] Since \(T=u'\), integration gives \(F_\nu(T(x))=F_\mu(x)\), hence \(T=F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu\). Thus the Monge--Ampère equation reduces exactly to monotone rearrangement.

Radial measures. If \(f,g\) are radial and \(T(x)=t(r)x/r\), mass conservation inside balls gives \[ \int_0^r f_0(s)s^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}s = \int_0^{t(r)}g_0(s)s^{d-1}\,\mathrm{d}s. \] Differentiation yields \[ f_0(r)r^{d-1}=g_0(t(r))t(r)^{d-1}t'(r), \] which equals the determinant formula because the radial and tangential eigenvalues of \(DT\) are \(t'(r)\) and \(t(r)/r\).

Quadratic potentials. For \(u(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Ax+b\cdot x\) with \(A>0\), \(\det D^2u=\det A\). The gradient map sends ellipsoids to ellipsoids and recovers the Gaussian transport matrix from Quadratic transport and Brenier's theorem.

Regularity questions

The equation suggests that smooth positive \(f,g\) should produce a smooth potential, but this is false without geometry. Convexity of the domains, bounds away from zero and infinity, and boundary regularity are essential. The next page separates interior regularity, global regularity, and the additional curvature condition required by general costs.

Sources and proof details. Santambrogio, Optimal transport for applied mathematicians, Chapter 1, for the relation between Brenier potentials and the second boundary-value problem; the regularity chapter that follows explains its geometric hypotheses.

Practice: volume and boundary data

  1. For transport from uniform \([0,1]\) to uniform \([0,2]\), solve the one-dimensional Jacobian equation.

    Hint and answer

    Here \(f=1,g=1/2\), so \(u\,\!\prime\prime=2\). The image condition fixes \(u\,\!\prime(x)=2x\); adding a constant to \(u\) is irrelevant.

  2. Why does \(\det D^2u=1\) alone not specify a unique transport?

    Hint and answer

    In \(d\ge2\), many positive-definite matrices \(A\) have determinant one. The potentials \(x^\top Ax/2\) send a given source to different target shapes; the boundary image chooses the target.
