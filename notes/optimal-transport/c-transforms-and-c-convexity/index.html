Learning goal. Translate touching inequalities into geometric restrictions on the support of an optimal plan.

Duality says that optimal mass moves only where \(\varphi(x)+\psi(y)=c(x,y)\). The \(c\)-transform organizes all such touching pairs, while cyclical monotonicity describes them without mentioning the potentials. Together they are the geometric language of optimality.

The \(c\)-transform

Definition. For finite continuous cost on compact \(X,Y\), and a bounded real function \(\varphi:X\to\mathbb R\), define its \(c\)-transform on \(Y\) by \[ \varphi^c(y)=\inf_{x\in X}\{c(x,y)-\varphi(x)\}. \] For bounded real \(\psi:Y\to\mathbb R\), define \(\psi^c(x)=\inf_y\{c(x,y)-\psi(y)\}\). A function is \(c\)-concave if it equals the \(c\)-transform of some function. Some authors use the sign-reversed term \(c\)-convex; the present note follows the dual inequality \(\varphi+\psi\le c\).

Proposition. The \(c\)-transform reverses order. Moreover, \[ \varphi\le\varphi^{cc}, \qquad \varphi^{ccc}=\varphi^c. \] A function \(\varphi\) is \(c\)-concave exactly when \(\varphi=\varphi^{cc}\), in this bounded compact setting.

Proof. In the bounded compact setting all infima below are finite. If \(\varphi_1\le\varphi_2\), then for every \(y\), \[ \varphi_1^c(y)=\inf_x(c(x,y)-\varphi_1(x)) \ge\inf_x(c(x,y)-\varphi_2(x))=\varphi_2^c(y). \] For every \(x,y\), \[ \varphi^c(y)\le c(x,y)-\varphi(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi(x)\le c(x,y)-\varphi^c(y). \] Taking the infimum over \(y\) gives \(\varphi\le\varphi^{cc}\). Applying the two established facts gives opposite inequalities: \[ \varphi\le\varphi^{cc}\ \Longrightarrow\ \varphi^c\ge\varphi^{ccc}, \qquad \varphi^c\le(\varphi^c)^{cc}=\varphi^{ccc}. \] Thus \(\varphi^{ccc}=\varphi^c\). Finally, \[ \varphi=\psi^c\ \Longrightarrow\ \varphi^{cc}=\psi^{ccc}=\psi^c=\varphi, \qquad \varphi=\varphi^{cc}\ \Longrightarrow\ \varphi=(\varphi^c)^c. \] These are precisely the two directions of the characterization of \(c\)-concavity.

Reduction of the dual problem

Proposition. If \((\varphi,\psi)\) is dual feasible, then \(\psi\le\varphi^c\). Replacing \(\psi\) by \(\varphi^c\), and then \(\varphi\) by \(\varphi^{cc}\), preserves feasibility and cannot decrease the dual objective. Consequently the dual problem may be written \[ K_c(\mu,\nu) =\sup_{\varphi=\varphi^{cc}} \left\{\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\varphi^c\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\}. \]

Proof. Feasibility implies, for each \(y\), \[ \psi(y)\le c(x,y)-\varphi(x)\quad(\forall x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\psi(y)\le\inf_x(c(x,y)-\varphi(x))=\varphi^c(y). \] The transform definition gives both feasibility inequalities \[ \varphi(x)+\varphi^c(y)\le c(x,y), \qquad \varphi^{cc}(x)+\varphi^c(y)\le c(x,y). \] Since \(\varphi^{cc}\ge\varphi\) and \(\varphi^c\ge\psi\), \[ J(\varphi,\psi)\le J(\varphi,\varphi^c)\le J(\varphi^{cc},\varphi^c). \] Set \(f=\varphi^{cc}\). The triple-transform identity gives \(f^c=\varphi^{ccc}=\varphi^c\) and \(f^{cc}=\varphi^{cccc}=\varphi^{cc}=f\). Every feasible pair is therefore dominated in value by a pair \((f,f^c)\) in the restricted class. Conversely every such pair is feasible. Taking suprema and using Kantorovich duality proves the stated formula.

The \(c\)-superdifferential

Definition. For a \(c\)-concave \(\varphi\), its \(c\)-superdifferential is \[ \partial^c\varphi =\{(x,y):\varphi(x)+\varphi^c(y)=c(x,y)\}. \] The fiber \(\partial^c\varphi(x)=\{y:(x,y)\in\partial^c\varphi\}\) contains the destinations at which the \(c\)-support associated with \(y\) touches \(\varphi\) at \(x\).

Corollary. If \(\varphi\) is an optimal Kantorovich potential and \(\pi\) is an optimal plan, then \[ \operatorname{supp}\pi\subset\partial^c\varphi \] whenever the potentials and cost are continuous; in general the inclusion holds \(\pi\)-almost surely.

Cyclical monotonicity

Definition. For finite cost \(c\), a set \(\Gamma\subset X\times Y\) is \(c\)-cyclically monotone if, for any \(N\), pairs \((x_i,y_i)\in\Gamma\), and permutation \(\sigma\), \[ \sum_{i=1}^N c(x_i,y_i)\le\sum_{i=1}^N c(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)}). \] No finite reassignment of the destinations improves the cost. Testing all cycles is equivalent to testing all permutations.

Contact sets are cyclically monotone. If finite potentials satisfy \(\varphi+\psi\le c\), their equality set is \(c\)-cyclically monotone.

Proof. If \((x_i,y_i)\), \(1\le i\le N\), lie in the contact set and \(\sigma\) is a permutation, then \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^N c(x_i,y_i) &=\sum_{i=1}^N\varphi(x_i)+\sum_{i=1}^N\psi(y_i) &&\text{(contact)}\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^N\bigl(\varphi(x_i)+\psi(y_{\sigma(i)})\bigr) &&\text{(permutation)}\\ &\le\sum_{i=1}^N c(x_i,y_{\sigma(i)}) &&\text{(feasibility).} \end{aligned} \] This is \(c\)-cyclical monotonicity. For the preceding support corollary, the optimality certificate gives \(\pi(s>0)=0\), with \(s=c-\varphi-\varphi^c\ge0\). If \(s\) is continuous and \(s(z)>0\) at a support point, some neighborhood \(U\ni z\) has \(s\ge s(z)/2\), whence \[ \int s\,\mathrm{d}\pi\ge\tfrac12s(z)\pi(U)>0, \] a contradiction. Thus every support point lies in the contact set when the slack is continuous.

Optimal support: the continuous-cost case. Let \(X,Y\) be Polish, \(c\) finite continuous, and \(\pi\) a finite-cost optimal plan. Then \(\operatorname{supp}\pi\) is \(c\)-cyclically monotone. Conversely, a finite-cost plan is optimal if it is concentrated on a contact set of feasible potentials \(\varphi\in L^1(\mu),\psi\in L^1(\nu)\).

Proof of necessity. Suppose support points \((x_i,y_i)\), \(1\le i\le N\), violate the cyclic inequality. Continuity gives product neighborhoods \(U_i\), numbers \(m_i=\pi(U_i)>0\), and \(\delta>0\) such that \[ \sum_i c(a_i,b_i)-\sum_i c(a_i,b_{\sigma(i)})\ge\delta \quad\text{whenever }(a_i,b_i)\in U_i. \] Shrink the neighborhoods so that each of the finitely many original and reassigned costs is bounded there. Put \[ \gamma_i=\pi|_{U_i}/m_i,\quad Q=\bigotimes_{i=1}^N\gamma_i, \quad\beta_i=(a_i,b_{\sigma(i)})_\#Q. \] For \(0<\varepsilon\le(\sum_i m_i^{-1})^{-1}\), define \[ \pi'=\pi-\varepsilon\sum_i\gamma_i+\varepsilon\sum_i\beta_i. \] Even if neighborhoods overlap, \[ \varepsilon\sum_i\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_i}{\mathrm{d}\pi} =\varepsilon\sum_i\frac{\mathbf1_{U_i}}{m_i}\le1, \] so \(\pi'\ge0\). Since all \(\gamma_i,\beta_i\) are probabilities, \(\pi'(X\times Y)=1-\varepsilon N+\varepsilon N=1\). Moreover, \[ (\beta_i)_X=(\gamma_i)_X,\qquad (\beta_i)_Y=(\gamma_{\sigma(i)})_Y, \] so \[ \pi'_X=\mu-\varepsilon\sum_i(\gamma_i)_X+\varepsilon\sum_i(\gamma_i)_X=\mu, \quad \pi'_Y=\nu-\varepsilon\sum_i(\gamma_i)_Y+\varepsilon\sum_i(\gamma_{\sigma(i)})_Y=\nu. \] Only bounded-cost pieces have been changed, so subtraction of their integrals is legitimate. By the pushforward identity, \[ \begin{aligned} \int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi'-\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi &=\varepsilon\int\left[\sum_i c(a_i,b_{\sigma(i)})-\sum_i c(a_i,b_i)\right]\,\mathrm{d}Q\\ &\le-\varepsilon\delta<0, \end{aligned} \] contradicting optimality.

Proof of sufficiency. For integrable feasible contact potentials and any competitor \(\widetilde\pi\), \[ \int c\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde\pi \ge\int(\varphi(x)+\psi(y))\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde\pi =\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\nu =\int c\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] Thus \(\pi\) minimizes the cost.

Why cycles lead to potentials

A supporting potential assigns values consistently along chains of paired points. Cyclical monotonicity rules out an improving closed chain, just as the absence of negative cycles permits consistent shortest-path costs on a finite graph. Constructing a finite integrable potential for a general cost requires additional hypotheses; cyclic monotonicity alone should not be used as an unrestricted optimality certificate. For quadratic cost, Rockafellar's convex subgradient theorem supplies precisely the structure needed on the next page.

Quadratic cost and ordinary convexity

Proposition. For \(c(x,y)=\tfrac12|x-y|^2\) on \(\mathbb R^d\), let \[ u(x)=\tfrac12|x|^2-\varphi(x). \] With the extended-value transforms interpreted for proper potentials on the full Euclidean spaces, \(\varphi\) is \(c\)-concave exactly when \(u\) is lower-semicontinuous and convex, and \[ y\in\partial^c\varphi(x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y\in\partial u(x). \]

Proof. Let \(u^*(y)=\sup_x\{x\cdot y-u(x)\}\). For proper extended-value potentials, expansion of the square gives \[ \begin{aligned} \varphi^c(y) &=\inf_x\left\{\tfrac12|x|^2+\tfrac12|y|^2-x\cdot y-\varphi(x)\right\}\\ &=\tfrac12|y|^2+\inf_x\{u(x)-x\cdot y\} =\tfrac12|y|^2-u^*(y),\\ \varphi^{cc}(x)&=\tfrac12|x|^2-u^{**}(x). \end{aligned} \] Thus \(\varphi=\varphi^{cc}\) if and only if \(u=u^{**}\). The Fenchel–Moreau theorem identifies the latter with proper lower-semicontinuous convexity. At points where contact is asserted the relevant values are finite, and \[ \begin{aligned} \varphi(x)+\varphi^c(y)=\tfrac12|x-y|^2 &\Longleftrightarrow u(x)+u^*(y)=x\cdot y\\ &\Longleftrightarrow z\cdot y-u(z)\le x\cdot y-u(x)\quad(\forall z)\\ &\Longleftrightarrow u(z)\ge u(x)+y\cdot(z-x)\quad(\forall z)\\ &\Longleftrightarrow y\in\partial u(x). \end{aligned} \] The middle equivalence follows directly from the supremum defining \(u^*\).

Two-point condition. For quadratic cost, the two-cycle inequality is \[ |x_1-y_1|^2+|x_2-y_2|^2 \le |x_1-y_2|^2+|x_2-y_1|^2, \] which simplifies to \[ (x_1-x_2)\cdot(y_1-y_2)\ge0. \] In one dimension this forbids crossings: \(x_1<x_2\) implies \(y_1\le y_2\). In higher dimensions, all cycle lengths are needed to characterize gradients of convex functions.

Sources and proof details. Ambrosio–Gigli, A user's guide to optimal transport, §1.2, for the optimal support theorem and potential constructions beyond the compact setting.

Practice: cycles and convex potentials

  1. For quadratic cost, verify that \(T(x)=Ax\) is cyclically monotone when \(A\) is symmetric positive semidefinite.

    Hint and answer

    It is the gradient of \(u(x)=x^\top Ax/2\). Sum the subgradient inequalities around a cycle.

  2. In \(\mathbb R^2\), the quarter-turn \(J(x_1,x_2)=(-x_2,x_1)\) satisfies the two-point monotonicity inequality. Is that sufficient?

    Hint and answer

    No: \(h\cdot Jh=0\) for every \(h\), but the points \(0,e_1,e_2\) and their images violate the three-cycle pairing inequality under a suitable cyclic reassignment. Two-point monotonicity does not characterize convex gradients.
