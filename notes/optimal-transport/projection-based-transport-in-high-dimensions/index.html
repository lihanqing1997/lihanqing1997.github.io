Learning goal. Compute sliced discrepancies while keeping their statistical, directional, and geometric limitations separate.

Projection-based distances replace one difficult high-dimensional transport problem by a family of low-dimensional ones. Their appeal is mathematical as well as computational: one-dimensional transport is explicit, empirical rates improve, and the Cramér--Wold theorem preserves identifiability when all directions are retained.

Sliced Wasserstein distance

Definition 23.1 (Sliced Wasserstein distance). Let \(1\le p<\infty\) and \(\mu,\nu\in\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R^d)\). For \(\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}\), let \(P_\theta(x)=\theta^\top x\), and let \(\sigma\) be normalized surface measure on the sphere. Define \[ \operatorname{SW}_p^p(\mu,\nu) =\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}} W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta). \] The max-sliced distance is \[ \operatorname{MSW}_p(\mu,\nu) =\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb S^{d-1}} W_p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu). \]

Theorem 23.2 (Metric property). \(\operatorname{SW}_p\) and \(\operatorname{MSW}_p\) are metrics on \(\mathcal P_p(\mathbb R^d)\).

Proof. Symmetry and nonnegativity are immediate. Write \(a_\theta=W_p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu)\), \(b_\theta=W_p(P_{\theta\#}\nu,P_{\theta\#}\eta)\). The one-dimensional triangle inequality and Minkowski give \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{SW}_p(\mu,\eta) &\le\left(\int(a_\theta+b_\theta)^p\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p}\\ &\le\left(\int a_\theta^p\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p} +\left(\int b_\theta^p\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{1/p} =\operatorname{SW}_p(\mu,\nu)+\operatorname{SW}_p(\nu,\eta). \end{aligned} \] For the maximum version, \(\sup_\theta(a_\theta+b_\theta)\le\sup_\theta a_\theta+\sup_\theta b_\theta\) proves the analogous inequality. Finiteness follows from the projection bound in the next proposition. Measurability in direction follows from \[ W_p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\eta\#}\mu)\le|\theta-\eta|\left(\int|x|^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/p}, \] Indeed, the coupling \((P_\theta,P_\eta)_\#\mu\) has cost \[ \int|(\theta-\eta)^\top x|^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x) \le|\theta-\eta|^p\int|x|^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x). \] Writing \(M_p(\mu)=(\int|x|^p\,\mathrm{d}\mu)^{1/p}\), triangle inequality gives \[ |a_\theta-a_\eta|\le|\theta-\eta|(M_p(\mu)+M_p(\nu)). \] Thus the directional integrand is continuous and measurable. If \(\operatorname{SW}_p=0\), the integrand vanishes on a full-surface-measure set \(A\). This set is dense because every nonempty open patch has positive surface measure (for \(d=1\), each of the two directions has positive mass). For each \(\theta\), choose \(\theta_j\in A\) tending to it. For every \(r\in\mathbb R\), \[ e^{ir\theta_j\cdot x}\to e^{ir\theta\cdot x},\qquad |e^{ir\theta_j\cdot x}|=1\in L^1(\mu)\cap L^1(\nu). \] Dominated convergence therefore gives \[ \widehat\mu(r\theta)=\lim_j\widehat\mu(r\theta_j)=\lim_j\widehat\nu(r\theta_j)=\widehat\nu(r\theta). \] Every frequency has the form \(r\theta\); uniqueness of characteristic functions gives \(\mu=\nu\). If \(\operatorname{MSW}_p=0\), every direction already has equal projected laws, so the same conclusion holds.

Comparison with ordinary Wasserstein distance

Proposition 23.3 (Projection contraction). \[ \operatorname{SW}_p(\mu,\nu) \le\operatorname{MSW}_p(\mu,\nu) \le W_p(\mu,\nu). \]

Proof. Surface measure \(\sigma\) is normalized to mass one. For the nonnegative function \(a_\theta=W_p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu)\), \[ \int a_\theta^p\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\le(\sup_\theta a_\theta)^p\sigma(\mathbb S^{d-1})=(\operatorname{MSW}_p)^p. \] For any \(\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)\), \((P_\theta,P_\theta)_\#\pi\) couples the projected laws, and \(|\theta|=1\) gives \[ W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu) \le\int|\theta^\top(x-y)|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi \le\int|x-y|^p\,\mathrm{d}\pi. \] Taking the infimum over \(\pi\), then the supremum over \(\theta\), and finally positive \(p\)-th roots, yields \[ \operatorname{SW}_p\le\operatorname{MSW}_p\le W_p. \]

A reverse inequality requires restrictions such as compact support or regularity, and its constant generally deteriorates with dimension. Projection distances are therefore weaker quantitatively even though they identify the law.

Quantile representation

Every projected problem is one-dimensional: \[ \operatorname{SW}_p^p(\mu,\nu) = \int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\int_0^1 |F_{\mu,\theta}^{-1}(u)-F_{\nu,\theta}^{-1}(u)|^p \,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta). \] Here \(F_{\mu,\theta}\) is the distribution function of \(\theta^\top X\). This formula replaces a high-dimensional coupling by sorting along directions.

Identifiability through the Radon transform

If \(\mu\) has density \(f\), the density of \(P_{\theta\#}\mu\) is the Radon transform \[ \mathcal Rf(\theta,t) =\int_{\{x:\theta^\top x=t\}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal H^{d-1}(x). \] The Fourier-slice theorem states that the one-dimensional Fourier transform in \(t\) equals \(\widehat f(s\theta)\). Thus all projected laws determine the full characteristic function and hence the original distribution.

Empirical behavior

Proposition 23.4 (Reduction to one-dimensional rates). For the empirical measure \(\widehat\mu_n\), \[ \mathbb E\operatorname{SW}_p^p(\widehat\mu_n,\mu) = \int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}} \mathbb E W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\widehat\mu_n,P_{\theta\#}\mu) \,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta). \] Any one-dimensional bound that is uniform in \(\theta\) therefore transfers directly to the sliced distance without the \(n^{-p/d}\) full-dimensional quantization barrier.

Proof. For each Borel \(A\subset\mathbb R\), \[ (P_{\theta\#}\widehat\mu_n)(A) =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf1_A(\theta^\top X_i), \] so \(P_{\theta\#}\widehat\mu_n=n^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{\theta^\top X_i}\), the empirical law of i.i.d. samples from \(P_{\theta\#}\mu\). The function \(Z(\omega,\theta)=W_p^p(P_{\theta\#}\widehat\mu_n(\omega),P_{\theta\#}\mu)\) is nonnegative and jointly measurable: it is measurable in the samples and continuous in direction by the preceding proof. Tonelli therefore gives \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\operatorname{SW}_p^p(\widehat\mu_n,\mu) &=\int\left[\int Z(\omega,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta)\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb P(\omega)\\ &=\int\left[\int Z(\omega,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb P(\omega)\right]\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta). \end{aligned} \] If the inner expectation is at most \(R_n\) for every direction, the right side is at most \(R_n\sigma(\mathbb S^{d-1})=R_n\).

The constants may still depend on dimension through projected moments or through uniformity over directions. Slicing mitigates the geometric curse of matching but does not make high-dimensional statistics entirely dimension free.

The sliced Wasserstein process

For inference, one studies the random field \[ \theta\longmapsto W_p(P_{\theta\#}\widehat\mu_n,P_{\theta\#}\mu). \] Uniform weak convergence over \(\theta\) requires empirical-process control of projected distribution or quantile functions. Continuous functionals of this process yield limit laws for average-sliced, max-sliced, and randomly sliced statistics. Max-slicing is nonsmooth because it also estimates the maximizing direction.

Monte Carlo approximation over directions

Proposition 23.5 (Random slicing). For independent uniform directions \(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_L\), \[ \widehat{\operatorname{SW}}_{p,L}^p =\frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^L W_p^p(P_{\theta_\ell\#}\mu,P_{\theta_\ell\#}\nu) \] is unbiased for \(\operatorname{SW}_p^p\). If the integrand has finite variance, its directional Monte Carlo error is \(O_{\mathbb P}(L^{-1/2})\).

With empirical measures there are two distinct errors: sample error in the projected laws and numerical integration error over directions. Their budgets should be balanced rather than conflated.

Maximizing over subspaces

Definition 23.6 (Maximizing over subspaces). For \(1\le k\le d\), define \[ \operatorname{Proj}_{p,k}(\mu,\nu) = \sup_{E\in\operatorname{Gr}(k,d)} W_p(P_{E\#}\mu,P_{E\#}\nu), \] where \(P_E\) is orthogonal projection and \(\operatorname{Gr}(k,d)\) is the Grassmannian. At \(k=1\) this is max-sliced transport; at \(k=d\) it is ordinary \(W_p\).

This is the supremum of projected Wasserstein distances. Some literature reserves “projection-robust Wasserstein” for a different sup–inf order; we specify the formula to avoid conflating them. The parameter \(k\) interpolates statistical tractability and geometric fidelity. Optimization over \(E\) is nonconvex, and a data-dependent maximizing subspace contributes additional uncertainty.

Gaussian example

Example 23.7 (Projected Gaussians). If \(\mu=N(m_0,\Sigma_0)\), \(\nu=N(m_1,\Sigma_1)\), then \[ W_2^2(P_{\theta\#}\mu,P_{\theta\#}\nu) = \{\theta^\top(m_0-m_1)\}^2 + \left( \sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_0\theta} - \sqrt{\theta^\top\Sigma_1\theta} \right)^2. \] Slicing averages this explicit directional discrepancy; max-slicing searches for the direction where mean or variance differs most.

Limitations

Different directions are not coupled consistently by separate one-dimensional optimal plans, so sliced geodesics need not correspond to a single flow in \(\mathbb R^d\). A discrepancy concentrated in a small set of directions can be diluted by averaging. Finally, using finitely many random directions makes the computed quantity a random approximation to the metric. These are structural differences, not merely implementation details.

Exercises

what projections retain

  1. Exercise 23.1. For \(\mu=\delta_0\), \(\nu=\delta_a\), compute \(\operatorname{SW}_2\) and \(\operatorname{MSW}_2\).

    Hint

    Project the displacement onto a unit direction, then distinguish averaging over directions from maximizing.

    Solution

    Rotational symmetry gives \(\int\theta\theta^\top\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=I/d\). Thus \(\operatorname{SW}_2=|a|/\sqrt d\), while \(\operatorname{MSW}_2=|a|\).

  2. Exercise 23.2. In two dimensions, can just the two coordinate projections identify a law?

    Hint

    Seek two joint distributions with identical coordinate marginals but different dependence.

    Solution

    No. Compare equal mass at \((1,1),(-1,-1)\) with equal mass at \((1,-1),(-1,1)\). Coordinate marginals agree; a diagonal projection separates them.
