Stability theory studies how much an algorithm changes when one training point is replaced. Stable algorithms generalize because no single observation can dominate the fitted rule.

Uniform stability

Uniform stability is an algorithmic condition, not a property of the function class alone.

Definition. An algorithm \(A\) has uniform stability \(\beta_n\) with respect to loss \(\ell\) if for any two samples \(S,S'\) differing in one observation, \[ \sup_z |\ell(A(S),z)-\ell(A(S'),z)|\le\beta_n. \]

Remark. This definition is deliberately worst-case. Weaker notions such as hypothesis stability or on-average stability can give sharper but more delicate results.

Expected generalization gap

Uniform stability directly controls the expected difference between population and empirical risk.

Theorem. If \(A\) has uniform stability \(\beta_n\), then \[ \left|\mathbb E\{R(A(S))-R_n(A(S))\}\right|\le\beta_n. \]

Proof. Let \(S^{(i)}\) replace \(Z_i\) by an independent copy \(Z_i'\). By exchangeability, \[ \mathbb E R(A(S))=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb E\,\ell(A(S^{(i)}),Z_i). \] Compare this with \(n^{-1}\sum_i\mathbb E\,\ell(A(S),Z_i)\). Each term differs by at most \(\beta_n\) by stability.

High-probability stability

Stability also gives concentration when the loss is bounded.

Proposition. If \(0\le\ell\le M\) and \(A\) has stability \(\beta_n\), then the generalization gap concentrates at a scale controlled by \(\beta_n\) and \(M/\sqrt n\).

Proof. Changing one data point changes the generalization gap by at most a constant multiple of \(\beta_n+M/n\). Apply the bounded-differences inequality to the gap and combine it with the expected stability bound.

Regularized ERM

Strong convexity is a common route from optimization geometry to stability.

Theorem. For \(L\)-Lipschitz losses and \(\lambda\)-strongly convex regularization, regularized ERM has stability of order \(L^2/(\lambda n)\).

Proof. Compare the two regularized objectives for samples differing in one point. Strong convexity controls the distance between minimizers, while Lipschitzness converts that distance into a difference in losses. Since only one empirical term changes, the factor \(1/n\) appears.

Replace-one and remove-one conventions

Two conventions coexist. Replace-one stability compares samples of equal size that differ at one coordinate. Remove-one stability compares \(S\) with \(S\setminus\{Z_i\}\). Their constants differ slightly, but under regularity they encode the same principle. Proofs must state which convention is used because exchangeability identities depend on it.

A high-probability bound with explicit constants

Theorem. Suppose \(0\le\ell\le M\) and \(A\) has replace-one uniform stability \(\beta\). Then, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), \[ R(A(S))-R_n(A(S)) \le \beta+(2n\beta+M) \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}}. \]

Proof. Write \(G(S)=R(A(S))-R_n(A(S))\). Its expectation is at most \(\beta\). Replacing \(Z_i\) changes the population-risk term by at most \(\beta\). Among empirical terms, \(n-1\) shared observations each change by at most \(\beta/n\), while the replaced term changes by at most \(M/n\). Hence \[ |G(S)-G(S^{(i)})|\le2\beta+M/n. \] McDiarmid's inequality gives \(G-\mathbb EG\le(2n\beta+M)\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2n)}\).

This classical bound is informative when \(\beta=O(1/n)\), but its \(\sqrt n\,\beta\) term can be suboptimal. Sharper exponential inequalities for stable algorithms recover rates closer to \(\beta\log n\log(1/\delta)+M\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}\).

Average stability and excess risk

Definition. The expected leave-one-out stability is \[ \operatorname{stab}(A)= \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb E\left[ \ell(A(S^{(-i)}),Z_i)-\ell(A(S),Z_i)\right]. \]

Proposition. For ERM under a convex smooth loss, expected average stability often equals the expected optimism \[ \mathbb E\{R(A(S))-R_n(A(S))\} \] up to the convention used for sample size.

Proof. Replace a fresh test point in the population risk by a uniformly selected training index using exchangeability. The fitted rule trained without that index has the same joint law as a rule trained on an independent sample of size \(n-1\) evaluated at a fresh point. Subtract and add its loss at the omitted observation. Averaging produces the leave-one-out difference.

Stability of stochastic gradient methods

Theorem. Suppose each \(\ell(\cdot,z)\) is convex, \(L\)-Lipschitz, and \(\gamma\)-smooth. Couple two runs of stochastic gradient descent on neighboring datasets with the same sampled indices: \[ w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta_t\nabla\ell(w_t,Z_{I_t}), \qquad \eta_t\le2/\gamma. \] Then after \(T\) steps, \[ \sup_z\mathbb E|\ell(w_T,z)-\ell(w_T',z)| \le\frac{2L^2}{n}\sum_{t=1}^T\eta_t. \]

Proof. Gradient descent on a convex \(\gamma\)-smooth function is nonexpansive for \(\eta\le2/\gamma\). Thus the coupled distance can increase only when the sampled index is the differing example, an event of probability \(1/n\); in that case the increase is at most \(2L\eta_t\). Recursion gives \(\mathbb E\|w_T-w_T'\|\le(2L/n)\sum_t\eta_t\). Apply \(L\)-Lipschitzness of the test loss.

The bound grows with training time. Early stopping is therefore simultaneously an optimization choice and a stability regularizer.

Strong convexity removes time dependence

Theorem. If the population of per-example objectives is additionally \(\mu\)-strongly convex, contractive recursions yield SGD stability of order \[ \frac{L^2}{\mu n} \] under suitable decreasing step sizes, uniformly over long training horizons.

Proof outline. Strong convexity turns the nonexpansive factor into \(1-\mu\eta_t\). The neighboring-example shock at time \(t\) has size \(2L\eta_t/n\), but it is multiplied by \(\prod_{s>t}(1-\mu\eta_s)\) before the final iterate. Summing this damped impulse response is \(O(L/(\mu n))\), and Lipschitzness supplies the second factor \(L\).

Stability does not imply good approximation

Counterexample. The algorithm that always returns the zero predictor has stability \(0\), yet it may have arbitrarily poor risk. Stability controls the generalization gap \(R(\widehat f)-R_n(\widehat f)\); it does not control empirical optimization error or approximation error. A complete excess-risk result must combine all three.

Differential privacy as randomized stability

Proposition. An \(\varepsilon\)-differentially private algorithm satisfies, for neighboring samples \(S,S'\), \[ P(A(S)\in B)\le e^\varepsilon P(A(S')\in B) \] for every measurable output event \(B\). For bounded losses, this multiplicative distributional stability implies expectation generalization bounds of order \(e^\varepsilon-1\), with stronger high-probability statements available under approximate privacy.

Uniform stability is a pointwise deterministic comparison of losses; differential privacy compares output laws. Neither subsumes the other without extra assumptions, but both formalize limited dependence on one individual observation.
